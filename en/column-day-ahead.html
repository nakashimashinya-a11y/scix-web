What the Balancing Market's Shift to Day-Ahead Trading Means
— The End of Weekly Products and the Impact on Battery Operation

On March 13, 2026, Primary through Tertiary Reserve 1 in the balancing market moved from "weekly products" to "day-ahead trading." We set out what changed, and how it affects a battery's daily allocation decisions against JEPX.

In three lines
  1. The shift to day-ahead trading is the rule change under which Primary through Tertiary Reserve 1 moved from weekly products to day-ahead trading from transactions on March 13, 2026.
  2. The next day is now bid at 14:00 the day before, in 30-minute slots instead of 3-hour blocks, so the balancing market sits beside JEPX's 48 daily slots at the same time granularity.
  3. From the same March 13, 2026 transactions the price cap for Primary, Secondary 1 and the combined product fell from ¥19.51 to ¥15 (¥10 from delivery on September 1, 2026), and daily allocation decisions now separate revenue.
Update as of November 2026

In EPRX's price cap table, the ¥10 cap for Primary Reserve, Secondary Reserve 1 and the combined product applies from delivery on September 1, 2026, with the end date given as "for the time being" (a note says it may change depending on the outcome of future council deliberations). The table gives no date for a cut to ¥7.21. The ¥7.21 cap for Secondary Reserve 2 and Tertiary Reserve 1 continues for the time being. For Primary Reserve in September 2026 (preliminary values, nationwide), the bid ratio (bid volume ÷ procured volume) was 123.1% online and 84.0% offline. This is a different period and a different measure from the fulfillment rate in the text (final figures, April 2025 – February 2026), so the two cannot be compared directly. In September, after procurement volume was reduced to the 1σ equivalent in March, online bids exceeded the volume procured.

What Day-Ahead Trading Is — The Rule Change of March 13, 2026

In the supply and demand balancing market (operated by EPRX), the trading method for Primary Reserve (FCR) through Tertiary Reserve 1 (RR) changed substantially from transactions on March 13, 2026. What had been bid a week at a time as "weekly products" moved to "day-ahead trading," in which the next day is bid at 14:00 the day before. At the same time, the trading unit was subdivided from 3-hour blocks into 30-minute slots, and the duration of every product was unified at 30 minutes (Tertiary Reserve 2 (RR-FIT) had already changed to 30 minutes ahead of the others, from FY2025).

This change is not a mere adjustment of administrative procedure. It is a switch from a system that locks in balancing capacity a week ahead to one that decides allocation day by day with the next day's supply and demand outlook factored in, and for battery operators it makes daily operating decisions matter much more. This column focuses on the shift to day-ahead trading itself. The overall product design — the five balancing market products and the difference between online and offline — is covered in detail in The 5 Balancing Market Products.

The 3 things that changed from transactions on March 13, 2026:
• Trading timing … Weekly (a week's worth in advance, in one batch) → Day-ahead trading (the next day bid at 14:00 the day before)
• Trading unit … 3-hour blocks → 30-minute slots (duration of every product unified at 30 minutes)
• Price cap and procurement volume … Price cap for Primary, Secondary Reserve 1 (S-FRR) and the combined product lowered from ¥19.51 to ¥15; procurement volume for Primary and Secondary 1 reduced from 3σ to 1σ

The products covered are Primary, Secondary 1, Secondary Reserve 2 (FRR) and Tertiary 1, as well as the combined product. Tertiary 2 has already been traded separately as a day-ahead product.

What Weekly Products Were — and Why They Were Abolished

From the start of trading in all products in April 2024 until March 12, 2026, Primary, Secondary 1 and 2 and Tertiary 1 were traded as "weekly products." A weekly product is a method in which a week's worth of balancing capacity is bid and cleared in one batch, about a week before delivery. Transmission and distribution companies could secure the volume they needed week by week, and operators bid in one batch at the start of each week.

The weakness of this method was that dividing capacity between markets was difficult. A battery's revenue is built by combining the buying and selling of electrical energy (kWh) on JEPX (the wholesale electricity market) with the provision of balancing capacity (ΔkW = standby) in the balancing market. Once balancing capacity has been cleared a week ahead, however, the battery is tied to the balancing market in those hours whatever the JEPX spot price turns out to be. Even if the next day's spot price spikes, capacity already committed to standby in the balancing market cannot be redirected to JEPX. The long, week-long reservation period stood in the way of nimble arbitrage between the two markets.

Day-ahead trading shortens this time horizon from "a week ahead" to "the day before," so that allocation can be decided with the next day's supply and demand outlook factored in. Together with the subdivision of the trading unit into 30-minute slots, this means that, slot by slot, an operator can choose whether to put capacity into JEPX or into the balancing market.

30-Minute Slots and "48 Slots" — Where the Balancing Market Meets JEPX

The practical core of day-ahead trading is that the trading unit became the 30-minute slot. The JEPX spot market has always traded the day in 48 slots of 30 minutes each. Now that the balancing market has also moved to 30-minute slots, the two markets sit side by side at the same time granularity.

As a result, every day an operator decides, for each of the next day's 48 slots, whether to "sell electrical energy on JEPX (the wholesale electricity market) or stand by in the balancing market." Moreover, the two markets cannot be chosen independently. Under the rule that only volume bid into the JEPX spot market and left unsold can be offered into the balancing market, a sequence arises: first decide the JEPX bids, then build the balancing market offers on the results. A decision that in the era of weekly products could only be changed week by week now comes up 48 times every day.

The daily decision loop created by day-ahead trading (for a battery):
① Forecast the next day's JEPX spot prices and the balancing market outlook
② For each of the 48 slots, assign charging, discharging into JEPX, and standby in the balancing market
③ Bid into the JEPX spot market (gate closure at 10:00 the day before)
④ Offer the unsold capacity into the balancing market (14:00 the day before)
⑤ Clearing → charging/discharging, standby and response during actual delivery → settlement

A battery can offer the charging it would pause even in slots where it is charging. But the slots it charges in change its state of charge and how much it can offer in each slot, so how it places charging and offers across the 48 slots directly determines its revenue.

One point to watch is the physical constraint of the battery's state of charge. A battery can offer the charging it would pause as upward reserve even in slots where it is charging (EPRX handling guide). But as long as its basic operation is to charge in low-price hours (late at night, and in the daytime hours of solar surplus) and to discharge or stand by in high-price hours, the slots it charges in change its state of charge and how much it can offer in each slot, and it cannot offer the same volume in all 48 slots. On the wholesale market (JEPX), a battery is a buyer in the slots where it charges, so it cannot sell in them. In effect, day-ahead trading confronts operators every day with an optimization problem: how, and to which of JEPX and the balancing market, to allocate this limited stored energy.

The Price Cap and Procurement Volume Were Cut at the Same Time

From the same March 13, 2026 transactions as the shift to day-ahead trading, two cuts were also made on the price side. They are changes to keep in view together with day-ahead trading.

ItemThrough FY2025From FY2026
Price cap for Primary, Secondary 1 and the combined product¥19.51/ΔkW per 30 min¥15/ΔkW per 30 min
Price cap for Secondary 2 and Tertiary 1¥7.21/ΔkW per 30 min¥7.21/ΔkW per 30 min (unchanged)
Procurement volume (Primary, Secondary 1)3σ equivalent1σ equivalent
Outlook for further cuts—Stepwise cuts to ¥10 → ¥7.21 under consideration

The price cap was eased to ¥15 from the initial proposal of ¥7.21, but it is still a cut of about 23% from the previous ¥19.51. Furthermore, a policy has been set out of lowering it in stages, to ¥10 and then ¥7.21, if no improvement is seen in the competitive state of the market. As for procurement volume, the procurement standard for Primary and Secondary 1 was changed from the 3σ equivalent (covering 99.7% of forecast errors) to the 1σ equivalent (covering 68%), so that the total volume of this balancing capacity procured through the market was itself cut substantially.

Update: Of these, the ¥10 cap has applied to Primary Reserve, Secondary Reserve 1 and the combined product from delivery on September 1, 2026. Our article estimating the amount that disappears, across 9 areas × 4 products from EPRX clearing results, is The balancing market price cap falls to ¥10.

Where day-ahead trading is a change to "the timing and granularity of trading," the cuts to the price cap and procurement volume are a change to "the revenue ceiling and the size of the market." With both taking effect at once, battery operation is moving toward "finer-grained, more daily, competing for a more limited pie." For actual figures on unit-price ranges and fulfillment rates by product, see The 5 Balancing Market Products.

Source: Agency for Natural Resources and Energy, "On the Balancing Market," materials for the 110th meeting of the System Design Working Group (January 23, 2026)

The Impact on Battery Operation — What Separates Revenue

Day-ahead trading has created a market environment in which annual revenue readily differs even between identical battery facilities. What creates the difference is not the performance of the equipment itself, but how skillfully the daily allocation decisions are made.

① Daily allocation between JEPX and the balancing market

Forecast the next day's spot prices and the balancing market's unit prices and clearing outlook, and decide how to assign the 48 slots. This has become the central variable of revenue since the shift to day-ahead trading. On days when JEPX spot prices are high, capacity goes there; in hours when balancing market unit prices can be expected, it goes to standby — and this decision must be made every day, slot by slot.

② The aggregator's operational capability

Optimizing the allocation of 48 slots is not a workload that can be run accurately by hand. The aggregator's operating algorithm handles everything from optimizing charging timing, through allocation between JEPX and the balancing market, to setting offer prices. With day-ahead trading, the relative strength of this algorithm now feeds directly into annual revenue. Even with the same facility, revenue changes with the choice of aggregator. Because how fees are taken (gross / net) also bears directly on revenue, the operating design is worth reviewing together with The 3 Revenue Sources of Batteries.

③ Factoring in where the rules are heading

The price cap has already been lowered to ¥15, and a policy of further stepwise cuts has also been set out. At the same time, the fulfillment rate for Primary Reserve is low, at 40.8% online and 3.4% offline, and the shortage remains large. The picture for now is that, although the revenue ceiling is coming down, revenue opportunities remain depending on product selection and daily operation. Facilities need to be assessed with the downward direction of the rules built in, not only on short-term unit prices.

⚠ Day-ahead trading has not "made it easier to earn":
Day-ahead trading has made nimble division of capacity with JEPX possible, but it was introduced at the same time as the lower price cap and the reduced procurement volume. For operators who can run daily allocation skillfully, opportunities widen; without operations to match, a business takes only the effect of the lower revenue ceiling. This is a phase in which, more than the quality of the equipment, operating design and the choice of aggregator decide a project's viability.
Source: EPRX trading results (final figures), April 2025 – February 2026 / Agency for Natural Resources and Energy, "On the Balancing Market," materials for the 110th meeting of the System Design Working Group (January 23, 2026)

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the balancing market's shift to day-ahead trading?
It refers to the change, from transactions on March 13, 2026, in which the "weekly products" for Primary through Tertiary Reserve 1, previously bid a week at a time, moved to "day-ahead trading," in which the next day is bid at 14:00 the day before. The trading unit was also subdivided from 3-hour blocks into 30-minute slots, and the duration of every product was unified at 30 minutes.
Why were weekly products abolished?
Weekly products secured balancing capacity a week ahead, which made it difficult to divide resources optimally between JEPX (the wholesale electricity market) and the balancing market. With day-ahead trading, allocation can be decided daily with the next day's supply and demand outlook factored in, which makes arbitrage between the markets work more easily. At the same time, the trading unit was subdivided into 30-minute slots.
How does day-ahead trading affect battery operation?
Every day, operators must decide for each of the next day's 48 slots whether to "sell on JEPX or stand by in the balancing market." Under the rule that only volume bid into the JEPX spot market and left unsold can be offered into the balancing market, dividing capacity between the two markets has become more strategic. This daily allocation optimization depends on the aggregator's operational capability.
Did the price cap change at the same time as the shift to day-ahead trading?
Yes. From the same March 13, 2026 transactions, the price cap for Primary Reserve, Secondary Reserve 1 and the combined product was lowered from ¥19.51 to ¥15 (¥/ΔkW per 30 minutes), and the procurement volume for Primary Reserve and Secondary Reserve 1 was reduced from the 3σ equivalent to the 1σ equivalent. The revenue ceiling came down, but the fulfillment rate for Primary Reserve is low, at 40.8% online and 3.4% offline, and the shortage remains large.
When did Japan's balancing market start trading all products, and when did day-ahead trading begin?
Trading in all products started in April 2024, and until March 12, 2026 Primary, Secondary 1 and 2 and Tertiary 1 were traded as weekly products, bid and cleared a week at a time. From transactions on March 13, 2026 they moved to day-ahead trading, in which the next day is bid at 14:00 the day before. Tertiary 2 has already been traded separately as a day-ahead product.
