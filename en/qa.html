Grid-Scale BESS Owner's Q&A

What actually happens when you buy and own a grid-scale battery storage plant in Japan — set out in the order you meet it: before you buy, while you own it, and when you exit. The questions are ones we have genuinely been asked by investors, buyers and owners. Answers draw on published material from METI, OCCTO, EPRX and IPA, and on actual market clearing results.
Answers reflect regulations and market data as of the time of writing. This page is general commentary, not a solicitation to invest in any specific project nor legal, tax, or financial advice.

Before You Buy

The revenue plan I have been shown — where should I start doubting it?

Check three things and you will usually be able to tell.

First, the price the revenue starts from. The balancing market — where the utility buys the ability to raise and lower output at short notice — has a cap on bid prices. But a cap is a ceiling, not the price things actually trade at. A plan that starts from the cap is starting high. Actual trading prices are published, so check that the plan starts at, or below, those.

Second, whether the discounts are there. You do not win every bid. Normal practice is to multiply revenue by the share of bids you actually win, so a table built on "we win all 47 bids a day" is optimistic from that point on.

Third, whether the numbers depend on one operator's skill. A plan built from published market results still holds if the operator changes. A plan that says "these numbers come out because this particular company runs it" cannot be checked afterwards.

Are balancing-market prices going to keep falling? What happens to my income?

The price ceiling has been coming down in steps. From JPY 19.51 to 15 (March 2026), with 10 due from September 2026 and a stated intention to go to 7.21. But — and this bears repeating — that is a ceiling, not the price things actually trade at.

What matters more is that the centre of gravity is moving. While balancing-market prices are held down, capacity-market prices (see the next question) have been rising. From "earn on balancing" to "build a floor on capacity and add balancing on top" — that reading is closer to reality.

There are two ways to prepare. Before you buy, confirm that the business survives even if prices fall all the way to the announced floor. And since the lower prices go, the more valuable it becomes simply not to be offline, put money into the operating setup instead.

How much can I count on capacity-market income?

Quite a lot. The capacity market pays not for electricity delivered but for staying ready to deliver, and the price is fixed four years before the year it applies to. So income for the next few years is already a known amount. For the 2029 year, all nine areas of Japan cleared at high prices.

That "fixed four years ahead" quality acts as the floor under your fixed costs. Market income moves around, but this contracted portion covers a good share of the annual fixed costs. That is the backbone of a storage plant's economics.

There is one condition. If you are offline, the amount is reduced. Capacity income is not money you receive; it is money you receive provided you do not stop. And the higher the price, the more is deducted when you do.

Does the same 2 MW really earn that differently depending on where it is?

It does. When we compared published results across Japan's nine grid areas and three markets, the same 2 MW-class plant looked capable of about JPY 290 million a year in one area and only about JPY 60 million in another. Same equipment, same operation — that far apart.

Three reasons. How much balancing capability that area needs. How much competing storage and thermal capacity is already there. And what the capacity-market price is. On top of that, in smaller areas relatively few trading slots are offered at all, so even a high price does not give you enough chances to earn.

So lining projects up by headline yield alone tells you very little. Ask which area, and which period of actual results, that yield was built from.

Before buying, which documents should I read myself?

Leave the technical checking to specialists, but four documents are worth your own attention.

First, the interconnection study response. It can carry a limit on how fast you may change output — a condition set by the utility — and a tight limit shuts you out of the fast-responding, better-paid products. Clever control alone will not remove it; it takes the utility's agreement.

Second, the non-firm connection consent. Almost every project today connects this way: you are allowed to connect on the basis that you can be curtailed without compensation when the grid is congested. The same response document also records past fault history ("0.667 times/year" means twice in three years), which gives you a rough sense of outage risk.

Third, the receipt for the grid connection contribution, and the order status of long-lead equipment such as the substation. Whether these are done changes the character of the remaining work entirely. Fourth, the EPC contract — whether it carries a completion guarantee and damages for delay, and whether the contractor could actually pay them.

What kind of site attracts buyers? What are buyers looking at?

"It clears every regulation, and it still failed screening" — a common story in this business. Buyers keep internal criteria separate from the law, and how strict they are varies enormously between companies. On distance to housing alone, some accept about 30 m provided there is noise mitigation, while others insist on 100 m or more. Some draw a flat line on modelled flood depth; others restrict which regions they will invest in.

This matters not only when you buy. When you sell, you become the one being screened out.

So at the point of acquisition, capture the measured distance to the nearest house, the hazard information, the zoning and the applicable noise limits as numbers, and keep them on file. That record is exactly what makes the asset easy to sell later. Judging a site by the colour on a hazard map is too crude: flood risk means different things depending on depth and frequency, so restating it as "roughly how much loss per year" also connects it to the insurance design.

How loud is it? And after commissioning, who deals with the neighbours?

First: the noise comes from the PCS (the unit that converts between DC and AC), not from the battery. Battery enclosures are usually more than 10 dB quieter, so noise control is really a question of what you do about the PCS.

PCS models differ a lot. Some large units run at around 85 dB, quiet models at around 60 dB, and newer designs claim around 45 dB. A 10 dB difference sounds about twice as loud, so choosing the model can be the single most effective measure. Limits depend on the municipality and the zoning — often roughly 40–45 dB at night in residential areas, and around 55 dB at the site boundary elsewhere. Putting a large PCS in a strictly regulated area means combining measures: placing noisy equipment as far from houses as possible, acoustic barriers, quieter equipment, and adjusting how the plant is run.

Now the part owners should know. When a complaint arrives after commissioning, the person at the counter is the owner who holds the asset. Not the aggregator, not the EPC. So when you buy, check that the record of the pre-construction residents' briefing is handed over, that noise measurements exist, and that your liability policy covers noise complaints.

If the EPC has not been chosen yet, will the project actually get built?

That there is completion risk should be conceded plainly. What you can do is judge the kind of risk.

First, what is left. If the grid rights (interconnection agreement, contribution paid), the land, and long-lead equipment such as the substation are all secured, what remains is ordinary construction — a completely different kind of risk from an early-stage development.

Second, whether the risk has been moved by contract. Require a completion guarantee and damages for delay in the EPC contract, and choose a contractor with the financial strength to accept them, and the risk moves to the EPC. The contractor's credit standing is what matters here; choose on price alone and the guarantee is worth nothing.

Third, what handover actually contains. Completion is not the same as handover complete. Is the testing and inspection window reserved in the schedule? And are the communications records — which device carries which settings — part of what you receive? Without those, nobody can find the fault when communications drop after the plant is running.

While You Own It

Once I have bought it, what do I actually do? Who do I hand things to?

You will often hear that three parties — the aggregator, the chief electrical engineer, and the manufacturers — are all it takes. But each of them has a scope they do not accept, and adding them together still does not cover the plant. The aggregator takes your plant to market on the assumption it is running; it does not repair it. The chief electrical engineer guards the substation on the AC side, and few can go further and restore the PCS or the control system. Manufacturers stop at their own equipment.

The result is projects where nobody is named in any contract as the one who goes and looks first when the PCS, the battery or the communications throw a fault.

So the owner's job is not day-to-day operation. It is deciding, by contract, who owns that gap. In practice you put an O&M company at the front — one that manages the whole plant and acts as your single point of contact — and run the chief electrical engineer, the manufacturers, the insurers and the communications underneath it. After that, your own work is to read the monthly report, decide on renewals, and keep the answer to "who do I call" down to exactly one.

When the plant stops, who repairs it?

Law and practice have to be looked at separately.

In law, the Electricity Business Act places responsibility for supervising the safety of the whole installation on the chief electrical engineer. Under the statutory definition of a storage plant, the PCS and the protective equipment are included; there is no provision anywhere that carves out the DC side. The responsibility is properly assigned.

But what is assigned is supervision — confirming that things meet the standards and reporting to the owner if they do not — rather than repairing the PCS or the battery personally. No provision names who actually does the repair, and that is the gap contracts have to fill. Manufacturer warranties add to it: they typically lapse if anyone outside the manufacturer's authorisation touches the equipment. The DC side is not untouchable; touching it voids something else.

The layer most often missed is communications. "Communication failure" on the screen tells you nothing about whether the local router is down, the carrier's side has failed, or a setting has been lost — and who you call changes completely. Whether your O&M contract spells out "first response on the DC side" and "isolating communications faults" is the practical answer to this question.

If it goes offline, how much does that cost me?

The damage arrives in three stages.

First, the revenue you would have earned in those hours. Second, the balancing-market penalty: once you have cleared, you are obliged to respond to dispatch, and failing to do so deducts an amount equal to what you cleared. Do it three times in one month on the same product and new trading in that product is suspended.

Third, the capacity market — and this is where it really bites. You are allowed to deduct the equivalent of 180 days a year from your total offline time, but the counting is not even-handed. Maintenance notified in advance counts once. A sudden breakdown counts five times. The rules put a price on the difference between planned maintenance and unplanned failure, and that price is five to one.

That factor of five is the whole reason to spend money on O&M. It is not about never stopping. Spot the problem early, convert it into a planned outage, and the penalty shrinks to as little as a fifth. That difference is what O&M is buying.

What does maintenance (O&M) cost per year?

Honestly: there is no established public benchmark yet. The public figures that exist are an operating cost of JPY 5,000/kW·year used in a government model, and an international benchmark of roughly 2.5% of construction cost. Quotes from providers run to around JPY 5 million a year including insurance, or JPY 2.5–3 million excluding it — but none of these is a going rate.

So it is safer not to take any single figure as the market. What is useful is breaking the quote apart: the chief electrical engineer, 24-hour monitoring, emergency call-outs (how many a month are in the base fee), the communications line and its upkeep, insurance, and standing in for manufacturer inspections. Only once you can see where the base fee ends, where the extras begin and what falls to you can you argue about whether the price is right.

Quotes can take weeks. That is not the company being slow — as the next question explains, finding a chief electrical engineer takes time.

By when do I need a chief electrical engineer in place?

Six months before commissioning, have three things settled: the O&M company, the chief electrical engineer, and the communications arrangement.

Six months, because finding one takes time. At high voltage you can contract the role out to a safety corporation, but on two conditions: the person must be able to reach the site within two hours, and there is a cap on how many sites one person may take on. In other words, they have to live nearby and still have capacity. On top of that, government material points to a possible shortfall of around 1,000 engineers by FY2030, and roughly 60% of licence holders are over 50.

Extra-high voltage is harder still. Contracting the role out is not available at all — you appoint internally or externally — and a higher grade of licence is required, so the pool of people is simply smaller.

If you are buying a completed plant, the smoothest handover is to keep the existing engineer until just before commercial operation while switching across to the O&M company's arrangement.

What insurance do I need? Can I use the same design as solar?

No. The comprehensive movable-property policy commonly used for solar excludes generating plants of 500 kW and above in its own wording, so a storage plant's main equipment cannot be written under it at all. Take it to a solar broker and you may get a quote whose underlying premise does not hold.

You need six kinds of cover: property (fire, lightning, wind and flood), machinery (things that break from the inside, such as a burnt-out PCS), business interruption (income lost while the plant is out of action), liability, cyber, and theft.

Three things you cannot skip in the design. Set the sum insured at what it would cost to rebuild today, not what you paid. Set the business-interruption period around lead times for parts — four to eight months for a PCS. And make sure business interruption covers every revenue stream: capacity market, balancing market and the wholesale market alike.

One more thing. Establishing the cause of a fire can take more than a year, and payment is effectively frozen until it is. Hold your cash reserves on that basis.

How do I choose an aggregator? And “5% of revenue” — 5% of what?

The fee first. Exactly one aggregator in Japan publishes its rate, at 5% of market profit. Others appear to sit around 10%, negotiable, so the general range is about 5–15%.

The problem is what the 5% is charged on. Is it total revenue, before deducting the cost of the electricity you bought to charge with? Or profit after deducting charging costs, network charges and penalties? The same 5% can mean nearly twice the money. So when you are told "5% of revenue", ask four things. Does that revenue include capacity-market income? Are charging costs deducted before or after? Who pays imbalance penalties? And is the exchange's own trading fee included or extra?

Something matters more than the rate, though. Japanese contracts are almost all revenue-share, but elsewhere there are contracts with a guaranteed floor, contracts where someone buys your output for a fixed sum, and arrangements where you pay a flat software fee and run the plant yourself. They differ in who loses money when the market falls. Decide who carries that risk first; decide the fee second.

From April 2027, could I be told a replacement device “cannot be connected”?

You could. The government has set out a policy of requiring PCS and control systems with communications functions to hold JC-STAR Level 1 — a scheme under which the state confirms a device's security — as a condition of grid connection. High voltage is scheduled for April 2027, low voltage for October the same year.

Two things for owners to hold on to.

One: the date that counts is the date you apply for the interconnection contract, not the date the works finish. For projects applying after the change, equipment that does not meet the requirement generally cannot be connected.

Two: a common misunderstanding. "If the router at the entrance holds Level 1, everything behind it is fine." It is not. The requirement applies to each communicating device individually, and it is not something obtained for a battery system as one bundle.

Battery cells themselves have no communications function and are out of scope; the scope is the control system and the PCS. As an owner, list your communicating devices and record whether each holds Level 1 — and note that building that list is exactly why you need the settings records handed over at completion.

Money, Tax and Exit

I hear batteries qualify for immediate depreciation. Is that true?

The headline is not wrong, but three things are missing from it.

One: the measure has not started. It is waiting on the enabling law to come into force. Two: the words "battery" and "storage plant" do not appear once in the text of the tax reform outline. Whether they qualify will be settled by a ministerial ordinance and by the Minister's confirmation of each plan. Three: the outline excludes equipment held for the purpose of letting others use it — so a structure where a company owns the plant and a third party uses it risks falling out here.

There are other gates: a floor on the investment amount (JPY 3.5 billion; JPY 500 million for smaller companies), a required return on investment of 15%, and obtaining the Minister's confirmation before you buy.

And then the most important point. Immediate depreciation does not reduce the total tax you pay by one yen. It simply pulls 17 years of expense into year one, and the later years lose that expense in exchange. What you gain is the value of paying tax later. On a JPY 3 billion plant, that is worth on the order of JPY 200 million. Confirm the specifics with your tax adviser.

What taxes come with owning a storage plant?

Three buckets make it easier to hold in mind.

First, depreciable-asset tax on the equipment — batteries, PCS, substation equipment, foundations. Second, land. Storage sites are usually forest or miscellaneous land and not expensive, but holding land attracts fixed-asset tax on it. Whether you buy the land or take it on a superficies right or a fixed-term lease determines where that falls.

Third, corporate tax, where depreciation does the work. Battery equipment is written off over 17 years as a rule (or in year one, if the measure above becomes available). When the period over which you recover your investment and the period over which you expense it diverge, book profit and actual cash stop matching. If you are building a structure that distributes to investors, settle this early with a tax adviser.

Consumption tax also depends on how you buy: exempt if you buy the company, taxable if you buy the equipment directly (see below). Treatment varies with the terms, so always confirm your own case with a tax adviser and lawyer.

Am I better off taking the subsidy?

A subsidy is not something to take; it is something to decide about.

The headline is easy: national programmes run from one-third to two-thirds depending on scale, with ceilings from JPY 1 billion to 4 billion. The other side is where the problem is. Take the subsidy and for 17 years you need prior approval to sell, lease or dispose of the asset. Add three years of reporting, five years of document retention, constraints on which equipment you may choose, and the schedule constraint of waiting for the grant decision.

What bites hardest for an owner is that the 17-year restriction sits on your sale price as a discount for the whole period.

And taking the subsidy tends to raise your procurement cost. Government material puts the system price for subsidised projects at JPY 68,000/kWh, while acknowledging that projects using overseas systems outside subsidy programmes are seen at JPY 20,000–40,000/kWh. If the price rises in order to capture a one-third subsidy, the effective subsidy is far smaller than the headline. The shorter your intended hold, the more often declining is the rational choice.

Can I buy a storage plant with debt, or is it cash only?

Cash does dominate, but "no debt because it can't be taken as security" is not accurate. The published facts say otherwise. A 40 MW project in Fukushima raised a JPY 6.2 billion syndicated loan over 20 years. A 49 MW project in Niigata raised a JPY 10 billion bond. Fourteen high-voltage sites nationwide carry JPY 4.9 billion of non-recourse lending.

So why is cash the norm? A difference in speed. Sellers often want settlement in about a month, while a buyer needs one and a half to two months for internal approval and bank credit review, and six months to a year for project finance. Projects do not wait. Cash that can decide today beats debt that is cheaper but slower.

What separates financeable from unfinanceable is not security but contracts. Long-term fixed income increases what you can borrow; where revenue is left to the market, the review turns on the substance of your business plan. It is worth designing a two-stage approach from the point of purchase: secure the entry with cash, then refinance into debt once you have an operating record.

What is the difference between buying the company and buying the equipment?

The biggest difference is consumption tax. Buying the company's shares is exempt; buying the equipment or rights directly is taxable. At extra-high voltage the sums are large, so the tax difference is large too — which is one practical reason deals are structured as company sales. Some sellers, for their own tax reasons, will sell no other way.

On the other hand, buying the company means inheriting everything inside it. Not just the visible assets but existing contracts, future payment obligations, and in some cases debts that are not on the books. So always establish how much the company still owes in future payments, separately from the price of the shares. A price that looks cheap can come out the same once the inherited obligations are added.

The difference persists after you buy. Take over the company and its aggregator contract, O&M contract and land agreements all become your operating conditions as they stand. If there are contracts you will want to change later, read the termination clauses before you buy.

When can I sell? Can I sell soon after it starts running?

You can. The market does exactly this: connect to the grid, spend a few months in the balancing market building an operating record, then sell. Not "build it and flip it", but "show with measurements that it earns, then sell".

Why does a record command a price? For buyers, and especially financial ones, the biggest worry is whether the plant really earns what the model says. However finely built, a model is still a forecast. Even a few months of real clearing, response and payment records replaces part of that worry with measurement, and the project starts to reach more cautious buyers.

Which means: to capture that uplift you need the records. Monthly reporting, clearing data, outage history — whether your contracts leave those in your own hands feeds straight through to the sale price. Note too that a subsidised project carries the 17-year restriction and sits poorly with an early exit.

In 20 years, what happens to the equipment and the land? Who pays to remove it?

This is settled only by contract, so here are the points to check.

First, the land agreement. Sites are owned outright or held on a superficies right or a fixed-term business lease, so check in the wording whether the term matches the project life, whether it can be renewed, and whether you are required to remove the equipment and return the land clear at the end.

Second, provision for removal. There is no statutory reserve scheme for storage of the kind solar has. Whether to set money aside, and how much, is your decision. If the asset sits in a company and you keep distributing to investors, that company can be empty at year 20 with only the removal obligation left. Practitioners get ahead of this by having a separate entity own the land and lease it in, or by retaining an amount each year.

Third, what the equipment is worth by then. Not necessarily nothing. Meaningful capacity is expected to remain even at year 10, and replacing some cells to extend life, replacing the plant to keep using the grid slot, or selling it as an asset with a track record are all genuinely on the table. The right to connect to the grid outlives the equipment.

If an overseas company or individual becomes the owner, are there procedures?

Yes — under the Foreign Exchange and Foreign Trade Act. The electricity business is subject to prior notification, and the trigger is a foreign investor acquiring shares in the company that owns the plant. The obligation to file sits with the buyer.

You may sign the contract before filing, but you cannot complete settlement and handover before the notification has been accepted. So in practice the sale agreement states that clearing this procedure is a condition of settlement.

How many filings depends on the structure. A foreign investor who sets up a Japanese company with the electricity business among its purposes files twice — at incorporation and on acquiring the shares. An individual acquiring the shares directly files once. Setting up the Japanese company itself is outside the Act.

It matters when you sell, too. Sell to foreign capital and the buyer must file again; sell to a Japanese company and in principle no further filing is needed. In other words, this procedure changes how wide your pool of buyers is. Requirements vary by deal and by date, so always confirm with a lawyer for an actual transaction.
