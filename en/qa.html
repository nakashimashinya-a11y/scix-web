Grid-Scale BESS Owner's Q&A

What actually happens when you buy and own a grid-scale battery storage plant in Japan — set out in the order you meet it: before you buy, while you own it, and when you exit. The questions are ones we have genuinely been asked by investors, buyers and owners. Answers draw on published material from METI, OCCTO, EPRX and IPA, and on actual market clearing results.
Answers reflect regulations and market data as of the time of writing. This page is general commentary, not a solicitation to invest in any specific project nor legal, tax, or financial advice.

Before You Buy

How should an owner go about stress-testing the revenue model they have been shown?

There is an order to it. The first thing to check is whether the price at the top of the revenue stack is the market's actual clearing level or the regulatory ceiling. The balancing market — where transmission and distribution operators procure the regulating capacity that keeps grid frequency stable — has a price cap on bids, but a cap is a ceiling, not a clearing price. A model anchored to the cap may be running on assumptions well above what the market actually pays. An honest model starts at, or below, the published clearing results.

The second thing to look at is how the discounts are applied. You do not win every slot you bid into, so revenue should be discounted by a clearing ratio — the share of bids that actually clear. And for products such as tertiary reserve 2, where some slots are never tendered at all, you also need to multiply by the share of slots actually offered. Whether both of these discounts are present makes a large difference to how much the model can be trusted. A table built on winning all 47 slots a day is optimistic from that point onward.

The third question is whether the revenue depends on somebody's skill. If prices and clearing ratios are set from published market results rather than from a particular aggregator's business plan, the assumptions survive a change of operator. A model whose logic is "these numbers come out because this operator runs it" becomes impossible to verify the moment that operator is gone. The last thing to ask for, as an owner, is the model in a form where you can substitute the assumptions yourself — input cells you can switch. Take delivery of how the numbers were built, not just the numbers.

Will balancing-market prices keep falling? What does that mean for an owner's income?

The price cap has been stepped down. From JPY 19.51/ΔkW·30min to 15 (alongside the March 2026 rule change that moved trading to day-ahead), with the next step to 10 scheduled to apply from the September 1, 2026 delivery date, and a stated policy direction of going further to 7.21 (timing undetermined, depending on competitive conditions). But to repeat the point: this is a ceiling, not the clearing price. Actual results are published — battery primary reserve, for example, has been observed clearing in the range of JPY 9.6–13.5/ΔkW·30min.

What matters more for income is that the centre of gravity is shifting. While cap reductions and smaller tendered volumes hold down the regulating-capacity (ΔkW) side, the capacity market (kW) cleared above the reference price in all nine areas for delivery year FY2029. A design that earns from regulating capacity is giving way to one where capacity underpins the floor and regulating capacity adds on top — that reading is closer to what the data shows.

There are two ways for an owner to prepare. One is to confirm, before acquisition, that the business survives a conservative case in which prices fall all the way to the announced final cap level. The other is to invest in the operating setup, because the lower prices go, the more valuable it becomes simply not to be offline. In the capacity market, the higher the clearing price, the greater the exposure to non-delivery penalties. Price levels and O&M are not separate topics.

How firm is capacity-market revenue?

The capacity market pays not for energy delivered (kWh) but for holding supply capability (kW) available. The main auction is held four years ahead of the delivery year, and clearing locks in the price for that year. Revenue for the next few delivery years can therefore be treated as contracted. Prices differ by area: for delivery year FY2028 they ranged roughly from JPY 8,785 to 14,812/kW·year by area, and for FY2029 all nine areas cleared above the reference price (Net CONE JPY 10,075/kW), with Hokkaido at 14,972, Tohoku and Tokyo at 15,111 and Kyushu at 15,112.

That "price fixed four years ahead" quality works, on an owner's income statement, as a floor under fixed costs. Market-linked revenue moves around, but the contracted capacity revenue covers a substantial share of annual fixed costs — this is the backbone of a storage plant's economics. Look at whether that floor is working before you look at the revenue total.

Contracted revenue comes with obligations, though. A resource under a capacity-provision contract carries a continuous-operation requirement, and falling short reduces the contracted amount. Capacity revenue is not something you receive; it is something you receive provided you do not go offline. And the higher the clearing price, the larger the penalty exposure. This connects directly to the O&M discussion in the next section.

Does the same 2MW really earn that differently depending on the area?

It does. When we reconciled published results across nine grid areas and three markets, the same 2MW-class plant produced roughly JPY 290 million a year in one area and around JPY 60 million in another. The reason a single specification with a single operating pattern spreads that far is that balancing-market clearing prices and tendered volumes are genuinely different area by area.

Three things drive the gap: the depth of that area's need for regulating capacity (demand size and renewable share), the volume of competing storage and thermal capacity, and the capacity-market area price. On top of that, in smaller-demand areas relatively few slots are tendered at all, so even a high unit price does not translate into enough winnable slots. Looking only at prices hides that constraint.

So comparing projects on headline yield alone is not very meaningful. What an owner wants to know is which area, which product and which period of actual results that yield is anchored to. Change the area and the same set of assumptions produces a different answer.

Before buying, which documents should an owner read personally?

Leave the technical verification to specialists, but four documents repay reading yourself. First, the interconnection study response. It can impose a ramp-rate limit — a transmission-operator condition on how many percent of rated output the plant may change per minute — and a tight limit shuts you out of the fast-responding, higher-priced products. This is not something EMS (energy management system) control alone can work around; relaxing it requires the transmission operator's agreement. Always confirm whether a limit exists and where any relaxation talks stand.

Second, the non-firm connection consent. Essentially every project in development today connects on a non-firm basis — connection is granted on the premise of uncompensated curtailment when the grid is congested — and the consent contains language to the effect that market participation may be restricted. Alongside it, look at the N-1 fault history recorded in the study response: how many times the relevant line or transformer has faulted in the past. "0.667 times/year", for instance, means twice in three years, and that frequency together with restoration times gives you a rough annual outage risk.

Third, proof of payment of the grid connection contribution, and the order status of long-lead equipment such as the substation and dedicated line. Whether these are done changes the character of the remaining work entirely. Fourth, the EPC (engineering, procurement and construction) contract: whether it carries a completion guarantee and liquidated damages (LDs — pre-agreed compensation for delay), and whether the counterparty has the financial strength to honour them. A guarantee clause only means something if there is someone able to perform it.

What determines siting? What are buyers and leasing companies actually looking at?

"It clears every regulation, and it still failed screening" — that is not an unusual story in storage transactions. Buyers maintain internal siting criteria separate from the law, and the thresholds vary widely between companies. Among the examples we have encountered, setback from housing ranges from firms that accept about 30m provided noise mitigation is in place, to firms that require 100m or more from housing to the PCS (power conditioning system). Others draw a flat line on modelled flood depth, restrict the areas they will invest in, or begin by asking whether the site sits in a strict noise-regulation zone.

For an owner, this matters not only when buying. When you come to sell, you become the one being screened out. So at acquisition, capture the measured setback to the nearest dwelling, the hazard data, the zoning designation and the applicable noise limits as numbers, and keep them on file. That record is your future liquidity.

One caution: deciding a site on the colour of a hazard map is too coarse. Flood exposure means different things depending on the combination of depth and frequency, so restating it as an expected annual loss (EAL) lets you make a judgement that connects to the insurance design as well. Whether a site is good or bad can be expressed in money, not colour.

How loud is a storage plant, and after commissioning, who deals with the neighbours?

Start from the fact that the main noise source is not the battery units but the PCS (power conditioning system — the equipment converting between DC and AC). Battery enclosures are typically more than 10 dB quieter than the PCS, so noise mitigation is, in practice, a question of how the PCS is handled. PCS noise varies widely by manufacturer and model: some common large units run at around 85 dB, quieter models at around 60 dB, and newer designs advertising roughly 45 dB have come to market. A 10 dB difference is perceived as roughly twice as loud, so equipment selection itself can be the single most effective noise measure.

Applicable limits differ by municipality and zoning. As a guide, residential zones often apply roughly 40–45 dB at night, and elsewhere around 55 dB at the site boundary. Putting a large PCS into a low-limit area means combining measures: a layout that places noise-emitting equipment as far from dwellings as possible, acoustic barriers, quieter equipment, and adjustments to the operating schedule.

What an owner should note is what comes after that. When a complaint arrives post-commissioning, the party at the counter is the owner who holds the asset — not the aggregator, not the EPC. So at acquisition it is worth confirming that the record of the pre-construction residents' briefing (date, attendees, materials distributed) transfers with the asset, that noise measurement data exists, and that the liability policy (PL) covers claims relating to noise and electromagnetic emissions. Neighbour relations are a question of numbers and, equally, a question of what documentation was handed over.

For a project where the EPC has not yet been selected, how should completion risk be viewed?

That such a project carries completion risk is a fact worth conceding plainly. What we can add is a way to distinguish the quality of that risk. First, the nature of the work remaining. If the grid rights (interconnection agreement, contribution paid), land title, and long-lead equipment such as the substation are all secured, what remains is standard construction — a completely different kind of risk from an early-stage development.

Second, contractual risk transfer. Require a completion guarantee and liquidated damages in the EPC contract, and select a contractor with the credit standing to accept them, and completion risk moves contractually to the EPC. The contractor's own creditworthiness is what matters here: a guarantee clause is meaningful only where there is the financial strength to perform it. Selecting an EPC on price alone can leave you with a guarantee on paper only.

Third, what "handover" actually contains. For an owner, completion is not the same as handover complete. Is the testing and inspection window between interconnection and commercial operation properly reserved in the schedule? And are the as-built communications records — which router carries which configuration, which IP address connects to what — part of what is handed over? Without those records, nobody can isolate the fault when communications drop after commissioning. Write it into the contract at signing.

While You Own It

Once you own a storage plant, what does the owner actually do? Who is responsible for what?

Operations are usually described in terms of three parties: the aggregator, the chief electrical engineer, and the manufacturers. But each of the three has a scope it accepts and a scope it does not, and stacking them does not cover the whole plant. The aggregator faces the market on the assumption that the equipment is running; it is not the party that maintains it. The chief electrical engineer guards the AC side — the substation — and few can restore the EMS or PCS beneath it single-handed. Manufacturers look after their own equipment and no further.

The result is projects where, if something goes wrong on the DC side (EMS, PCS, batteries, BMS) or in communications, no contract names who performs the first diagnosis and restoration. This is not a gap in the regulations, though. Look into it and the truth runs the other way: the Electricity Business Act places supervisory responsibility for the safety of the whole installation on the chief electrical engineer. What is missing is not the law but who discharges that responsibility on site — a mismatch between responsibility that is statutory and performance that is unassigned.

So what an owner actually does is not day-to-day operation; it is deciding, by contract, to whom that gap belongs. In practice this means placing an O&M provider at the front as the single point of contact who integrates management of the whole installation, and running the chief electrical engineer, the manufacturers, the insurers and the communications layer beneath it. Beyond that, the owner's job is to read the monthly reporting, decide on contract renewals, and maintain a state in which "who do I call when something breaks" has exactly one answer.

When the plant stops, who repairs it?

Law and practice need to be looked at separately. Article 43(1) of the Electricity Business Act requires anyone installing electric facilities for business use to appoint a chief electrical engineer to supervise safety in the construction, maintenance and operation of those facilities. And under the statutory definition of a storage plant, the inverter (PCS) driving the storage device and the protective equipment are themselves electric facilities. Nothing anywhere excludes the DC side from the scope of supervision. Supervisory responsibility extends, by law, to the whole installation.

What the chief electrical engineer owes, however, is supervision — confirming conformity with technical standards and reporting to the installer where non-conformity is suspected — not personally rectifying the PCS or the batteries. No provision names who actually carries out the repair; that is territory to be filled by contract. Manufacturer warranties add a further constraint: they typically lapse if someone outside the manufacturer's authorisation works on the equipment, qualified or not. It is more accurate to say the DC side is not "untouchable" but rather that "touching it voids something else" — fenced in by skills, manufacturer authorisation, and warranty scope.

The layer most often missed is communications. When the EMS screen reads "communication failure", whether the local router has dropped, the carrier's side has gone down, or a configuration has been lost changes both who to call and what to do. Projects where no contract assigns that diagnosis are common. The reason is that the required contents of the safety regulations contain no provision demanding as-built records or restoration responsibility for communications equipment — what the rules do not require, nobody records. For an owner, the practical answer is whether the O&M contract explicitly assigns "first response on the DC side" and "communications diagnosis and restoration".

If the plant goes offline, how much does it cost?

The damage from an outage is not only lost opportunity. It arrives by three routes. The first is the revenue you would have earned in those hours. The second is balancing-market penalties. A cleared resource is obliged to respond to dispatch, and failing Assessment II — the check on actual response — attracts a penalty at 1.0 times the cleared amount. Furthermore, three or more failures within one calendar month for the same resource and product suspend new trading in that product. Offline you cannot respond; failing to respond you are docked; repeat it and you are shut out of the market — a three-stage structure.

The third is the capacity market. A storage plant under a capacity-provision contract carries a continuous-operation requirement, and the economic penalty applies to cumulative non-delivery blocks after deducting 8,640 blocks (roughly 180 days). What deserves attention is how those blocks are counted: maintenance notified in advance as planned outage counts once, but unplanned, sudden outages count five times. The rules explicitly quantify the economic difference between planned maintenance driven by early warning and unplanned breakdown at five to one.

That factor of five is the sharpest explanation of what O&M is economically worth. This is not about reducing outages to zero. It is about shifting the character of outages toward the planned side. Detect anomalies early, diagnose and restore quickly, and a sudden failure becomes a planned outage — cutting penalty exposure to around one-fifth at best. Note also that for events caused by the grid side and not foreseeable at bidding, such as curtailment, there is a relief framework setting the penalty multiplier at 1.0 and excluding the event from the non-conformity count. Whether you stopped yourself or the grid stopped you is treated differently — here too, isolating the cause pays.

What does O&M cost per year, and what does it include?

Honestly: there is still no established public benchmark for fixed O&M on grid-scale storage in Japan. The few public anchors are the operating and maintenance cost used for the model plant in the Long-Term Decarbonisation Power Source Auction, where METI's working-group material puts operating cost (labour) at JPY 5,000/kW·year, and the international benchmark from NREL's ATB, which sets fixed O&M at roughly 2.5% of CAPEX (including battery augmentation, on a 15-year operating basis). On the ground, some take a generous view at around JPY 5 million a year including insurance, while others put it at JPY 2.5–3 million excluding insurance — but these are provider-quoted figures, not publicly established levels.

It is therefore safer not to take any single annual figure as "the going rate". What is useful to an owner is breaking the quote into line items: the chief electrical engineer (appointment and statutory inspections), 24/7 monitoring and alarm analysis, emergency call-outs (how many per month are in the base fee), the communications line and its maintenance, insurance, manufacturer-inspection agency, and cybersecurity. Only once you can see how far the base fee reaches, where the options begin, and what falls to you can the level be argued about sensibly.

Quotes can take weeks to arrive, and that is not a matter of any particular firm being slow. As the next question explains, appointing a chief electrical engineer is constrained by distance, workload caps and qualification class, so it takes time to find a candidate and form a view on the appointment. Conversely, if a quote comes back the same day, it is worth checking whether the chief electrical engineer really has been secured.

By when, and from where, must a chief electrical engineer be appointed?

For storage plants at high voltage and above, appointing a chief electrical engineer is a legal requirement. It can be done by internal appointment — a licence holder among your own officers or employees — or by external appointment, using an employee of an electrical safety corporation or similar. In addition, there is the outsourcing-approval route, under which safety management is contracted out in place of an appointment, with the approval of the Director of the Industrial Safety and Inspection Department. That route, however, is available only for solar or storage plants with output below 5,000 kW connecting at 7,000 V or less. Put the other way: outsourcing is not an option for extra-high-voltage storage plants, leaving internal or external appointment. High voltage and extra-high voltage are different vessels from the outset.

Outsourcing carries two constraints. One is that the safety officer must in principle be able to reach the site within two hours (the "two-hour rule"). The other is a points cap limiting the combined converted value of the sites one officer may take on to no more than 33. In other words, the appointee must be someone nearby who still has capacity. Then there is the supply of people. METI material indicates that without new measures, by FY2030 there could be a shortfall of roughly 1,000 Class-2 and 800 Class-3 engineers, and that roughly 60% of licence holders are over 50 and roughly 40% over 60. On the extra-high-voltage side there is a further constraint: storage plants of 5,000 kW and above fall outside the Class-3 supervisory scope, so the absolute number of Class-2 and above licence holders becomes the binding limit.

Practically, it is safest to have the O&M provider, the chief electrical engineer and the communications arrangement all settled six months before commissioning. Where you are acquiring a completed asset, the smoothest handover is to keep using the incumbent chief electrical engineer up to just before commercial operation while switching across to the O&M provider's arrangement. The state to avoid above all is reaching the interconnection date with none of this written into any contract.

What insurance is needed? Can the solar package be reused?

It cannot. The movable-property comprehensive policy that dominates solar excludes generating plants of 500 kW and above by the express terms of the major insurers, so the body of a grid-scale storage plant essentially cannot be underwritten under it. Take the project to a solar-focused broker and a single movable-property quote may come back, but the underwriting premise may not hold.

What needs to be covered during operation runs to six layers: property (external perils such as fire, lightning, wind and flood), machinery (internal breakdown such as PCS burnout or BMS failure), business interruption (BI — lost revenue during an outage caused by physical damage), liability (product and premises), cyber, and theft. As for design: set property and machinery sums insured on a replacement-cost basis rather than acquisition cost; design BI indemnity periods around restoration lead times (roughly 4–8 months for a PCS, 4–6 months for a battery container, about 6 months for switchgear and transformers); and make sure BI covers every revenue stream — capacity market, balancing market and JEPX. For a 2MW-class plant, liability limits of JPY 100–500 million are standard, with JPY 1 billion or more suggested where housing is adjacent.

Two issues specific to storage. One is to have it stated expressly at inception whether fire caused by thermal runaway falls within the property cover — treatment under the policy wording can turn on whether the ignition was spontaneous or externally caused. The other is that establishing the cause of an incident tends to take a long time, during which claim payment is effectively frozen; there are reported domestic cases still unpaid more than a year after the event. Design the BI elimination period and your liquidity headroom on that basis.

How should an aggregator be chosen? And when they say “5% of revenue” — 5% of what, exactly?

Start with the fee. Exactly one aggregator in Japan publishes its rate: 5% of market profit, with a contract term of either one or five years (no initial fee on the five-year option). Its own comparison table describes competitors as "around 10%, negotiable", from which the general Japanese level can be inferred at roughly 5–15% of market profit. But there is no shared definition of "market profit" across providers. That is where the trap sits.

There are two broad calculation bases. Gross — total revenue earned in the market, before deducting charging costs and penalties. And net — profit after deducting charging costs, wheeling charges, imbalance penalties and so on from total revenue. The same "5%" can mean nearly double the money depending on the base. So when you are told "5% of revenue", always establish four things. Does that revenue include capacity-market income? Is the charging cost (power purchased on JEPX) deducted before or after? Who bears imbalance penalties? And is the EPRX trading fee (JPY 0.06/ΔkW·30min) included or charged separately?

Something matters more than the rate, though. Japanese contracts are almost universally revenue-share, but internationally there are alternatives: floor-plus-share (the aggregator guarantees an annual revenue floor and the upside above it is split), tolling (the aggregator leases the charge/discharge rights for a period and pays a fixed fee), hybrid, and SaaS (a flat licence fee, with dispatch decisions staying with the owner). Each puts the market risk in a different place. In the UK, a major utility has entered into floor-price contracts covering several hundred MW. The more fixed the income, the better lenders view it — but you give up the upside. The right order of decision is not "whose fee is lowest" but "who carries the market risk under this contract".

From April 2027, could replacing a device run into “this equipment cannot be connected”?

It could. The Agency for Natural Resources and Energy's grid-code working group has set out a policy of requiring, as a technical condition of interconnection, that control systems with communications functions adopted by distributed resources (PCS, EMS and the like) use products certified at JC-STAR Level 1 — the security-requirement labelling scheme operated by IPA. Application is scheduled for April 2027 at high voltage and October 2027 for low voltage below 50 kW.

What matters for an owner is that the trigger is the date of the interconnection contract application, not the date works complete. For projects applying after the revision, equipment not meeting the requirement is in principle not connectable. There is a second, practical misunderstanding: that "if the gateway router holds Level 1, everything behind it is out of scope". The design intent is not network-wide certification but a requirement applied per product (system) using IP communications. Certifying the router alone does not exempt downstream devices, and this is not something obtained for a battery system as a single bundle.

Battery cells and packs themselves have no network function and are out of scope. What is in scope is the network-exposed layer — EMS and PCS, gateways included. Levels 1 and 2 are manufacturer self-declarations; Level 3 and above involve third-party certification. As an owner, list the IP-communicating devices in your installation and record whether each is a Level 1 certified product — and note that building that list is precisely why the as-built records handed over at completion matter.

Money, Tax and Exit

We hear grid-scale storage qualifies for immediate depreciation. Is that true?

The headline is not wrong, but three things are left out. What is being discussed is the "bold investment promotion tax measure" (formally, the tax measure for investment in specified productivity-enhancing equipment) in the FY2026 tax reform outline, which offers a choice between immediate depreciation and a tax credit of 7% of acquisition cost. The first omission is that the measure has not started; it awaits the entry into force of the amended Industrial Competitiveness Enhancement Act. The second is that the words "grid-scale battery" and "storage plant" appear nowhere in the outline's text — whether they qualify will be settled in two stages, by a forthcoming METI ordinance and by the Minister's confirmation of each plan. The third is that the outline excludes "leasing (equipment held for the purpose of letting others use it)". A structure in which an SPC holds the plant and lets a third party use it — tolling or leaseback — risks falling out here.

There are further gates. The size threshold is JPY 3.5 billion per investment plan (JPY 500 million for SMEs), there is a requirement for an average annual return on investment of 15% or more (the formula is still being finalised), and equipment acquired before receiving the Minister's confirmation does not qualify. Confirmation must be obtained by March 31, 2029, and acquisition and business use must follow within five years of confirmation — a window that suits the long build times of storage plants.

Beyond all that, a more important point. Immediate depreciation does not reduce the total tax paid by a single yen. The statutory useful life of storage equipment is 17 years, and immediate depreciation simply pulls those 17 years of expense into year one. What you pull forward, you lose in later years. The gain is the time value of paying tax later, not the depreciation amount itself. On an acquisition of JPY 3 billion at a 30% effective rate and a 5% discount rate, the net benefit is on the order of JPY 200 million. Whether you can use it (the gates) and whether you should (exit strategy and taxable-income design) are separate questions. Confirm the specifics with your tax adviser.

What taxes does owning a storage plant attract?

Three buckets make it easier to hold in mind. First, depreciable-asset tax on the equipment (the fixed-asset tax applying to depreciable assets). Batteries, PCS, substation equipment and structures such as foundations are within scope. Second, land. Storage sites are often classified as forest or miscellaneous land and are not expensive in themselves, but holding land attracts fixed-asset tax on it. Whether you buy the land or take it under a superficies right or a fixed-term business lease determines where that burden sits.

Third, corporate tax — where depreciation does the work. The statutory useful life of storage equipment is 17 years. As above, immediate depreciation lets you expense the whole cost in year one if it is available; if it is not, the cost is spread across 17 years (the declining-balance method front-loads the charge but does not shorten the period). Where the equity payback period and the depreciation period diverge, book profit and cash movement pull apart. In structures that distribute from an SPC to investors, this is a point to settle early with a tax adviser.

Consumption tax also differs at the point of entry: acquiring the SPC's equity (shares) is exempt, while buying the equipment or business rights directly is a taxable transaction (see below). Tax treatment can change with the terms of the deal and the circumstances of the parties, so always confirm your specific case with a tax adviser and legal counsel.

Is it better to take the subsidy?

A subsidy is not something to be taken; it is something to be decided about. The headline rate is easy to read — under the FY2025 grid-scale storage deployment support programme administered by SII, up to one-third with a ceiling of JPY 1 billion for maximum receiving power below 10MW, up to one-half with a ceiling of JPY 4 billion at 10MW and above, and up to two-thirds with a ceiling of JPY 2 billion for long-duration energy storage. The Tokyo Metropolitan programme runs at two-thirds with a JPY 2 billion ceiling, and where combined with national subsidy the total is likewise capped at two-thirds.

The problem is the denominator. Take the subsidy and the constraints stack up. A 17-year disposal restriction — assets acquired with subsidy require prior approval for sale, exchange, lease, disposal or pledging during that period. Three years of effect reporting from the start of market supply, and five years of document retention after the end of the completion fiscal year. Constraints on equipment selection (JC-STAR Level 1 certification and conformity with various safety standards become requirements). And schedule constraints from the lead time to grant decision and the completion deadline.

From an owner's point of view, what bites hardest is that the 17-year disposal restriction sits as an implicit discount on the exit price for the whole period. On top of that, procurement prices themselves differ between subsidised and unsubsidised projects. METI working-group material puts the system price for subsidised grid-scale storage at JPY 68,000/kWh in total (battery 41,000 + PCS 6,000 + construction 14,000), while material from another session of the same group acknowledges that "projects adopting overseas storage systems outside subsidy programmes are also seen at levels of JPY 20,000–40,000/kWh". If procurement costs rise in order to capture a one-third headline subsidy, the effective rate is considerably smaller than the headline. The shorter the exit an owner has in mind, the more often declining the subsidy is the rational choice.

Can a storage plant be bought with debt, or is it cash only?

Cash settlement does dominate, but "no debt because it cannot be taken as security" is not an accurate account. The published facts say otherwise. The 40MW/200MWh project in Nishigo, Fukushima secured a syndicated loan of JPY 6.2 billion over a 20-year term against total project costs of about JPY 6.5 billion. The 49MW/231MWh project in Niigata was funded by Japan's first project bond raised for development-stage use, at JPY 10 billion. Fourteen high-voltage storage facilities nationwide carry non-recourse lending with a facility limit of JPY 4.9 billion, and even a 99MW full-merchant project with no revenue contract attracted project finance.

So why is cash the norm? Because of an asymmetry in speed. Sellers frequently want settlement within about a month, whereas a buyer needs one and a half to two months for internal approval and bank credit review even on a corporate loan, and six months to a year for project finance. Projects do not wait. The result is that "cash that can decide today" beats "debt that is cheaper but slower" — that is the market's mechanics.

What separates financeable projects from unfinanceable ones is not security but contracts. Long-term fixed income — a tolling contract, or a win in the Long-Term Decarbonisation Power Source Auction — enlarges the debt vessel; where revenue is purely market-linked, the review turns on the substance of the business plan. And there the question always comes: "what is the basis for this bid price?" For an owner, it is worth designing from the point of acquisition a two-stage approach — secure the entry in cash, then refinance into debt once operating results have accumulated.

For an owner, what is the difference between an SPC equity transfer and an asset transfer?

The largest difference is consumption tax. Transferring shares (SPC equity) is exempt, whereas an asset transfer — a sale of equipment or business rights — is a taxable transaction. Extra-high-voltage storage plants involve large sums, so the consumption-tax impact is correspondingly large, and this is one practical reason equity transfers (the M&A route, buying the company that holds the project) are chosen. There really are projects where the seller's own tax position means "equity transfer only".

On the other hand, an equity transfer means inheriting a whole company. Beyond the visible assets, you may take on existing contractual relationships, future payment obligations and, in some cases, off-balance-sheet liabilities. Due diligence carries more weight than in an asset transfer, and one point to confirm without fail is how much in future payment obligations the SPC will inherit separately from the equity price. A headline transfer price that looks cheap can, once assumed liabilities are added, come out at the same effective cost.

The difference persists once you are the owner. Acquire via equity transfer and what you inherit is the SPC's entire contract set: the aggregator agreement, the O&M contract, the land lease or superficies right, the EPC contract and its guarantees. Who those are with, how long they run and on what terms become your operating conditions. If there are contracts you will want to restructure after acquisition, check the termination provisions before you buy. Tax treatment turns on individual circumstances, so confirm with a tax adviser and legal counsel.

What does exit look like? Can a recently commissioned plant be sold?

It can. The market has been observed doing exactly this: after interconnection, participating in the balancing market for a few months to build an operating record, then selling. Not "build it and flip it", but "demonstrate with measured results that it earns, then sell".

Why does a track record command a price? For buyers, and particularly for financial investors, the largest uncertainty is whether this plant really earns in the market as projected. However finely modelled, an expected value is still a forecast. Even a few months of actual clearing, response and revenue records replaces part of that uncertainty with measurement, and the project starts to reach a more conservative class of buyer. Which is to say: to capture that uplift, an owner needs records. Monthly reporting, clearing data, assessment results, outage history — whether your contracts are written so that these come to you in a form that survives goes straight to the exit price.

Exit is not only resale. Long-term holding on a written-down book value is one route, and securitisation — pooling multiple sites into a fund or trust vehicle — is being explored. Note, though, that projects using subsidy carry the 17-year disposal restriction and sit poorly with a short exit. Exit design is already half-decided at the moment you choose the acquisition structure and whether to take subsidy.

In 20 years, what happens to the equipment and the land? Who pays for decommissioning?

This is territory settled only by contract, so we set it out as the points to confirm. First, the term and return conditions of the land rights. Sites are held outright, or secured through a superficies right or a fixed-term business lease; check in the contract wording how that term maps to the project life (commonly assumed at around 20 years), what the renewal conditions are, and whether restoration to original condition — removing the equipment and returning the land — is required at termination.

Second, provision for removal costs. There is no statutory decommissioning-reserve scheme for grid-scale storage equivalent to the one under the solar FIT/FIP regime. Whether to reserve, and how much, is therefore the owner's design decision. Where the asset sits in an SPC, continuing to distribute to investors can leave the SPC empty at year 20 with only the removal obligation remaining. Practitioners get ahead of this by having a separate entity hold the land and lease it to the SPC, or by retaining an amount equivalent to removal cost each period.

Third, what the equipment is worth at that point. It will not necessarily be zero. On roughly one cycle a day, meaningful residual capacity can be expected even at year 10, and extending life by partial cell replacement, replacing the plant to keep using the grid slot, or selling as an asset with a track record are all options genuinely under consideration. The interconnection right is an asset with a longer life than the equipment. Decommissioning is only one way of ending; what matters in contract design is which endings you keep available.

If an overseas company or individual becomes the owner, what procedures apply?

Procedures under the Foreign Exchange and Foreign Trade Act (FEFTA) apply. The electricity business is among the sectors subject to prior notification for inward direct investment, and the trigger is the point at which a foreign investor acquires shares in the SPC holding the Japanese storage plant. Filing is the buyer's obligation. The contract itself may be signed before filing, but closing — final payment and handover — cannot occur before the notification has been accepted. In practice the sale agreement states that obtaining FEFTA clearance is a condition precedent to closing.

The number of filings depends on the structure. Where a foreign investor incorporates a Japanese company whose stated purpose includes the electricity business, there is one filing at incorporation and one on acquiring the SPC shares — two in total. Where a foreign investor acquires the SPC shares directly as an individual, there is a single filing at acquisition. Incorporating the SPC itself, as a Japanese company, falls outside FEFTA.

It also affects future resale. Selling to foreign capital requires that buyer to file again; selling to a Japanese entity in principle requires no further filing or report. In practical terms, the breadth of your buyer pool changes with this procedure. Whether and how a filing is required can vary with the size of the deal, the attributes of the investor and the cabinet orders and public notices in force at the time, so always confirm with legal counsel for an actual transaction.
