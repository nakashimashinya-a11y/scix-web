Grid-Scale Battery Storage A Beginner's Q&A

For anyone who has heard of grid-scale battery storage in Japan but does not really know what it is. How it works, what it costs, how it earns, whether it is risky, and where to start — answered in order, without jargon. When you want more depth, follow the article links under each answer.

What Is This Business?

What is grid-scale battery storage, exactly?

You connect a very large rechargeable battery to the power company's transmission network, buy electricity in the hours when it is cheap, store it, and sell it in the hours when it is expensive. That is grid-scale battery storage. "The grid" here simply means the transmission network.

Unlike solar panels, it does not generate any electricity of its own. All it does is take electricity that already exists and move it from one time of day to another. That is why the weather does not affect it.

To look at, it is just a few containers lined up — a quiet piece of equipment with nobody stationed on site. For the most common size, a 2 MW high-voltage-class plant, the land you need is around 1,000 m².

How is this different from solar?

The biggest difference is where the money comes from. Solar had a scheme that bought your output at a fixed price; battery storage has no such scheme. You sell on the market, on your own judgement. Which means the price moves day by day.

On the other hand, the weather does not dictate it, and it works at night. Sunlight does not matter for the land either. It does not have to face south, and nobody asks how many hours of sun it gets. What you need is not sunshine but a connection to the transmission network.

One more thing. It has more parts than a solar plant, and it runs connected over a communications link. So deciding in advance who deals with it when it stops matters far more here than it does with solar.

Who do you sell the stored electricity to? How does it actually make money?

There are three sources of income.

The first is buying and selling the electricity itself. On a market called the wholesale electricity exchange, you buy in the cheap hours and sell in the expensive ones. The second is what the utility pays you for being able to raise and lower your output at short notice. Electricity has to be produced in exactly the amount it is being used, or the lights go out, so you are paid simply for standing by as one of the things that keeps the two in balance. The third is a scheme under which payment for staying ready to deliver is promised to you four years in advance.

For a typical 2 MW high-voltage-class plant, the three together come to roughly JPY 60–110 million of revenue a year — that is the rough figure the published data gives. What is left after costs is your profit.

Why now? How long will this boom last?

As solar and wind have grown, the amount of electricity on the system now swings widely with the weather. There is not enough on the system to absorb those swings, and that is where batteries come in. The government has also been putting the market machinery in place.

But let us be straight about it. The profit is getting thinner, not fatter. As more players have entered, a price cap has been put on what you are paid for standing by, and the stated policy is to lower that cap in stages — from JPY 19.51 to JPY 15, then to JPY 10, then into the JPY 7 range.

At the same time, the payment for staying ready to deliver has been going up in price. The reading closest to reality is that the centre of gravity of how you earn is shifting. It is not "too late", but neither is this a period where you make money just by putting one on the ground.

What kind of people are buying these?

Broadly, three kinds.

One is leasing companies, trading houses and utility-affiliated firms. They buy large projects worth anywhere from several billion to tens of billions of yen, measured against their own internal criteria. The second is companies and owner-run businesses. Most commonly they buy a single 2 MW high-voltage-class plant with cash on hand. The third is overseas investors.

This may surprise you, but in this market the buyer who can pay cash has the upper hand. Sellers often want to settle in about a month, and a buyer who needs one and a half to two months for a bank's credit review can simply miss the window. Speed of decision counts for more here than the size of your pocket.

How much land does it take, and what makes a good site?

For a 2 MW high-voltage-class plant, figure on around 1,000 m². It has to be flat, and there has to be a road that a large trailer and a crane can get down. A single container weighs about 30 tonnes, so the access route is the first thing you look at.

Sunlight is irrelevant. What matters instead is how close you are to a power line or a substation. If that distance is long, the charge you pay to connect to the grid shoots up, and the same equipment ends up with completely different economics. A tenfold difference in connection cost really does happen.

There is one more thing buyers look at that has nothing to do with the law: the distance to the nearest house. Every company sets its own threshold — some will accept 30 m provided soundproofing is in place, others require 100 m or more. Some draw a flat line based on the projected flood depth instead.

What It Costs, What It Earns

How much do I need to get started?

For a 2 MW high-voltage-class plant (around 8,000 kWh), which is the most common way in, construction costs roughly JPY 500–640 million. The equipment itself runs about JPY 70,000–80,000 per kWh, and on top of that come the land and the charge you pay to connect to the grid.

The item that swings the total most is that connection charge. Depending on how far you are from the transmission network and what work is needed, the same equipment can add up to a very different total. Look at it as "what does it cost on this particular site", not "what does the equipment cost".

There are smaller, low-voltage projects too, but the markets they can take part in are limited. Go the other way, to extra-high voltage (tens of MW), and you are into the several billion to over JPY 10 billion range, where the buyers are mainly institutional investors.

📖 Related articles: 30MW+ vs 2MW — battery business models by scale ・ How development rights are priced — 5 variables

How much money does it make?

Working from published data for a 2 MW high-voltage-class plant, the rough picture is revenue of JPY 60–110 million a year, and after maintenance, insurance, network use charges and so on, operating profit of something like JPY 40–80 million a year.

Note how wide that range is. The width is not measurement error — it is a real difference in performance. When we compared the nine power areas across Japan using actual results, the same 2 MW earned JPY 290 million a year in one area and only JPY 60 million in another. Identical equipment; location and operation alone make that much difference.

So when someone says "the yield is X%", do not take that one line at face value. Always ask which area, and which period's actual results, the number was built from.

How many years until I get my money back?

On published calculations that assume JPY 640 million of construction cost, 8 to 16 years. The range is wide because the income depends on how market prices move.

You will sometimes hear a bullish pitch that says "you get it back in four years". That is based on the price levels of a period when the payment for standing by was high, and with the cap on what you are paid for standing by now being lowered in stages, you cannot make the same assumption.

As a way of judging it, do not trust a single number. Have the figures run under three sets of assumptions — base, conservative and bullish — and look at whether it holds up under the worst one. That is the safe approach. If it does not work under the conservative assumptions, walking away from that project is a perfectly reasonable conclusion.

What does it cost to run each year, and on what?

Four items, largest first.

First, maintenance: round-the-clock monitoring, inspections, call-outs when something breaks. Budget 1–2% of the construction cost per year. Second, the fee to the operating company, paid for handling the buying and selling on the market on your behalf, at roughly 5–15% of revenue. Third, insurance. Fourth, network use charges and taxes.

There is also a large expense that tends to be forgotten. Batteries degrade the more you use them, so in years 10 to 15 you may face the cost of replacing them. When you look at a 20-year plan, always check whether that cost is included.

And note that some documents call a figure "profit" when none of the above has been deducted yet. Each time, check exactly what has and has not been taken out.

Do I have to pay cash? Is borrowing not an option?

You can borrow. You will hear it said that "no bank will lend because there is nothing to take as security", and that is not accurate. A 40 MW project in Fukushima raised a 20-year, JPY 6.2 billion loan; a 49 MW project in Niigata raised JPY 10 billion in bonds; and a portfolio of high-voltage projects at 14 sites nationwide raised JPY 4.9 billion in loans.

That said, it is also true that cash is the norm, and the reason is speed. Sellers usually want to settle in about a month, while a buyer who borrows needs one and a half to two months for internal approval plus the bank's review. The deal will not wait for you.

What decides whether a loan happens is not the collateral but the contracts. The more long-term fixed income a project has, the easier it is to borrow against. In practice, the easiest sequence is to secure the project with cash first, then switch to debt once it has an operating track record.

Can I get a subsidy? Am I better off taking one?

The schemes exist. Depending on the size of the project they cover one third to two thirds of the cost, with ceilings of JPY 1 billion to JPY 4 billion.

But you are not necessarily better off. If you take a subsidy, then for 17 years you need prior approval before you sell, lease out or dispose of the asset. Three years of reporting obligations and five years of document retention come with it as well. That 17-year restriction stays attached and keeps working as a discount on the price whenever you come to sell.

On top of that, taking a subsidy tends to push up the purchase price of the equipment itself. Government material sets out subsidised projects at JPY 68,000 per kWh and projects that skip the subsidy and use imported equipment at JPY 20,000–40,000 per kWh. If the price rises because you are chasing a one-third subsidy, the real gain is far smaller than the headline. If you intend to sell early, choosing not to use one is entirely rational.

What about tax? I have heard you can write the whole thing off in the first year.

Three taxes apply: depreciable-asset tax on the equipment, fixed-asset tax on the land, and corporate tax on the profit.

The claim that you can expense the whole amount in the first year (immediate depreciation) needs care. That scheme has not started yet. It is waiting for the underlying law to take effect, and on top of that, the word "battery storage" does not appear even once in the tax-reform documents. Whether it will qualify is still to be decided.

And here is the more important point. Even if you could use it, the total tax you pay would not fall by a single yen. All it does is bunch into the first year what you would otherwise expense over 17 years, which leaves less to deduct in the later years. The only gain is that you have deferred the tax; this is not a tax-saving product. For your actual decision, always confirm with your tax accountant.

After You Buy It

I don't know anything about electricity. Can I still do this? And what do I actually do after I buy one?

Yes, you can. In fact, most owners are not electrical specialists.

What the owner personally does after buying is not the day-to-day running. The buying and selling on the market is done by an operating company, the upkeep of the equipment by a maintenance company, and the inspections required by law by a licensed chief electrical engineer. The owner's job is mainly to read the monthly reports and decide whether to renew the contracts.

There is one part, though, that you cannot hand over entirely: deciding in the contracts who is responsible for what. If you start operating with that left vague, then the day the plant stops you begin by hunting around for whose job it is. The next question covers this.

Who actually runs it for me? How should I choose the company that operates it?

The instructions to charge and discharge come from an operating agent known as an aggregator — the company that trades on your behalf. It bids into the market and delivers whatever it wins. You do not need to watch prices every day yourself.

The fee is mostly performance-based, and 5–15% of revenue is the usual range. The one thing you must check here is: 5% of what? Is it taken from total sales, or from the profit left after subtracting things like the electricity you bought to charge the batteries? The same 5% can mean nearly twice as much money out the door.

One more thing matters more than a cheap fee: under the contract, who takes the loss when market prices fall? Overseas there are contracts that come with a guaranteed minimum. Settle that point before you choose on price.

If it breaks, who fixes it?

This is the single most misunderstood point in the whole business.

People often say you are covered as long as you have three parties: the operating company, the chief electrical engineer and the manufacturer. In practice, the operating company's job is buying and selling on the market — it does not repair equipment. The chief electrical engineer's remit stops at the power-receiving and transforming equipment, and not many of them can repair the conversion units or control units beyond that. The manufacturer covers only its own kit.

The result is that projects really do exist in which no contract anywhere says who goes and looks first when something goes wrong.

So it becomes the owner's job to appoint a maintenance company that looks after the whole plant and acts as the single point of contact. It is safest to have this settled six months before operation starts. Securing licensed staff also takes time.

If it stops running, how much do I lose?

You earn nothing while it is down, which is obvious enough, but it does not end there. There are penalties.

Once you have won a bid in the market, you are obliged to perform as instructed, and if you cannot, you are docked an amount equal to what you won. Do that three times in one month for the same product and you are barred from trading that product.

Bigger still is the side that pays you for staying ready to deliver. You are allowed to subtract the equivalent of 180 days a year from your total downtime, but a planned outage notified in advance counts once, while an unplanned breakdown counts five times over. In other words, the rules price the difference between planned maintenance and an unplanned breakdown at five to one.

That is the reason to spend money on maintenance. It is not about never stopping; it is about turning unplanned stops into planned ones.

Could it catch fire? Is it dangerous?

The risk is not zero. To be straight about it: in Japan, in March 2024, a storage facility burned to the ground; it took about 20 hours to put out and four firefighters were injured. There have been large fires overseas as well.

On the other hand, there is a framework for handling this. The liquid inside the cells counts as a hazardous material under the Fire Service Act, so installing a plant requires prior consultation with the fire department, and a certain amount of open space has to be kept around the batteries. Round-the-clock remote monitoring to catch problems early is also standard.

As an owner there are two things to pin down. Is there a record showing that the consultation with the fire department was properly carried out? And is the insurance for a fire properly designed? Investigating the cause can take more than a year, and there are cases where the insurance money is effectively held up for that whole time, so allow yourself some headroom in cash as well.

Will it be a nuisance to the neighbours? How much noise does it make?

What makes the noise is not the batteries but the unit that converts the electricity — the PCS. The battery containers are normally at least 10 dB quieter than that, so noise control is in practice a question of what you do about the conversion units.

The difference between models is large: some big units are around 85 dB, while quiet models are around 60 dB, and newer products at around 45 dB have appeared. A 10 dB difference sounds about twice as loud to the ear, so choosing the model can be the biggest single measure you take. Limits vary from one municipality to the next, and in residential areas the nighttime figure is often around 40–45 dB.

And here is the important point. Once operation has begun and a complaint comes in, the one who has to face it is the owner. Not the operating company, and not the contractor who built it. When you buy, check whether the records of the briefings given to local residents before construction come with the project, and whether noise complaints are covered by the insurance.

Do I need insurance? Is the same cover as for solar fine?

You do need it, and no, you cannot use the same design as for solar. The policies commonly used for solar exclude plants of 500 kW and above in their terms, so the storage plant itself will not be accepted in the first place.

You need six covers. Property cover for fire, storm and flood damage. Machinery cover for failures that come from inside the equipment. Cover that makes up the income lost while the plant is broken and idle. Third-party liability. Cyber. And theft.

If there is one point you cannot leave out, it is that the sum insured should be set not at what you paid but at what it would cost to build the same thing again today. And because this is equipment for which parts take a long time to source (4–8 months for the conversion units), decide the number of days of cover for the idle period on that basis.

Before You Start

If I were to lose money, what would cause it?

Here they are, most common first.

The first is a business plan that was too optimistic. Mistaking the price cap for the actual market price, assuming you win every bid, leaving costs out — any one of these and less comes in than you assumed.

The second is location. The charge you pay to connect to the grid comes in higher than expected, or the area simply does not earn well to begin with. With the same equipment, where it stands changes the result enormously.

The third is stopping. An unplanned breakdown carries five times the penalty. If you start operating without having settled your maintenance arrangements, this is where you come unstuck.

The fourth is changes to the rules. The cuts to the price cap are genuinely under way. There is no avoiding that, so the only answer is to pick a project that still works on the lower assumption.

If I want to get out partway through, can I sell it?

You can. Second-hand deals do actually get done.

In fact there is a pattern in this market of running a plant for just a few months to build a track record and then selling it. A buyer's biggest worry is whether it really earns what the plan says, so even a few months of real records takes part of that worry away and makes it easier to get a price.

So if you have selling in mind, the records matter. Monthly income statements, bidding data, the history of outages — when you buy, check that the contracts leave all of this in your own hands.

One caveat: for a project that used a subsidy, selling requires prior approval for 17 years. If you intend to sell early, deciding not to use a subsidy at all is a perfectly reasonable option.

What happens to the equipment and the land after 20 years?

Nothing is fixed in advance; it is a question of what you set down in the contracts. There are three things to check.

First, the term of the land contract. If you are leasing, check in the contract itself whether the term matches the life of the business, whether it can be renewed, and whether you have agreed to remove the equipment and hand the land back cleared at the end.

Second, the cost of removal. Battery storage has no legally mandated reserve scheme of the kind solar has. Whether you set money aside is your own decision. If you hold the asset through a company and keep paying out dividends, the company can be empty after 20 years, with nothing left but the obligation to dismantle.

Third, what the plant is worth at that point. It is not necessarily zero after 20 years. You can replace some of the cells to extend its life, or swap the equipment out and go on using your slot on the transmission network. The right to connect to the grid outlives the hardware.

What should I do first?

There is an order to it.

If you own land, start by finding out whether that land can be used at all. Distance to the transmission network, how flat it is, the access road, distance to the nearest houses. If the outlook is not promising at this stage, there is no point going further.

If you have no land and want to buy in as a business, you will be looking for projects where the right to connect has already been secured. In this market, projects are bought and sold with the land and the rights as a package.

Either way, the first thing to decide is not the amount of money. It is how much of it you take on yourself. Do you buy only the rights and build it yourself, buy a finished plant, or buy one that already has a track record? The price differs even for the same 2 MW, and so do the work involved and the risk.
