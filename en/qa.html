What Is This Business?

What is grid-scale battery storage, exactly?

Grid-scale battery storage means connecting a large rechargeable battery to the utility's grid, buying and storing electricity when it is cheap, and selling it when it is expensive. The word "grid" here refers to the utility's transmission network.

It does not generate electricity of its own, the way solar panels do. It only takes electricity that already exists and shifts it to a different time of day. That is why the weather does not affect it.

Its purpose is the opposite of a home battery. A home battery is installed so that your own house has power during an outage. A grid-scale battery is installed to sell electricity into the market. It is not equipment for handing out power to the neighborhood when the lights go out.

Physically, it is a few containers plus a row of units that convert the electricity. Nobody is stationed on site: at the high-voltage scale, the rules allow the licensed electrical professional to be an outside contractor. The cooling fans do run 24 hours a day, though, so the site makes noise. Distance to the nearest homes is one of the first conditions to check.

For the most common size, a 2 MW high-voltage-connected system, the rule of thumb is a site of roughly 1,000 sq m.

How is this different from solar?

The biggest difference is where the revenue comes from. Solar had a program that bought the power at a fixed price. Battery storage has no purchase program. You sell into the market on your own judgment, and that means the price moves day to day.

On the other hand, the weather does not matter and the system runs at night. Sunlight is irrelevant to the land as well. The site does not need to face south, and hours of sunshine do not come into it. What you need is not sun but a connection to the grid.

SolarGrid-scale battery storage
RevenueA fixed-price purchase program existedNo purchase program. You sell into the market
WeatherOutput is set by how much sun there isNot affected
What the land needsSunlight and spaceProximity to the grid
Hours of operationDaytime only24 hours
If it stopsYou simply generate nothingRevenue stops, and the market can impose penalties

One more point. A battery is a more complex system than solar, and it runs connected over a communications link. It charges and discharges in response to instructions from the market, so if communications go down, revenue stops even when the equipment itself is fine. Deciding in advance who responds when it stops therefore matters far more here than it does with solar.

Who do you sell the stored electricity to? How does it actually make money?

There are three sources of revenue. The formal names sound difficult, but the substance is simple.

RevenueFormal nameWhat it is
1. Buying and selling electricityThe wholesale power exchange (JEPX)Buy when prices are low, sell when they are high
2. Payment for standing byThe balancing marketYou are paid for waiting in a state where you can raise or lower output immediately
3. Payment for existingThe capacity marketYou are paid for keeping the plant ready to run, at a price set four years in advance

Electricity supply and demand must match at all times, or the power goes out. The second stream is the payment for standing by as that balancing resource. You receive it even if you never actually charge or discharge.

This may come as a surprise, but when we measured the past 12 months, about 80% of the revenue of a 2 MW high-voltage-connected system came from this second stream, the payment for standing by. If you look at a project only through the image of "buy low, sell high," you will judge it wrong.

What that comes to per year is covered in Q8.

Why now? How long will this boom last?

As solar and wind have grown, the amount of electricity on the system now swings widely with the weather. There is not enough capacity to absorb those swings, and that is where batteries come in. The government has also been putting the market structures in place.

But let us be honest. The trend is toward thinner returns.

The reason is not that more players have entered. If anything it is the opposite: the payment for standing by (the balancing market) has been short of bids and has kept clearing at high prices. So the government is moving to lower the price cap. It has gone from 19.51 yen to 15 yen (for delivery from March 2026) to 10 yen (confirmed to apply from the September 2026 delivery period), with a level of 7.21 yen indicated beyond that.

Meanwhile, the payment for existing (the capacity market) is rising. Clearing prices went from 5,226 yen in the third auction to 7,847 yen in the fourth and 11,134 yen in the fifth (all per kW). Behind this is growing electricity demand from data centers and semiconductor plants.

The view closest to reality is that the center of gravity is shifting from the payment for standing by to the payment for existing. It is not too late, but this is also not a period when you can simply put one in the ground and make money. If you are looking at this now, the dividing line is whether the project still works on the assumption that the payment for standing by has come down.

What kind of people are buying these?

There are three broad types.

The first is leasing companies and trading houses. Names such as Tokyo Century, Sumitomo Mitsui Finance and Leasing, Ricoh Leasing, and Sumitomo Corporation appear in publicly announced deals. They buy at a scale of several billion up to the ten-billion-yen range per project, measured against their own internal criteria.

The second is operating companies and owner-run firms. Kamigumi, Japan Petroleum Exploration, Idemitsu Kosan, Tokyu Land, West Holdings — the industries are all over the map, and entrants from unrelated sectors keep coming. Many of them buy a single 2 MW high-voltage-connected system with cash on hand.

The third is overseas investors.

This may come as a surprise, but in this market the buyer who can pay cash is the strong one. Sellers often want to close in about a month, while a buyer using debt needs 1.5 to 2 months for internal approval and bank review. The deal will not wait. Speed of decision counts for more here than depth of capital.

There are no reliable published statistics on review timelines; the above is our own sense from the field.

How much land does it take, and what makes a good site?

For a 2 MW high-voltage-connected system, around 1,000 sq m is the rule of thumb. But there are items you should look at before area.

What to checkRule of thumbWhat happens if it misses
AreaAround 1,000 sq mThe equipment simply does not fit
Distance to the connection pointFor high voltage, ideally within 30 to 40 m of a utility poleAt more than 1 km, there are cases where the grid connection charge exceeded 100 million yen
TerrainFlatSite preparation costs get added
Access roadAt least 4 m wide, able to take a 16 m trailerEquipment weighing tens of tons cannot be delivered
Distance to homesWithin 50 m is the caution line for noise, and within 100 m you are often asked to raise the limit on liability insuranceIt shows up in complaints and premiums
Hazard zonesPass on flood zones designated for possible house collapse, and special landslide-hazard zonesNothing can rescue the location

Sunlight is irrelevant. What matters instead is being close to transmission lines and substations. If that is far, the grid connection charge jumps, and the same equipment ends up with completely different economics. A tenfold difference in construction cost really does happen.

In practice, the most common pattern is the buyer who rules out anything inside a designated flood zone, full stop. But those maps are made for evacuation, and what they draw is the single worst case, one that occurs with a probability of 0.1% or less in a year. For a business you will run for 20 years, it fits reality better to translate depth and frequency into an expected loss per year. There is also an estimate in which simply raising the equipment foundation by 1 m reduced the expected annual loss by 8.8 million yen.

What It Costs, What It Earns

How much do I need to get started?

For the most common entry point, a 2 MW high-voltage-connected system (around 8,000 kWh), keep two numbers separate in your head. What it costs to build and what it costs to buy are different things.

How you acquire itRule of thumb for a 2 MW / 8 MWh systemWhat is in the price
Build it yourself on bare landRoughly 500 to 600 million yen150 to 200 million yen for the land and the grid connection rights, plus 350 to 450 million yen of actual equipment and construction cost. A total that includes the grid connection charge
Buy a completed system700 to 800 million yenThe cost above, plus the seller's profit and the price of having someone else carry the risk of getting it finished
Buy a system already in operation800 million to 1 billion yenNot one yen more of equipment. It is the price of no longer having to wonder whether it really earns

The one publicly announced transaction with a price attached is the acquisition of an approximately 2 MW / 8 MWh system in Takayama, Gifu Prefecture, for about 700 million yen (a timely disclosure filing dated July 30, 2026).

Most of that range is created by the grid connection charge. The grid connection charge accounts for 2 to 14% of construction cost, but depending on the distance to the grid and whether the grid has spare capacity, it moves from a few hundred million yen to several billion yen. Look at what it costs on that particular piece of land, not at what the equipment costs.

Above this size, extra-high-voltage projects in the tens of MW run from several billion into the ten-billion-yen range, and the lineup of buyers changes as well.

The 150 to 200 million yen for rights, 700 to 800 million yen for a completed system, and 800 million to 1 billion yen for one with an operating record are our own field observations (before tax), not published statistics. The only figure we can confirm as public is the roughly 700 million yen above.

How much money does it make?

We compiled 456 days of published data from JEPX (the wholesale power exchange) and EPRX (the exchange for the balancing market). Annual revenue for a 2 MW high-voltage-connected system landed in a band of 270 to 290 million yen in all nine grid areas. Tohoku was the overall leader at about 290 million yen. These are not preliminary figures from committee papers but measured results, compiled from every daily clearing result.

Do not apply these numbers straight to the future, though. There are two reasons.

First, this is the record of the past 12 months, and it includes a period when the payment for standing by (the balancing market) was still high. Here is how we see the cruising level once the price cap reductions have worked through.

AreaBull caseBase caseBear case
TohokuAbout 86 million yenAbout 68 million yenAbout 53 million yen
TokyoAbout 84 million yenAbout 66 million yenAbout 52 million yen
HokkaidoAbout 80 million yenAbout 64 million yenAbout 50 million yen
KyushuAbout 70 million yenAbout 58 million yenAbout 44 million yen
Chubu, Chugoku and KansaiAbout 62 to 64 million yenAbout 52 to 54 million yenAbout 42 to 44 million yen
Hokuriku and ShikokuAbout 60 million yenAbout 50 million yenAbout 40 million yen

Approximate annual revenue for a 2 MW system. Bull case = the 15 yen cap holds; base case = one step down to 10 yen; bear case = 7.21 yen is reached and competition surges. These are estimates on stated assumptions, not settled figures.

Second, the difference between areas is not a matter of several times over. In the recent measured data, the nine areas all fell between 271 and 286 million yen, and the location difference you can actually count on is about 15 million yen a year, or roughly 300 million yen over 20 years. What creates the difference is the grid connection charge and how well the system is operated, more than which area it sits in.

So do not take a one-line "the yield is X%" at face value. There are three things to ask. Which area is the number from? What period of actual results was it built on? And what bid success rate was assumed?

How many years until I get my money back?

On a conservative estimate built from published figures, 8 to 16 years. That assumes a construction cost of 640 million yen and an operating profit of 40 million to 80 million yen a year. The range is wide because revenue moves with market prices.

If the actual results in Q8 (270 million to 290 million yen a year) simply continued, payback would come much sooner. But you cannot assume they will. The price cap on the payment for standing by (the balancing market) has already been set to fall from 15 yen to 10 yen, and our own steady-state view is 40 million to 86 million yen a year. Treat the 8 to 16 years above as a figure closer to that side.

You will also hear bullish claims that the money comes back almost immediately. One industry estimate, for example, puts annual revenue at about 450 million yen and payback at about 1.5 years, on the assumption that every bid submitted is accepted. Check whether the assumption is full acceptance. Real acceptance rates are not that high.

The safer way to decide is not to trust a single number. Ask for the calculation under three sets of assumptions, standard, conservative and bullish, and look at whether the project survives the worst one. If it does not work under the conservative case, walking away is a perfectly reasonable call.

What does it cost to run each year, and on what?

Here they are, largest first.

Cost itemRough annual amountWhat it covers
Operations and maintenance (O&M)1-2% of construction cost24-hour monitoring, inspections, call-outs when something fails, keeping qualified people on hand
Fee to the operating company5-15% of market profitA percentage of what, exactly, can nearly double what you pay (Q15)
Insurance0.5-1.0% of replacement costYou cannot reuse the same design as for solar (Q20)
Grid charges and taxesDepends on the projectWheeling charges, the generator-side grid charge, the renewable energy levy and so on

There is also a large expense that tends to be forgotten. Batteries degrade with use, so replacing or adding capacity may cost money in years 10 to 15. Whenever you look at a 20-year plan, check that this cost is included.

To be honest about it, this field has no officially established standard unit cost for maintenance. What you can refer to is the 5,000 yen per kW per year that a government study group used as a model figure (10 million yen a year for a 2 MW system), and the roughly 2.5% of construction cost cited by NREL in the United States. So quotes vary from company to company. Looking only at the total tells you nothing. Unless you break it into line items, qualified staff, monitoring, call-outs, communications and insurance, you cannot judge whether a price is high or low.

One more thing: when a document says profit, check each time what has already been deducted to get there.

Do I have to pay cash? Is borrowing not an option?

Yes, you can borrow. You will hear that no lender will touch it because there is nothing to take as collateral, but that is not accurate. Here are published examples.

ProjectSizeFinancing raised
Nishigo, Fukushima40MW/200MWh6.2 billion yen syndicated loan, 20 years (total project cost about 6.5 billion yen)
Niigata Yamaya49MW/231MWh10 billion yen project bond, rated A- by R&I
14 high-voltage-connected sites nationwide-Non-recourse loan with a 4.9 billion yen lending limit
Fukuroi Ukari, Shizuoka99.9MW/280MWhAbout 7.2 billion yen of fully merchant project finance

What decides whether financing appears is not collateral but contracts. Nishigo got a 20-year loan because the long-term decarbonized power source auction had locked in 20 years of fixed revenue. Niigata Yamaya could issue a bond because Tokyo Gas had a contract to pay a fixed usage fee for 20 years. The more long-term fixed revenue a project has, the easier it is to borrow against.

That said, cash really is the norm. The reason is speed. Sellers often want to close in about a month, while a buyer who borrows needs one and a half to two months for internal approval and bank underwriting. The deal will not wait.

In practice, the easiest sequence is to secure the asset with cash first and refinance with debt once it has an operating track record.

Can I get a subsidy? Am I better off taking one?

The programs exist. The bands differ by size, though.

EligibilitySubsidy rateCap
Maximum receiving capacity under 10MW (a 2 MW high-voltage-connected system sits here)Up to 1/31 billion yen
10MW and aboveUp to 1/24 billion yen
Long-duration energy storageUp to 2/32 billion yen

For a 2 MW high-voltage-connected system, that leaves one option in practice: up to one third, capped at 1 billion yen.

But it is not always the better deal. If you take the subsidy, for 17 years you need prior approval to sell, lease or dismantle the asset. Three years of reporting and five years of document retention come with it. That 17-year restriction follows the asset as a discount on the price whenever you come to sell.

There is another point people miss. Subsidies carry safety-standard and certification conditions, which in practice limit you to Japanese-made equipment. So you cannot choose cheaper imports. Government material puts the cost of equipment plus installation at 68,000 yen per kWh for subsidized projects (54,000 yen for equipment plus 14,000 yen for installation), against 20,000 to 40,000 yen for projects that skip the subsidy and use imported equipment. Even after taking one third off 68,000 yen you are at about 45,000 yen, so buying imported equipment with your own money can come out cheaper.

If you intend to sell early, choosing not to use a subsidy is entirely rational.

These per-kWh figures cover equipment and installation only. Land, site preparation, the substation equipment and the grid connection charge are not eligible for the subsidy and are not included in these figures either. The 500 to 600 million yen in Q7 is an all-in total that includes them, so the two cannot be compared directly.

What about tax? I have heard you can write the whole thing off in the first year.

Besides corporate tax on profits, you pay annual taxes on the equipment and on the land.

The claim that you can write the whole thing off in the first year, known as immediate expensing, needs care. That program has not started yet. Applications become possible only once the amended Industrial Competitiveness Enhancement Act takes effect and METI publishes the ministerial ordinance and the application forms. Passed, in force, and open for applications are three different events. And the words storage battery do not appear once in the text of the tax reform outline. Whether it qualifies at all is still to be decided.

Then the more important point. Even if you could use it, the total tax you pay does not fall by a single yen. Battery equipment is written off over 17 years, and immediate expensing only moves all of that into the first year. Your deductions in the later years shrink by the same amount.

The gain is only the value of deferring the tax. On a purchase price of 3 billion yen, an effective tax rate of 30% and a discount rate of 5%, the net benefit works out at around 200 million yen. That is a large sum, but it is nothing like the 3 billion yen headline.

One more thing. Any portion covered by a subsidy is effectively excluded from immediate expensing in the first place. Subsidies and immediate expensing are not two independent savings; they are two ways of dividing up the same single amount.

This is not a tax shelter product. Always confirm the actual treatment with your tax accountant.

After You Buy It

I don't know anything about electricity. Can I still do this? And what do I actually do after I buy one?

Yes, you can. You can start without any technical knowledge of electricity.

What an owner does after buying is not the day-to-day operation. Trading in the markets is handled by the operating company (the aggregator), equipment maintenance by a maintenance company, and the inspections required by law by a licensed chief electrical engineer.

The owner's job comes down to three things.

  • Read the monthly report. Revenue by market, the bid acceptance rate and availability are the bare minimum
  • Keep the records in your own hands. Monthly results, plus the BMS and EMS logs. These records are your evidence when you claim on insurance and when you eventually sell
  • Decide on contract renewals

There is one part you cannot hand off: deciding in the contracts who is responsible for what. Start operating with that left vague and, the day the equipment stops, you begin by hunting for whose job it is. The next question covers this.

Who actually runs it for me? How should I choose the company that operates it?

The instructions to charge and discharge come from an operating agent known as an aggregator. It bids into the markets and executes whatever it wins. The owner does not need to watch prices every day.

Pay is mostly performance-based, typically around 5 to 15% of market profit, meaning profit after deducting items such as the electricity used for charging and grid usage charges. The one thing to check without fail is what the 5% is 5% of. Take a project with 100 million yen of annual revenue where charging and other costs run at 40%.

Base for the calculationFee at 5%
Gross revenue of 100 million yen5 million yen
Market profit (net) of 60 million yen3 million yen

The same 5% nearly doubles what you pay. Japanese aggregators work off market profit almost without exception, but a separate market rate of 3 to 10% of gross revenue also exists. Have the contract spell out the definition of market profit, that is, what has been deducted before you get there.

Something else matters more than a low fee: how well they actually operate. Fee rates move in negotiation; operating skill does not change easily. Ask whether they can disclose the last 12 months of results, namely revenue per MW, the bid acceptance rate and availability.

And ask who takes the loss when market prices fall. Overseas there are contracts with a floor guarantee. Settle that point before you choose on price.

If it breaks, who fixes it?

This is the most misunderstood part of the business.

The usual explanation is that an operating company, a chief electrical engineer and the manufacturer are all you need. In practice, the operating company's job is trading in the markets, and it does not repair equipment. The licensed chief electrical engineer is legally responsible for supervising the safety of the whole facility, but the working convention is to cover the substation equipment, and few of them can work on the converters and control systems beyond it. The manufacturer covers only its own hardware.

The result is that projects genuinely exist where no contract says who goes and looks first when something goes wrong.

Why are these people so hard to find? Part of the reason lies in the rules themselves.

  • If you outsource the licensed role, the engineer must be able to reach the site within two hours (the two-hour rule)
  • There is also a cap on how many sites one person can take on (a point total of 33)
  • Government material projects a shortfall of about 1,000 Class 2 and about 800 Class 3 engineers in fiscal 2030

On top of that, the convention is that the warranty lapses if someone without manufacturer certification works on the equipment, licensed or not. It is not that the equipment cannot be fixed; it is that fixing it costs you something else.

So the owner's job is to appoint a maintenance company that covers the whole facility and serves as the single point of contact. You want to start putting this together roughly six months before grid connection. Securing a licensed engineer takes time as well.

One benchmark for reading a contract. Overseas, it is standard for annual availability of around 97% to be guaranteed in the contract. Ask whether your Japanese contract has the same clause. If it does not, work out whether that is because the exclusions are broad or because there is simply no one there to stand behind the guarantee.

If it stops running, how much do I lose?

It hits you through three channels.

First, the revenue that never arrives. For a 2 MW system, a rough daily figure is 66,000 yen on conservative assumptions, 151,000 yen in the standard case, and 288,000 yen on the high side. In the standard case, a week of downtime means about 1 million yen you simply do not receive.

Second, penalties in the balancing market. Once you have won a bid, you are obliged to respond exactly as instructed, and if you cannot respond you are charged an amount equal to the winning bid (1.0 times). Do that three times in one month on the same product and you are barred from trading that product. If the cause lies on the grid side, it does not count against you.

Third, penalties in the capacity market. This is the largest one. You are charged on your cumulative downtime blocks after subtracting 8,640 half-hour blocks per year (about 180 days), and the way those blocks are counted is what matters: an outage filed in advance as planned counts once, while an unplanned failure counts five times.

So the rules are not saying "never stop." They are saying "turn unplanned outages into planned ones." That is exactly why maintenance is worth spending money on. If you can catch the warning signs and convert an outage into a planned one, your exposure to penalties shrinks to roughly one-fifth at best.

Could it catch fire? Is it dangerous?

It is not zero. Let me be honest about it.

In Japan, a storage facility burned to the ground in Kagoshima Prefecture on March 27, 2024. It ignited shortly after 6 p.m., gave off white smoke and then exploded, and the fire was not out until 2:35 p.m. the following day — about 20 hours — with four firefighters injured. The presumed cause was not published until more than a year later, in May 2025: an internal short circuit was thought to have started the fire, and as the unit overheated, flammable vapor formed and built up, then ignited and exploded.

What you should take from this is less the fire itself than how long it takes to establish the cause. With a thermal runaway fire, pinpointing the ignition point requires the fire service, the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, and the manufacturer to work together, and that takes a year or more. And insurance does not pay out automatically just because there has been an accident. The burden of proving that the damage came from an accidental event sits with the operator. In this case, it was reported that more than a year after the fire the claim still had not been paid.

There is a framework for prevention. The liquid inside the cells counts as a hazardous material under the Fire Service Act (Class 4, Second Petroleum Group), so you have to consult the local fire department before you build. A revision of the standards that took effect in January 2024 requires outdoor installations to sit at least 3 m from buildings as a rule. Around-the-clock remote monitoring, so that anomalies are caught early, is also standard.

Three things to check as an owner. Whether there is a record that the consultation with the fire department actually took place. Whether the insurance has been designed to cover a fire. And whether you are holding enough cash on hand, on the assumption that no money comes in until the investigation into the cause is finished.

Will it be a nuisance to the neighbours? How much noise does it make?

What makes the noise is not the battery cells themselves. It is the cooling fans and the units that convert the electricity (the PCS). A battery site also runs around the clock as a matter of course, and shutting down at night is essentially not an option if you want the revenue. So it is the night-time limits that matter.

Noise sourceRough level at 1 m
PCS cooling fans (at full speed)70–80 dB
Cooling unit on the battery container72–75 dB
Transformer (hum)65–75 dB

The limits themselves vary by zoning category and time of day.

Zoning categoryDaytimeNight
Category 1 (low-rise residential only, etc.)45–50 dB40–45 dB
Category 2 (mid- and high-rise residential only, etc.)50–60 dB40–50 dB
Category 3 (neighborhood commercial, commercial, etc.)60–65 dB50–55 dB
Category 4 (quasi-industrial, industrial, etc.)65–70 dB55–65 dB

The remedies are distance, layout, and walls. As a rule, noise drops by about 6 dB every time the distance doubles, but on a site where containers stand in a row, at short distances the drop can be only about 3 dB. Simply placing the noisiest equipment as far as possible from the houses buys 5–10 dB. A sound barrier gains about 5 dB just by blocking the line of sight, and 10–15 dB if it is tall enough.

And here is the point that matters. When complaints come in after operation has started, the person who has to face them is the owner — not the operating company, not the contractor. Before you buy, check that the records of the pre-construction briefings for local residents are handed over with the project, and that your liability insurance covers noise complaints. If the nearest house is within 50 m of the site boundary, be especially careful.

The limits are set specifically by each prefectural governor within a national range. The hours that count as night also differ from one municipality to another, so always confirm with the municipality concerned.

Do I need insurance? Is the same cover as for solar fine?

Yes, you do. And the design you use for solar will not work here. The comprehensive movables insurance commonly used for solar excludes generating plants of 500 kW and above in its policy wording, so the storage facility itself cannot be covered by it in the first place.

You need six things.

  • Property insurance — for damage caused from outside, such as fire, wind, or flood
  • Machinery insurance — for when nothing hit it from outside but something inside broke and brought it to a stop (a burned-out PCS, a BMS failure, insulation breakdown in a transformer, and so on)
  • Business interruption (BI) insurance — to replace the income lost while it is broken and idle
  • Liability insurance — for claims by third parties: fire spreading, electric shock, toxic gas, noise complaints. For a 2 MW system, 100 to 500 million yen is standard; next to housing, 1 billion yen or more is recommended
  • Cyber insurance
  • Theft

Three points you cannot skip.

First, set the sum insured at what it would cost to build the same thing today (replacement cost), not at what you paid. Japanese property policies pay out in proportion to how much cover you bought: if the replacement cost is 600 million yen and you have insured only 300 million yen, a 200 million yen loss is paid out at only 100 million yen.

Second, the number of days of business interruption cover. Parts for this equipment take time to source (four to eight months for a PCS, and there are cases where actual restoration took six months to a year). Get quotes at 90, 180, and 365 days and compare them. A 14-day deductible period is the standard in this industry.

Third, batteries keep getting cheaper. Do not leave the replacement cost figure untouched; review it at every renewal. Over-insuring is money wasted on premiums.

Before You Start

If I were to lose money, what would cause it?

Mainly four things.

The first is a revenue plan that was too optimistic. You mistook the price cap for the going rate, you assumed everything you bid would clear, or you left costs out. Any one of these and less comes in than you expected.

The second is location. The grid connection charge comes in above what you assumed, or the area is simply a hard place to earn in. Identical equipment produces different results in different places.

The third is downtime. An unplanned failure counts five times as heavily as a planned outage under the capacity market penalty. Start operating before you have settled your maintenance arrangements and this is where you trip.

The fourth is regulatory change. The price cap on the payment for standing by (the balancing market) is already coming down — the cut from 15 yen to 10 yen has been decided. There is no avoiding it, so all you can do is pick a project that still works at the lower level.

Turn that around and the ways to protect yourself are also four.

  • Run the numbers on conservative assumptions. Do not buy a project that only works on bullish ones
  • Check the confirmed grid connection charge in the connection study response
  • Line up your maintenance contractor and your licensed personnel at least six months before operation starts
  • Only buy at a price that still avoids a loss under the worst assumptions

If I want to get out partway through, can I sell it?

You can. But to be honest, there are not many examples yet.

The earliest disclosure by a listed company that explicitly stated it had acquired an operating storage facility as a going business from a third party, and that we have been able to confirm, is dated July 31, 2026. What it acquired was a high-voltage-connected 2 MW / 8 MWh system that had begun trading in the balancing market in May 2026, and the buyer raised its earnings guidance for the period.

There is a hint here about selling. The buyer's biggest worry is whether the thing really earns what it is supposed to. Even a few months of actual operating records removes part of that worry, and that makes it easier to put a price on. The equipment has not gained a single yen of value; the price rises because of the record.

So if you have selling in mind, records matter. Before you buy, check that the contract leaves the monthly revenue reports, the bidding data, and the outage history in your own hands.

Note, though, that a project built with a subsidy requires prior approval to be sold for 17 years. If you intend to sell early, deciding not to use a subsidy at all is a legitimate option.

The secondhand market itself is still a thin one. There is no established method for assessing degradation, and no established view on whether a system can be relocated or repurposed. Do not build your plan on the assumption that you can sell whenever you like.

What happens to the equipment and the land after 20 years?

Nothing is fixed in advance. It is a question of what you set out in the contracts. There are three things to check.

First, the land contract. If the land is leased, check in the contract whether the term matches the life of the business, whether it can be renewed, and what happens when it ends. While you are at it, look at whether the lease survives a change of landowner, whether there is a mortgage on the land, and whether the landowner's consent is required to assign or sublease.

Second, the cost of removal. There is no legally mandated reserve for batteries of the kind that exists for solar. Whether you set money aside is your own decision. If you hold the asset through a company (an SPC), check that the company's capital is not below the estimated removal cost with no reserve and no guarantee behind it.

Third, what the equipment will be worth at that point. Here it is safer not to expect much. The secondhand disposal market has not developed yet, there is no established method for assessing degradation (state of health), and it is not settled whether a system can be relocated or repurposed. Whether it will fetch anything is not something anyone can read today. Replacing some of the cells to extend the life is an option, but note that if you swap equipment out, the grid connection requirements in force at that time will apply.

What should I do first?

There is an order to it.

If you already own land, start by finding out whether that land can be used at all: the distance to the grid, how flat it is, the access road, the distance to the nearest houses (the table in Q6). If the outlook is not good at this stage, there is no point going further.

If you have no land and want to buy in as a business, you will be looking for projects that already hold a grid connection right.

Either way, the first thing to decide is not the price. It is how much of it you want to take on yourself.

It is also worth knowing how long it takes to develop from bare land: 1–3 months to select a suitable site, 3–12 months to secure the land and the permits, 6–18 months for the grid connection study and contract, 3–6 months for design and procurement, and 12–48 months for construction and connection. Two years is the fastest the whole process runs; three to five years is normal. That is why most people buy projects where the connection study response and the land are already settled — it cuts one to three years off the development period.

One more thing: applying for a connection study costs money, at 200,000 yen per site (before tax). For a high-voltage connection the response normally takes two to three months, and the response document is valid for one year.
