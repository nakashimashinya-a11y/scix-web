1. What the capacity market main auction is

The capacity market is run by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators, Japan (OCCTO) to secure future supply capability (kW) in advance. Unlike JEPX, where energy (kWh) itself is bought and sold, and the balancing market, where balancing capacity (ΔkW) is traded, it buys "the existence, in a specified year, of the generating and discharging capability that will be needed." A resource is paid for being available as supply capability and standing ready, whether or not it actually runs.

At its center is the main auction, held four years before the delivery year. Batteries can take part from 1,000 kW; the auction uses a single price (the price of the last successful bidder applies to everyone), and contracts are in principle for one year. Existing and new resources compete on the same footing.

2. What the LDA (Long-Term Decarbonized Power Source Auction) is

The government created the LDA in FY2023 as part of the capacity market, and OCCTO manages it. A winner's revenue has two tiers, "defensive" and "offensive." Defensive revenue covers fixed costs such as construction, personnel and maintenance, and is paid at a fixed amount by OCCTO for 20 years, with annual adjustments based on core CPI. Offensive revenue is the profit from JEPX, the balancing market, the non-fossil value market and so on. The auction is multi-price: each winner's bid price becomes its contract price.

Market profit is partly refunded. What is known as the "90% refund rule" is actually three tiers, at refund rates of 95%, 90% and 85%. Only market trading profit is subject to refunds; 100% of the LDA fixed income stays with the operator.

Only new construction, replacement and renovation projects are eligible. For batteries, the minimum bid capacity is 30 MW from Round 2 (10 MW in Round 1), operation of 6 hours or more is required from Round 3, supply must start within 4 years of winning, and obtaining a connection study response is a prerequisite for bidding.

3. What a tolling contract is — fixing revenue with a long-term contract

A tolling contract hands the storage asset's capacity and dispatch rights to a counterparty (the offtaker) for a fixed fee, and the offtaker takes on the market-price risk. In Japan the term is used almost interchangeably with "offtake contract," and the structure is the same as a thermal-plant toll (a commissioned-processing arrangement).

Because a battery does not generate net electricity, a PPA that sells a fixed volume of energy (kWh) at a fixed price does not map onto it. What a long-term storage contract fixes is not energy, but capacity and dispatch rights. Who carries the market-price risk is the single axis on which these contracts can be read.

On that axis there are four types: pure tolling (the offtaker carries all of it), floor + revenue share (downside to the offtaker, upside shared), full-service optimisation (the owner retains the risk), and hybrids (part toll, part merchant). What the contract nails down is availability guarantees (fee reductions on a shortfall), capacity guarantees including degradation, whether the fee is CPI-linked, responsibility for charging energy, dispatch rights and cycle limits, and credit support (parent guarantee, letter of credit, rating triggers).

4. What full merchant means

Full merchant is an operating policy under which a battery project runs solely on market revenue from JEPX (the wholesale power market), the balancing market and the capacity market, without relying on fixed-income contracts such as the LDA. "Merchant" means having no long-term fixed-price contract, so whatever is sold in the market becomes revenue as it is; bearing the fluctuation of that market price yourself is called "merchant risk."

Capacity revenue comes not from the LDA's 20-year fixed capacity payment but from the main auction (4-year forward, single-year contract), and the owner bears all market price fluctuation. Because cash-flow forecasts are uncertain, project finance is difficult to structure, so self-funding or corporate finance is the main approach.

5. How capacity market clearing prices have moved, and the gap between areas

Main auction prices have swung widely. The Round 1 clearing price (¥14,137/kW) was a high price close to the upper end of the demand curve; in Round 2 (¥3,495/kW) it fell sharply to about a quarter of that, and it has been on an upward trend since. The overall average unit price (after the deduction for transitional measures):

RoundDelivery yearOverall average unit price
Round 1FY2024¥9,534/kW
Round 2FY2025¥3,109/kW
Round 3FY2026¥5,226/kW
Round 4FY2027¥7,847/kW
Round 5FY2028¥11,134/kW
Round 6FY2029¥13,303/kW

In Round 6, whose results came out on January 20, 2026, the overall average unit price of ¥13,303/kW was above even Round 5's ¥11,134. The total contracted amount, approximately ¥2.2094 trillion, is the highest on record.

Prices split by area. In Round 5, the highest areas (Hokkaido, Tohoku and Tokyo) were pinned to the price cap of ¥14,812/kW and the lowest (Hokuriku, Kansai, Chugoku and Shikoku) cleared at ¥8,785/kW, a gap of about 70%. In Round 6 the areas split into four blocks: Kyushu ¥15,112/kW, Tohoku and Tokyo ¥15,111/kW, Hokkaido ¥14,972/kW, and Chubu, Hokuriku, Kansai, Chugoku and Shikoku ¥12,388/kW; the gap between the highest and the lowest narrowed to about 20%. Because capacity market revenue is a fixed income per kW, the gap in unit price feeds straight through as a gap in annual revenue.

The award rate for batteries is over 96%, so almost all of the volume offered is awarded. Even so, battery bids in the main auction remain at about 0.1% of the total (about 240,000 kW), and most large batteries have gone to the LDA in pursuit of 20 years of fixed income.

Sources: OCCTO clearing results for each round / Round 6 (published January 20, 2026; corrected January 23) / Agency for Natural Resources and Energy, "On the Capacity Market" (January 23, 2026, Document 3-3). Covers the rounds published as of October 2, 2026, up to Round 6.

In Round 3, battery bids fell to 2,731MW, about 40% of Round 2, and the win rate rose to approx. 46% (LiB 36%, non-LiB 58%). The 19 projects awarded are the lowest of the three rounds, and the average award per project is approx. 66MW. The weighted average contract price (all decarbonized sources) was 58,000 yen/kW/year in Round 1 and 68,000 yen/kW/year in Round 2.

From Round 3, the total cap for batteries, pumped hydro and LDES became 0.8GW, of which the LiB + pumped hydro replacement category is 0.4GW. The required operating duration rose from 3+ hours to continuous 6+ hours, and cell procurement from a specific country was limited to less than 30%.

OCCTO published the Round 4 call for bids on September 2, 2026. Bids are accepted from January 19 to 26, 2027. Before that came business operator registration (October 13–16, 2026) and power source registration (October 19–23), and a bid cannot be placed if these deadlines are missed (the column was confirmed as of September 27, 2026; both registration windows had passed by November 22, 2026). The solicitation cap is 400MW for lithium-ion batteries (LiB) and 400MW for non-LiB batteries; pumped hydro and LDES share a separate 400MW category. "Certified" LiB, using cells made at the manufacturer's own site by a manufacturer holding a supply assurance plan certification under the Economic Security Promotion Act (OEM not included), is awarded first, and LiB without certification may be awarded only if capacity remains in the category.

7. How long contracts run, and who is signing them

On tenor, every domestic offtake we could confirm was 20 years. Hirohara, Ishikari, Ashiya, and the HDRE (HD Renewable Energy) deal added in April 2026 are all about 20 years, in line with the 20-year tenors of the capacity market and the LDA. Only the floor contract is short, at 10 years, and that is a different form from offtake. Overseas tolling ranges from 5 to 15 years. Because tenor sets the loan tenor, the floor's 10 years is a constraint in project finance design.

For grid-scale storage, the one party publishing 20-year, dispatch-rights-transfer, fixed-fee offtake at scale and across multiple deals as the offtaker (the party taking dispatch rights) is, at present, clearly Tokyo Gas: Hirohara 30 MW, Ishikari 30 MW and Ashiya 50 MW, and in April 2026 about 190 MW with HDRE. In other forms, MIRARTH and PowerX are tolling, and Bison and Englehart CTP are Japan's first 10-year floor (agreed in principle). Domestic toll, offtake and floor unit prices are undisclosed between the parties.

The financing precedents are now all in place, all arranged by MUFG Bank: Hirohara, a fixed-revenue type built on offtake as the credit base (Japan's first storage PF); Ishikari, an offtake type; and Tanagawa, a full-merchant non-recourse deal repaid from market revenue alone (a Japanese first).

Sources: Tokyo Gas (2024-04-24 / 2025-06-30), HDRE (2026-04-16), MIRARTH (2025-09-08), Kyodo PR Wire (Bison, 2025-07-07).

8. What to watch

  • The main auction uses single-year contracts, so the clearing price moves every year; sharp falls such as Round 1's ¥14,137 → Round 2's ¥3,495 have actually happened. A design that leans revenue on the capacity market alone is high-risk; it only works on the premise of stacking with JEPX and the balancing market. (en/column-capacity-market)
  • Winning the LDA is not guaranteed. In Round 2 only 1 in 5 bids succeeded, and the biggest differentiators are obtaining the connection study response in advance and low interconnection costs. With 6 continuous hours required from Round 3, 3–4 hour LiB batteries find it difficult to take part. (en/column-lda)
  • Full merchant has to build in the downward trend of balancing market price caps (19.51 to 15 yen, then 10 yen from delivery on September 1, 2026; a further cut to 7.21 yen is also part of the design). (en/column-merchant)
  • Tolling presumes that the payer of the fixed fee (a major utility, gas company, etc.) is investment-grade equivalent or backed by a parent guarantee. Weak credit means no long-term debt. (en/column-long-term-contract)

9. Which to choose — main auction, LDA, tolling or full merchant

While both fall under the "capacity market" framework, the main auction and the LDA have fundamentally different design philosophies.

ItemMain auctionLDA
Contract period1 year (annual)20 years in principle
Auction formatSingle-priceMulti-price
Eligible sourcesAll sources, existing and newNew construction, replacement, renovation only
Min. battery capacity1,000kW30,000kW (from Round 2)
Revenue from other marketsNo restrictions (all of it retained)Obligation to refund ~90%

The column's decision framework is four questions. Existing asset? Main auction only (the LDA is not open to it). Under 30MW? Main auction only. Need project finance? Prioritize the LDA. Can you bear market price volatility risk (and do you have market operations capability)? Main auction plus 3-market full merchant; if you want to limit risk, the LDA.

Sources that win the LDA do not take part in the main auction; if unsuccessful in the LDA, they can take part in the main auction again. For projects over 30MW, a "two-stage" approach — bid in the LDA first and switch to full merchant if unsuccessful — is also an option.

With long-term contracts included, the questions to ask are: which of merchant, tolling, floor or LDA; over how many years; at what price; and who is the counterparty. And whichever of the main auction and the LDA you take part in, whether you can secure a suitable site with low grid connection costs is the biggest variable in profitability.

Frequently asked questions

What is a tolling contract?

An arrangement in which the owner hands a storage asset's capacity and dispatch rights to a counterparty (the offtaker) for a fixed fee, and the offtaker takes on the market-price risk. In Japan it is used almost interchangeably with "offtake contract." Every domestic offtake we could confirm was 20 years.

What is the LDA (Long-Term Decarbonized Power Source Auction)?

A scheme the government created in FY2023 as part of the capacity market, managed by OCCTO. It is open to new construction, replacement and renovation only, and a winner is paid a fixed amount covering fixed costs for 20 years. Market trading profit is refunded in three tiers (refund rates of 95%, 90% and 85%), while 100% of the LDA fixed income stays with the operator. Batteries need 30 MW or more from Round 2 and 6 hours or more of operation from Round 3.

Can a battery take part in both the main auction and the LDA?

Sources that win the LDA do not take part in the main auction. If unsuccessful in the LDA, they can take part in the main auction again.
