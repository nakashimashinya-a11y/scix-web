The Capacity Market Main Auction and Batteries
— How Clearing Prices Have Moved and How They Feed Through to Revenue

The main auction of the capacity market, which secures future supply capability (kW), gives a battery fixed revenue "simply for being available." We set out, from OCCTO's published data, how clearing prices have swung from Round 1 to Round 6 (published January 2026), how unit prices differ by area, and how all this feeds through to the revenue of a battery business.

In three lines
  1. Japan's capacity market main auction, run by OCCTO, secures supply capability (kW) four years ahead; batteries can bid from 1,000 kW.
  2. The overall average unit price has risen every round since Round 2 (¥3,109/kW), reaching a record ¥13,303/kW in Round 6 (FY2029).
  3. Batteries win over 96% of what they bid, but contracts run one year and prices move by year and area, so revenue is stacked with JEPX and the balancing market.
Update as of November 2026

This article covers the rounds published as of October 2, 2026, up to Round 6. For the next round, Round 7 (delivery year FY2030), OCCTO's auction guidelines (July 30, 2026) set the bidding period at October 13–23, 2026 and the publication of clearing results for around December 2026 (planned). The demand curve published the same day sets the indicative price at ¥20,911/kW and the price cap at ¥31,366.5/kW, about twice Round 6's indicative price (¥10,075/kW). The pricing rule also changed from Round 6: where the highest winning bid exceeds the indicative price, the clearing price for resources that bid at or below the indicative price is the indicative price.

What the Capacity Market Main Auction Is — A Market That Secures Future Supply Capability

The capacity market is run by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators, Japan (OCCTO) to secure future supply capability (kW) in advance. It differs both from JEPX, where electrical energy (kWh) itself is bought and sold, and from the supply and demand balancing market, where balancing capacity (ΔkW) for raising or lowering output is traded: what the capacity market buys is "the existence, in a specified year, of the generating and discharging capability that will be needed." A resource is paid for being available as supply capability and standing ready, whether or not it actually runs.

At its center is the main auction, held four years before the delivery year. Batteries can take part from 1,000 kW or more; the auction uses a single price (the price of the last successful bidder applies to everyone), and contracts are in principle for one year (a single fiscal year). Existing and new resources compete on the same footing, which is the fundamental difference from the LDA (Long-Term Decarbonized Power Source Auction), which is open to new builds only.

The 3 electricity markets a battery faces:
• JEPX (wholesale electricity market) … Electrical energy (kWh) is bought and sold. The arena for arbitrage — charging when prices are low and discharging when they are high.
• Balancing market (operated by EPRX) … Balancing capacity (ΔkW = standby) is traded. Payment for waiting in a state where output can be raised or lowered.
• Capacity market (operated by OCCTO) … Secures future supply capability (kW). Fixed capacity revenue for availability.

This article focuses on the third, the capacity market, and within it the main auction. For the overall picture of how the three markets are combined (stacking), see The 3 Revenue Sources of Batteries.

How Clearing Prices Have Moved — From a Sharp Fall to a Rise

Main auction prices have swung widely from Round 1 to Round 6. The Round 1 clearing price (¥14,137/kW) was a high price close to the upper end of the demand curve; in Round 2 (¥3,495/kW) it fell sharply to about a quarter of that, and it has been on an upward trend since. Behind this is the growth of new electricity demand, such as data centers and semiconductor plants.

Round 2FY2025¥3,109/kWClearing price ¥3,495/kW (¥5,242 in Hokkaido and Kyushu), a sharp fall to about 1/4 of the previous round
Round 3FY2026¥5,226/kWMarket splitting by area occurred

Round 5FY2028¥11,134/kWTotal contracted amount approx. ¥1.8506 trillion
Round 6FY2029¥13,303/kWNew record high. Total contracted amount approx. ¥2.2094 trillion. Areas split into 4 blocks

The overall average unit price in Round 6 (delivery year FY2029), whose results came out on January 20, 2026, was ¥13,303/kW (after the deduction for transitional measures), above even Round 5's ¥11,134. The total contracted amount, approximately ¥2.2094 trillion, is the highest on record. For the first time apart from Round 1, area prices exceeded the indicative price (¥10,075/kW) in every area. That prices move widely from one year to the next is inherent to the main auction, where capacity revenue is won anew at each annual renewal; since it is not a long-term fixed income, a business has to be designed with this volatility itself built in.

Unit Prices Split by Area — Location Decides Capacity Revenue

Since Round 3, the capacity market has cleared not as a single national market but split by area. In Round 5 (delivery year FY2028), the gaps in unit price between areas are decisively large. Build the same battery, and its annual revenue from the capacity market changes greatly depending on the area where it is placed.

In Round 5, the gap between the highest areas (Hokkaido, Tohoku and Tokyo at ¥14,812/kW, pinned to the price cap) and the lowest (Hokuriku, Kansai, Chugoku and Shikoku at ¥8,785/kW) was about 70%. Because capacity market revenue is a fixed income per kW, this gap in unit price feeds straight through as a gap in annual revenue. When people say that in battery economics "location is everything," it is because not only grid connection costs but also these area characteristics of the capacity market and the balancing market act in combination. Location assessment that also draws on balancing market data by area is covered in The 3 Revenue Sources of Batteries as well.

How It Feeds Through to Battery Revenue — Base Income "Simply for Being Available"

For a battery, capacity revenue from the main auction is the most stable income it has: it "comes in even without running." The award rate for batteries is over 96%, so almost all of the volume offered is awarded; once a battery takes part, capacity revenue is all but certain. Stacking JEPX arbitrage and balancing market revenue on top of this base income is the basic form of revenue design for the main-auction (full merchant) model.

Points that matter for a battery in the main auction:
• Award rate over 96% — almost all of the volume offered is awarded, so little capacity revenue is missed
• Contracts are in principle for one year — directly exposed to the year-to-year movement of the clearing price (not a long-term fixed income)
• No restriction on revenue from other markets — all JEPX and balancing market revenue is kept
• Minimum capacity 1,000 kW — small projects can take part too

Note, however, that the main auction is a scheme in which "you keep everything, and in exchange carry the year-to-year price risk yourself." If a swing like the sharp fall from Round 1 to Round 2 occurs, capacity revenue for that year shrinks substantially. In a phase of rising prices it is a tailwind, but a design that leans revenue on the capacity market alone is high-risk; it only works on the premise of stacking with JEPX and the balancing market.

⚠ "Get into the capacity market and you are safe" is not true

Because the main auction uses single-year contracts, the clearing price moves every year. Sharp falls such as Round 1's ¥14,137 → Round 2's ¥3,495 have actually happened. Capacity revenue is only a base; if you want LDA-level revenue stability, that is a different scheme altogether.

If you want to lock in revenue with 20 years of fixed income, the LDA; if you want to go after the year-to-year price movements while keeping all revenue from other markets, the main auction — that is how the two divide.

Few Batteries Bid into the Main Auction

Despite the high award rate of over 96%, the battery capacity bidding into the main auction is very small. Battery bids in the main auction remain at about 0.1% of the total (about 240,000 kW), and most large batteries have gone to the LDA in pursuit of 20 years of fixed income.

This is not "because the main auction is unpopular." For new large batteries, the LDA's 20 years of fixed income, which makes project finance easier to arrange, suits fundraising better. Put the other way, for existing batteries and for projects below 30,000 kW that do not reach the LDA's minimum capacity, the main auction is the realistic option for earning capacity revenue. Existing batteries cannot take part in the LDA (new builds only) in the first place, so for them the main auction is the only choice.

Batteries suited to the main auction:
• Existing batteries (the LDA is for new builds only, so they cannot take part)
• Projects below 30,000 kW (they do not reach the LDA's minimum bid capacity)
• Full merchant businesses with market-trading know-how that can take on price risk

For new large-scale projects, or where long-term stable income comes first, the analysis centers on the LDA side. A 7-point comparison of the two schemes is in Main Auction vs LDA.

Where the Main Auction and the LDA Stand

Although they sit under the same "capacity market" umbrella, the main auction and the LDA differ fundamentally in design philosophy. This article focuses on the main auction side, but the decision to take part is always made by contrasting the two.

ItemMain auctionLDA
Contract periodIn principle 1 year (single fiscal year)In principle 20 years
MethodSingle price (uniform)Multi-price (pay-as-bid)
Eligible resourcesAll resources, existing and newNew builds, replacements and refurbishments only
Minimum capacity for batteries1,000 kW30,000 kW (from Round 2)
Revenue from other marketsNo restriction (kept in full)Obligation to refund most of it
Award rate for batteriesOver 96%About 20–46% (Rounds 1–3)

The main auction is a market that "secures next year's supply capability, including keeping existing resources in service"; the LDA is a market that "guarantees new decarbonized resources 20 years of investment recovery." The question is not which is better: the best answer changes with whether the battery is existing or new, its size, the owner's risk tolerance and how it is financed. The details of the LDA's 20 years of fixed income and its refund rules are in What Is the LDA (Long-Term Decarbonized Power Source Auction)?, and a framework for deciding between the two schemes is set out in detail in Main Auction vs LDA.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the capacity market main auction?
It is a market run by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators, Japan (OCCTO) to secure future supply capability (kW) in advance. The auction is held four years before the delivery year, and it pays resources for being available and standing ready. It uses a single-price method, contracts are in principle for one year, all generation sources, existing or new, can take part, and batteries can participate from 1,000 kW.
How have capacity market clearing prices moved?
The overall average unit price (after the deduction for transitional measures) was ¥9,534/kW in Round 1 (delivery year FY2024), ¥3,109 in Round 2, ¥5,226 in Round 3, ¥7,847 in Round 4, ¥11,134 in Round 5 and ¥13,303/kW in Round 6 (delivery year FY2029). After the clearing price fell sharply from ¥14,137 to ¥3,495 in Round 2, prices turned upward, and the total contracted amount in Round 6, approximately ¥2.2094 trillion, is the highest on record (OCCTO published figures; aggregation by the Agency for Natural Resources and Energy).
Does capacity market revenue depend on where the battery is located?
Yes. In Round 5, Hokkaido, Tohoku and Tokyo cleared at the price cap of ¥14,812/kW, Kyushu at ¥13,177, Chubu at ¥10,280, and Hokuriku, Kansai, Chugoku and Shikoku at ¥8,785/kW, a gap of about 70% between the highest and the lowest. In Round 6, Kyushu cleared at ¥15,112, Tohoku and Tokyo at ¥15,111, Hokkaido at ¥14,972, and Chubu, Hokuriku, Kansai, Chugoku and Shikoku at ¥12,388/kW; the gap narrowed to about 20%, but the same battery still earns different capacity market revenue depending on the area where it is installed.
How does the capacity market main auction differ from the LDA?
The main auction uses contracts of in principle one year, a single price and covers all generation sources; existing batteries and projects below 30,000 kW can take part, and they keep all of their revenue from other markets. The LDA (Long-Term Decarbonized Power Source Auction) is for new builds only, with contracts of in principle 20 years and multi-price bidding, and it carries an obligation to refund most revenue from other markets. A detailed comparison of the two is in the column "Main Auction vs LDA."
What are the price cap and the indicative price in the capacity market, and what were they?
Unit prices are capped by the demand curve. In Round 5 (delivery year FY2028), Hokkaido, Tohoku and Tokyo cleared at ¥14,812/kW, pinned to the price cap, while Kyushu cleared at 89% of the cap, Chubu at 69%, and Hokuriku, Kansai, Chugoku and Shikoku at 59%. In Round 6 (FY2029), for the first time apart from Round 1, area prices exceeded the indicative price (¥10,075/kW) in every area.
How does the capacity market differ from the balancing market?
The capacity market, run by OCCTO, pays for the existence of generating and discharging capability in a specified year, whether or not the resource actually runs. The balancing market, operated by EPRX, trades balancing capacity (ΔkW): payment for waiting in a state where output can be raised or lowered. JEPX is where electrical energy (kWh) itself is bought and sold, and a battery stacks revenue across all three.
