What is JC-STAR — the ★1 to ★4 levels and in-scope devices,
and when ★1 becomes mandatory (the gate is the "contract application")

JC-STAR is Japan's scheme that rates the security of IoT devices from ★1 to ★4, and a Grid Code revision that would require its ★1 for grid connection is in motion. Extra-high and high voltage from April 2027, low voltage under 50 kW from October 2027 — the draft text agreed by committee in March 2026 keys all of them to the "contract application." But the guideline that would carry the rule is not yet promulgated. This is a primary-source read of which devices, by when, and against which date.

In three lines
  1. JC-STAR (also written JCSTAR or JC STAR) is Japan's scheme that rates IoT device security from ★1 to ★4; ★1 and ★2 are self-declarations, ★3 and ★4 third-party certification.
  2. Under the draft revision, grid-connected solar and storage need ★1 products from April 2027 at extra-high and high voltage, and from October 2027 below 50 kW.
  3. Under the draft, the trigger is acceptance of the contract application, not the connection date, and the scope is the PCS, EMS, gateways and other IP devices involved in control.

JC-STAR — designed in 2024 and operational since March 2025 — is fast ceasing to be a "nice-to-have" quality mark. Through three separate channels — grid connection, subsidies, and public procurement — it has become a variable that shapes the equipment selection of a grid-scale battery itself. The largest of these is the planned mandate for ★1-certified products in the April 2027 revision of the grid interconnection technical requirements. And yet the phrase "mandatory from April 2027" is half right and half premature. The direction is fixed; the text that would codify it has not been promulgated. This column separates what the draft revision has settled from what cannot yet be asserted, checking each against primary sources.

What is JC-STAR — a scheme that shows IoT device security in stars

JC-STAR is a labeling scheme that evaluates the security functions an IoT device should have and shows how far it conforms as a "number of stars." Its official name is the Security Requirements Conformity Assessment and Labeling Scheme (セキュリティ要件適合評価及びラベリング制度). It is run by the IPA (Information-technology Promotion Agency, Japan) under the supervision of the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry. It covers IoT products in general that have IP communication functions.

It assesses functions such as encrypted communication, access control, secure firmware updates and a vulnerability-handling process; the higher the level met, the more stars. The first thing to grasp in practice is the switch in assessment method: ★1 and ★2 are self-declarations of conformity; ★3 and ★4 are third-party certification. For grid connection it is the entry level, ★1, that matters for now, but knowing what each level demands makes it less likely that a maker's explanation or a label is misread. The level and assessment method of each of the four tiers are in the table in "How JC-STAR is built" below.

"More stars is better for grid connection" is not the case. What is required of grid connection for now is the use of ★1-certified products; it is not a scheme in which ★2 or above makes the grid-connection assessment more favorable. The star level shows how strong a security level is met; the first point to check is whether the level the Grid Code requires (= ★1) is met. Some criteria for the higher levels are still under study, so check public information for the latest.

Why a battery needs cybersecurity at all

A grid-scale battery is no longer "a box that stores electricity." It is a highly networked asset that talks to an aggregator's cloud platform over the internet around the clock, bidding into the JEPX spot market, the balancing market and the capacity market and cycling charge and discharge in real time. This "always-on external communication" is what creates the new risk. If the gateway or EMS that controls a battery is attacked and an unauthorized mass charge/discharge is executed at scale, the consequence need not stop at a local outage — it can propagate through frequency deviation toward a large-scale blackout.

This is not abstract. In May 2024, a remote-monitoring device for solar PV installations (Contec's SolarView Compact) had a known vulnerability exploited; a government document records a scale of "about 800 units" abused for fraudulent transfers in internet banking. The device's maker states that, because the affected product has no output-control function, there is no direct impact on the grid. The government text reads "about 800 units … were abused," and from the primary source alone one cannot tell how many were actually taken over versus how many merely held the vulnerability — so we do not assert a figure here. Either way, the case showed plainly that internet-exposed communication devices in the renewables-and-storage stack become real attack targets. That is the direct backdrop to the state's effort to wire an IoT-product security conformity scheme into the technical requirements for grid connection.

The path to here reads more clearly laid out by committee session.

For solar and storage, the committee decided to require the use of JC-STAR ★1-certified products in the April 2027 revision of the grid interconnection technical requirements; low voltage under 50 kW from October 2027. At this stage the wording was "newly connected to the grid on or after April 2027" — ambiguous as to application versus connection.

Trigger unified to "contract application"; wind / fuel cells added

The draft code revision made the basis explicit — "the contract application for grid access" — for EHV and HV as well. It also presented wind at April 2027 (limited to the gateway firewall) and fuel cells at April 2028, which the members endorsed.

Pre-promulgationas of June 2026

No text yet — guideline, interconnection technical requirements, Grid Interconnection Code

The formal revised versions incorporating the cybersecurity requirements are not yet promulgated. ANRE's published grid interconnection technical-requirements guideline remains the Dec 1, 2024 revision, and the Grid Interconnection Code (Japan Electric Association) is described as "to be reflected following the guideline revision." As of June 12, 2026, no scheduling information for a 22nd committee had been posted.

This is the heart of the column. In the first edition of this piece (April 2026), for HV the revised wording read only "newly connected to the grid," and we held back because one could not tell whether the basis was the connection (interconnection / commissioning) or the application. The 21st committee's draft unified that basis to "the contract application for grid access." EHV and HV, like LV, now read as keyed to the application stage rather than "the moment of connection (interconnection / commissioning)" — and that is the single biggest update here. The question that decides a project's life or death — "is it enough to file the connection-contract application by the deadline, or must the project reach commissioning in time?" — has acquired a direction.

It is worth quoting the draft itself. Note 1 to the draft guideline revision (21st committee, Document 4, p.9) reads — in unofficial translation — "the requirement applies to projects that file a contract application for grid access on or after the following dates: solar PV and battery storage facilities interconnecting at extra-high voltage and high voltage: April 2027." Not "projects that connect," but "projects that file a contract application." EHV and HV are named explicitly; this is not a low-voltage-only provision.

Meanwhile, the policy-briefing decks still carry the 20th-committee-era shorthand. Document 5 of the Electricity Sub-WG's 19th meeting (Feb 12, 2026) says "newly connected to the grid on or after April 2027" — read alone, that looks like a connection-date trigger. But it is not the regulatory wording. The same agency, ANRE, explains in its Next-Generation Power Grid WG material (Document 2, Feb 9, 2026) that, in unofficial translation, "projects filing an interconnection contract application after the revision of the technical requirements will, in principle, be unable to connect non-compliant facilities to the grid" — an application-based framing. Do not conflate a slide's summary with the draft provision; getting this wrong shifts a project's entire timeline arithmetic.

But this is exactly where one must not over-assert. "The contract application is the basis" / "filing is safe" can be said only to the extent that the draft revision has framed it that way. The texts — the guideline, each utility's interconnection technical requirements, and the Grid Interconnection Code — are all pre-promulgation; the stage is no more than "a draft revision endorsed by the deliberative committee." Further, for HV self-consumption types (roughly under 500 kW) and for projects built with the same PCS as a low-voltage interconnection, the Japan Photovoltaic Energy Association (JPEA) has requested a grace period, and the 21st minutes leave this point unresolved, handed to coordination between ANRE and JPEA. The field reading that "if you've already applied, you're clear" should be settled by the promulgation of the text, or by a direct query to the general transmission and distribution utility (TSO/DSO) with jurisdiction.

JPEA's request is concrete. At the 21st committee, for self-consumption types of 500 kW or less and for projects that — though receiving at high voltage — are built with the same product as a low-voltage interconnection's PCS, it asked for the same grace until October 2027 as low voltage. ANRE responded "to consult case by case," and the chair took it up with "ANRE and JPEA to coordinate as a matter of urgency." In short, even at high voltage there is room for application to be pushed back depending on a project's character. If you hold self-consumption or small-scale projects, the follow-up on this coordination looks worth tracking.

Which parts of a battery are in scope

On where the line is drawn, the two extremes one hears in the field are both wrong.

COMMON MISREAD ✕

"Make one gateway ★1-compliant and you're exempt"

The PCS itself is a communication device that performs remote control and monitoring, so it can be in scope on its own. A split architecture that concentrates control communication into a gateway is an effective hedge, but it does not let the whole system off by itself.

COMMON MISREAD ✕

"Every network device needs ★1"

General-purpose IP communication devices not involved in control (routers, etc.) are not subject to the obligation. The draft narrows the in-scope devices to "IP communication devices involved in control."

The correct line, in the draft's own wording, is: "among the control systems with communication functions adopted by a distributed energy resource, the devices that use IP communication." The center of gravity is the PCS and the EMS. Exclusion provisions are also set, but their reach is asymmetric by device. Internet-connection routers and the like are excluded only for general electrical work (consumer-grade installations), whereas monitoring cameras mediated by a gateway are excluded for all electrical works, including business-use. Conversely, even a device outside the in-scope range is pulled in if it has a control function, or if it links directly to a principal constituent product without passing through a gateway. The crux is that evaluation is "as a communication-and-control system," not "device by device."

"JC-STAR in-scope products" has two meanings. One is the kinds of devices the scheme covers, in the table above. The other is the list of products that have already obtained stars, which the IPA publishes as the "JC-STAR conformance-label product list" (also downloadable in Excel). A table picking out which makers have obtained ★1 for grid-scale battery PCS, EMS and gateways is in "The state of JC-STAR certified products" below. Before procurement, check that the device you will use is on this list and that its status is "valid." Conversely, battery cells and packs do not communicate directly with the network and are out of scope, so whether to adopt an overseas battery is little constrained by JC-STAR. What is in question is the communication module of the PCS that handles external communication, and the EMS and gateway. This distinction, and how to check a PCS's ★1 status when equipment is changed, are covered in Equipment changes after the connection study reply.

What about replacing existing equipment

This may look like a story only about new projects, but existing equipment is not entirely unaffected. The Grid Code has a general principle that even for equipment already interconnected, the latest requirements apply when it is replaced or when a device such as a power conditioner is swapped. However, how the cybersecurity requirement is treated when a PCS is replaced or an EMS is renewed is not stated explicitly in the 21st committee's draft revision. Since the application trigger is written as "projects that file a contract application," the relationship between replacement of existing equipment and this trigger is not yet settled in the text — that is the accurate way to put it. We avoid asserting anything here and leave it as an open point to confirm in the revised guideline text and in the FAQs of METI and each general transmission and distribution company.

Source: ANRE Next-Generation Power Grid WG, Document 4-1 (May 24, 2024) (general principle of applying the latest requirements at replacement / device swap)

How JC-STAR is built — ★1 is a self-declaration

JC-STAR is a labeling scheme that evaluates the security functions an IoT device should hold (encrypted communication, access control, secure firmware update, a vulnerability-handling process, and so on) and expresses the result as a number of stars. The point a practitioner cannot overlook is that ★1 is a self-declaration of conformity. It can be obtained on the operator's declaration; external third-party assessment enters not at ★2 but at ★3 and above (★2 is still self-declaration). So a claim of "★1-compliant" still warrants cross-checking against the conformance-label product list to confirm it is actually held.

A conformance label is valid for up to two years from issuance, and ★1 can be renewed in two-year increments. The ERAB guideline: an earlier de-facto mandate

Ahead of the April 2027 Grid Code revision, the aggregation business already requires JC-STAR conformance. The "Cybersecurity Guideline for ERAB Ver3.0," finalized on May 22, 2025, added a requirement that, when a resource aggregator newly introduces an IoT device as a control target, it select a product meeting JC-STAR ★1 or above. Specifically, on the aggregator–gateway link (R4) and the aggregator–energy-device direct communication (R6) it is framed as "recommended" (effectively required), and on the gateway–energy-device link (R5) as "advised" (best-effort). Ver3.0 also newly addresses cloud-type control that does not pass through a physical gateway, and gateway-independent demand response. As of June 2026 no revision beyond Ver3.0 is confirmed, so this is the latest version.

METI's DR/VPP-related subsidies also set ERAB-guideline conformance as a grant condition, so using these makes the selection of JC-STAR-conformant equipment effectively mandatory.

Source: METI, "Cybersecurity Guideline for ERAB Ver3.0" (May 22, 2025)

What changes for a battery developer — three channels

JC-STAR is not a "nice-to-have" quality mark. It is already biting as a real constraint through three channels: grid connection, subsidies, and procurement.

It connects directly to whether you can connect to the grid. Under the draft, projects that file a grid-access contract application on or after April 2027 are premised on the use of ★1-certified products. Build a storage facility with a vast investment, then stumble at grid connection because the equipment does not meet the requirement, and you can neither sell power nor join the markets — a direct path to the cash flow being cut off, the so-called stranded-asset risk. As noted above, this is a draft, pre-promulgation; it is not yet a stage at which one can flatly say "definitely mandatory from April 2027."

It is a grant condition for subsidies. In DR/VPP-related subsidies and in the grid-scale storage subsidies administered by SII, ERAB-guideline conformance (i.e., use of JC-STAR-conformant equipment) is built in as a grant condition. Choose non-conformant equipment and you fall outside the subsidy, which substantially breaks the project's economics.

It is advancing in public procurement. The direction is to require ★1 or above for IoT devices procured by government bodies, and a similar movement is spreading to local public bodies.

Working with overseas hardware — and its new trap

In the grid-scale storage market, overseas makers hold strong cost competitiveness. Since CAPEX drives IRR, ruling out cost-advantaged overseas hardware from the outset is unrealistic. What helps here is the distinction that JC-STAR targets "IoT devices that communicate with the internet," while the battery cell/pack itself is out of scope. This is not because a clause names cells/packs and "excludes" them; it is a consequence of the scope definition limiting the target to "devices that use IP communication." SII's grid-scale storage subsidy guidelines likewise frame it so that a device without IP-communication capability obtains ★1 as a configuration incorporating a protocol-conversion device — showing that a non-IP device is not a direct target. The battery itself is electrochemical hardware managed by a BMS and does not communicate directly with the internet. So adopting an overseas battery, in itself, is not constrained by JC-STAR. What is in question is the communication module of the PCS that talks to the external network, and the EMS / gateway above it.

Where an overseas maker's PCS has not obtained JC-STAR, the “gateway-separation architecture” has often been suggested: place a ★1-certified EMS / gateway as the security boundary and control the PCS behind it over a local link. ANRE's July 2026 FAQ narrowed this sharply. A PCS with IP communication needs ★1 even behind the gateway (A6), and logical separation does not exempt it (A7). The set-up works only where the PCS has no IP communication at all — for example, Modbus RTU over RS485 only (A9).

A ★1-certified EMS / gateway sits as the checkpoint; the PCS connects over local protocols (e.g., Modbus). The battery itself is out of JC-STAR scope, so the cost-optimal maker can be chosen.

So gateway separation is not a way round ★1 for a networked PCS. Check whether the PCS maker offers an RS485-only configuration (GoodWe announced one in August and September 2026), and whether the grid operator accepts it, project by project.

And the workaround itself creates a new risk. Concentrating control communication into a single gateway means that gateway becomes a single point of failure (SPOF). If the checkpoint goes down, the control of everything behind it stops together. Availability and redundancy design now arise as a separate question from security. If you adopt gateway separation, decide on it on the premise of designing the SPOF countermeasures (redundancy, fail-safe) as a set.

Due diligence at equipment selection

In equipment procurement, on top of the conventional electrical-safety certifications (JET certification, etc.), the following checks have become essential.

Toward the PCS maker. Confirm clearly with the Japanese subsidiary which JC-STAR status the communication module is in — already obtained, in process, or what the roadmap is. The efficient route is to pin down the public information on IPA's conformance-label product list first, then query.

Toward the aggregator / EMS. The conformance status of the gateway / EMS devices used, whether they support multi-maker batteries, and which markets (including primary reserve) the architecture can enter. Since the in-scope range at the EMS layer is still being sorted out, choosing an operator that has already obtained conformance is itself a hedge.

Periodic monitoring of the IPA list. Status can be not only "valid" but "grace before expiry," "expired," or "revoked." Validity runs up to two years from issuance; monitor periodically, allowing for status changing after a procurement decision.

The "non-tariff barrier" aspect, and international mutual recognition

Said plainly, a nation-specific security certification like JC-STAR works in part as a technical entry barrier (a non-tariff barrier) to overseas makers. Even a globally proven company must shoulder the lead time and cost of reworking communication protocols, redesigning firmware, and obtaining certification. At the same time, for an overseas maker that does obtain conformance it becomes a game-changer that lifts credibility in the Japanese market at a stroke. Overseas players — SMA (Germany), Power Electronics (Spain), Samsung SDI (Korea), SEETEL (Taiwan) — have already obtained it, and with security resilience backed by a public database, they have won a position to compete on even terms with the domestic heavy-electrical incumbents.

The one thing not to conflate: mutual recognition with PSTI / CLS is a different matter from compliance with the EU CRA. The CRA is not a mutual-recognition framework but a standalone regulation, carrying lifecycle-wide obligations — maintaining an SBOM (software bill of materials), reporting vulnerabilities to ENISA, and CE conformity. Obtaining JC-STAR ★1 does not, by itself, amount to CRA compliance. "Got ★1, therefore CRA-clear" does not follow.

Decided / still open

The debate around JC-STAR does not end as "a security story." It is a business risk that bears on equipment selection, procurement and schedule. Precisely for that reason, not mistaking what is confirmed for what is merely draft or under coordination is what helps most. Here is where things stand as of 15 November 2026, split in two.

Decided (safe to assert)

  • Timing: EHV/HV April 2027 / LV under 50 kW October 2027 / wind April 2027 (limited to the GW firewall) / fuel cells April 2028
  • Scope concept: PCS, EMS, etc., plus control-involved IP devices are in scope. General routers and GW-mediated monitoring cameras are excluded
  • The trigger is "acceptance of the contract application" (Article 88 of the Network Operation Guidelines: the point at which the documents are in order and receipt of the deposit is confirmed). A connection-study application alone does not keep the former treatment (ANRE FAQ)
  • ★1 is a self-declaration (third-party assessment from ★3)
  • Regulatory vehicle: revision of the guideline + each utility's interconnection technical requirements + the Grid Interconnection Code (the Network Operation Guidelines unchanged; not a ministerial-ordinance amendment)
  • Mutual recognition with the UK PSTI Act (from January 2026) and Singapore CLS (from 1 June 2026) is in force
  • The ERAB guideline Ver3.0 is the latest
  • Existing plants: not retroactive. When storage is added, only the added devices; when a device is replaced, the replacement in principle needs ★1 (a written plan can defer it if the burden is excessive). Switching to FIP or reaching the end of FIT: not applicable if equipment is unchanged (ANRE FAQ July 2026; draft guideline October 2026)→ COLUMN 52-3 Existing plants
  • The request for grace for HV projects that use the same PCS as low voltage was "decided to proceed as proposed" (22nd Grid Code Review Committee, Document 3, 21 August 2026) = the grace is not widened
  • The draft guideline was opened for public comment (e-Gov case number 620340012, 6 October to 6 November 2026). The requirement is "equipment bearing a conformance label of ★1 (Level 1) or above under the IPA labeling scheme"
  • A PCS with IP communication needs ★1 even behind a gateway (FAQ A6, A7). Devices without IP communication are out of scope (A9)

Still open (do not assert)

  • The post-revision texts of the guideline, each company's interconnection technical requirements and the Grid Interconnection Code. The draft guideline was open for public comment from 6 October to 6 November 2026. As of 15 November 2026 the post-revision texts of the ten general transmission and distribution companies are not to be found. ANRE says it will amend each company's interconnection technical requirements "within FY2026"→ e-Gov / ANRE / each T&D company's notices
  • Own-brand ★1 acquisition by major Chinese makers such as Huawei and Sungrow (none on the 12 November 2026 list either)→ latest IPA list / COLUMN 52

What a battery operator should do now

Even before the text is promulgated, there is plenty to move on in practice.

① Inventory communication/control devices and check ★1 conformance. List them around the PCS and EMS and check each device individually. Pay special attention to the line between a legacy PCS with RS-485 only and a cloud-connected EMS.

② For HV projects, check the acceptance date of the contract application. What matters is whether complete documents and the deposit are confirmed before 1 April 2027 (ANRE FAQ A1). A connection-study application or response is not enough.

③ For projects premised on a Chinese-affiliated PCS, judge the switch feasibility early. HUAWEI / SUNGROW hold no own-brand ★1 on the 12 November 2026 list. If you plan to adopt them, query the acquisition schedule, and ask the grid operator whether a configuration without IP communication would be accepted.

④ Re-read subsidy public-offering guidelines through the ERAB / JC-STAR lens. Check whether guideline conformance or the use of conformant equipment has become a grant condition or a scoring factor.

⑤ A gateway-as-checkpoint set-up works only where the PCS has no IP communication. A networked PCS needs ★1 itself (FAQ A6, A7). If you adopt it, design the SPOF countermeasures (redundancy, fail-safe) as a set.

⑥ Given that ★1 is a self-declaration, back conformance claims against the product list.

The era of choosing equipment on the hardware spec sheet and the per-kWh unit price alone is over. Cybersecurity compliance has become a variable that decides a project's success or failure. And yet the substance of that variable — the basis date, the in-scope range, the transitional measures — is not fully fixed as text. Separating what is decided from what is not, and back-calculating from connectivity and timeline project by project, looks like the right approach for now.

Frequently asked questions

Is the JC-STAR ★1 requirement triggered by the grid-connection date or by the connection-contract application?

In the draft revision at the 21st Grid Code Review Committee (March 31, 2026), the basis was unified, extra-high and high voltage included, to "the contract application for grid access." It reads as keyed to the application stage rather than the connection date (interconnection / commissioning). However, the text is not yet promulgated, so it is not yet a stage at which this can be asserted.

When does the ★1 requirement start for high-voltage (HV) batteries?

Under the draft revision, extra-high and high voltage solar and storage are from April 2027, applying to projects that file a grid-access contract application in or after that month. This is the plan at the stage where the committee endorsed the draft revision, and it is fixed on promulgation of the text.

When does it start for low voltage under 50 kW, and why later than HV?

Low voltage under 50 kW applies from October 2027, six months later than HV. It is arranged as a transitional measure, delaying by six months in consideration of clearing channel inventory such as existing power conditioners.

Can foreign-made PCS be used, and what must they satisfy?

Battery cells and packs do not communicate directly with the network and are out of scope, so adopting an overseas battery is not itself constrained. What is in question is the communication module of the PCS that handles external communication, and the EMS and gateway. If the PCS has IP communication, the PCS itself needs ★1 even behind a ★1-certified gateway (ANRE FAQ A6, A7). Devices without IP communication are out of scope (A9). Overseas makers such as SMA (Germany), Power Electronics (Spain), Samsung SDI (Korea) and SEETEL (Taiwan) have already obtained ★1.

Are replacements or updates of existing equipment in scope?

The Grid Code has a general principle that even existing equipment is subject to the latest requirements when it is replaced or swapped. However, how the cybersecurity requirement is treated when a PCS is replaced or an EMS is renewed is not stated explicitly in the 21st draft revision, and it is undecided at present. It is a point to confirm in the revised guideline text and in the FAQs of METI and each general transmission and distribution company.

Does ★1 require third-party certification?

★1 is a self-declaration of conformity and can be obtained on the operator's declaration. External third-party assessment enters from ★3 (★2 is also a self-declaration). A claim of "★1-compliant" therefore still warrants cross-checking against IPA's certified-product list.

What is the difference between JC-STAR ★1, ★2, ★3 and ★4?

The number of stars shows how high a security level is required. ★1 is the baseline requirements (16 items), centered on forced change of the initial password, secure communication and proof of carrying no known vulnerabilities. ★2 adds requirements by product class, ★3 is for critical infrastructure (tamper resistance and hardware-level security elements), and ★4 is the top tier, testing resistance to APT attacks. The assessment method is self-declaration for ★1 and ★2, and third-party certification for ★3 and ★4.

Which level (how many stars) applies to grid-scale batteries?

What the revision of the Grid Code (grid interconnection technical requirements) planned for April 2027 requires is the use of ★1-certified products. The draft revision does not suggest that ★2 or above would be required across the board for grid connection at present. However, this is a summary on the basis of the draft before promulgation, and some criteria for the higher levels are still under study, so check public information for the latest.

Which devices exactly are in scope of JC-STAR? Are battery cells and routers also covered?

The core is the PCS (inverter) and the EMS / control unit; gateways and remote-monitoring units are also in scope. The BMS is conditionally in scope, as exemplified in subsidy programs. On the other hand, battery cells and packs do not communicate directly with the network and are out of scope, and general-purpose IP communication devices not involved in control (routers, etc.) are not subject to the obligation either. The draft narrows the scope to "IP communication devices involved in control," and the crux is that evaluation is as a communication-and-control system, not device by device.

Is a solar PV PCS also in scope of JC-STAR? How does it differ from batteries?

Yes. Under the draft revision, use of ★1-certified products is the premise for both solar and storage from April 2027 at extra-high and high voltage and from October 2027 at low voltage under 50 kW (the basis being the grid-access contract application). Wind is shown at April 2027 and fuel cells at April 2028. There is no difference in star level by generation type, and the entry level is ★1 in every case.

Where can I find the list of JC-STAR certified products?

The IPA publishes it as the "JC-STAR conformance-label product list," which can also be downloaded in Excel. As of its 12 November 2026 update it had 395 entries, all ★1. Of these, 189 relate to solar and storage by our count; the full list with registration number, acquisition date and expiry date is in COLUMN 52-2. Before procurement, check that the device is on the list and that its status is "valid."

Do Huawei or Sungrow hold JC-STAR ★1?

Not under their own names. The 12 November 2026 edition of the IPA list has no own-brand entry for the 16 major Chinese makers, including Huawei and Sungrow. Nikkei (1 July 2026) reported the Chinese side's claim that applications are being rejected, and METI does not disclose individual decisions, so the reason cannot be verified from outside. If a project is premised on a Chinese-affiliated PCS, ask for the acquisition schedule and ask the grid operator whether a configuration without IP communication would be accepted.

Update (November 2026)

The grace for self-consumption types, etc., which the table marks as undecided: material for the 22nd Grid Code Review Committee (21 August 2026) says that after discussion it was decided "to proceed as proposed". The grace period was not widened. On replacing existing equipment, ANRE answered in its FAQ in July 2026, and the October 2026 draft revision of the Guideline on Technical Requirements for Grid Interconnection put it into the text. It does not apply retroactively to existing plants. When you replace a device, the new device generally needs ★1. Only in cases of "excessive burden", such as having to replace other devices too, can you submit a plan and get a grace period.
