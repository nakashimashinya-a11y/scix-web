Battery Storage Market Notes, July 2026 issue

"Grid-scale battery storage" (a large battery connected to the transmission grid that stores electricity and releases it) earns money by selling the "capacity" to correct electricity surpluses and shortages. It also uses the price differences between times of day. These notes use published figures and our own records to show this month's moves in figures and tables. This issue is a back issue, produced retroactively in November 2026. The grid connection study totals are as of the time of production (October 19, 2026).

01

The 3 revenue sources of a battery storage plant (how to read these notes)

A battery storage plant (grid-scale battery) earns revenue from 3 markets. ① The balancing market (EPRX) is where the "capacity" to correct electricity surpluses and shortages is sold to grid operators. ② The wholesale electricity market (JEPX) is where you buy and store electricity at cheap times and sell it at expensive times. ③ The capacity market (OCCTO) is where the government buys future electricity supply capacity in advance. These notes present the figures in the order of these 3 markets. The ①②③ at the start of each section title shows which market it covers.

Source: Rules of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX) and the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO).

02

Three trends this month

We picked 3 moves this month from the figures of the 3 markets.

  • In the balancing market (EPRX), the bid-to-cover ratio of Primary reserve (online) went above 100% at 106.8% in June 2026. This month it was 119.7%, and the average winning price was 4.35 yen/kW per 30 min.
  • In the balancing market (EPRX), 57.3% of Tertiary reserve 2 sales in July 2026 came from the top 10% of 30-minute slots by sales.
  • In the wholesale electricity market (JEPX), the monthly average spread between the highest 4 hours and the lowest 4 hours in July 2026 was 13.17 yen/kWh in Tokyo and 15.71 yen/kWh in Kyushu.
03

① Balancing market (EPRX) | 7 products, and prices by area

The balancing market is where grid operators buy, every 30 minutes, the "capacity" to correct electricity surpluses and shortages. Products are divided by how fast they change output (Primary reserve responds to changes in frequency; Secondary and Tertiary reserves respond to dispatch instructions from the grid operator). Primary reserve is the fastest and Tertiary reserve 2 is the slowest. Primary reserve is a product that senses changes in frequency by itself and responds. It has 2 categories: "online", where the grid operator monitors operation over a dedicated line, and "offline", where no dedicated line is laid and a record of operation is submitted afterwards. These are offered and traded separately, so we show them separately. A Composite product is a bid that combines several products. Prices are set in yen per 1 kW of available capacity per 30 minutes. kW is a unit of the size of power that can be delivered, which is different from kWh, the amount of electricity. Each 30-minute trading round is called a "30-minute slot". The figure shows the range of winning prices by area (by the area where the power source is located). The thin line runs from the lowest to the highest winning bid in the month, the thick band is the price range that covers 8 of every 10 30-minute slots with winning bids, and the dot is the average winning price. Nationwide figures are in the table in the "Market figures" section.

By area, the highest average winning price in the balancing market was Primary reserve (offline) in Tohoku at 14.49 yen/kW per 30 min, and the lowest was Tertiary reserve 2 in Shikoku at 0.38 yen/kW per 30 min.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our compilation.

① Balancing market (EPRX) | Primary reserve (online) became a competition for slots

Primary reserve is the fastest product. It responds to changes in frequency and changes output within 10 seconds (online) or within 30 seconds (offline). Batteries are often used for it. The left side is the online category and the right side is the offline category. The bars show the bid-to-cover ratio (bid volume ÷ volume procured by grid operators). Above 100% means more bids came in than the volume procured. Even below 100%, some bids do not win (see the win rate in the "Market figures" table). The bars rise both when bids increase and when the procured volume falls. The line shows the average winning price. These are monthly figures from April 2025, the data we have.

In the balancing market, the bid-to-cover ratio of Primary reserve (online) went above 100%, at 106.8% in June 2026 and 119.7% this month. Its average winning price was 2.89 yen/kW per 30 min in April 2025 and 4.35 yen/kW per 30 min this month. Primary reserve (offline) had a bid-to-cover ratio of 65.1% and an average winning price of 10.25 yen/kW per 30 min this month. From April 2025 to this month, the procured volume fell from 3,066.1 MW to 1,408.5 MW for online and from 1,122.5 MW to 495.7 MW for offline.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our compilation.

② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | Average prices

Wholesale electricity is electricity bought and sold between power companies and retailers. Its price is set on the "spot market" of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX) (a market where electricity for the next day is traded the day before). "System (nationwide)" is the price when all of Japan is treated as one market (the system price). When the transmission lines between regions become congested, prices split by area from Hokkaido to Kyushu (area prices).

The monthly average of the System (nationwide) price on the wholesale market (JEPX) in July 2026 was 17.91 yen/kWh, compared with 12.75 yen/kWh in the same month last year.

Source: Spot market trading results published by the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX). Simple average of 30-minute prices. Our compilation.

② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | How the price moves through the day

The wholesale electricity price changes every 30 minutes. The figure shows the daily shape, averaging the price at the same time of day across the month. If a battery charges at cheap times and discharges at expensive times, the difference becomes its revenue.

The daily low and peak of the wholesale price were 15.28 yen/kWh around 6:00 and 28.07 yen/kWh around 18:30 in Tokyo, and 9.85 yen/kWh around 12:30 and 27.02 yen/kWh around 18:30 in Kyushu.

Source: JEPX spot market. Our compilation.

② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | How much is the spread per kWh

For each area, we took the average price of the day's highest 4 hours minus the average price of the day's lowest 4 hours, and show the monthly average. This equals the difference per kWh if you buy 1 kWh in the cheapest 4 hours and sell it in the most expensive 4 hours. It is a simple difference: it does not deduct electricity lost in charging and discharging or fees, and it ignores the order of the cheap and expensive hours.

The monthly average spread between the highest 4 hours and the lowest 4 hours of the wholesale price was largest in Shikoku at 16.78 yen/kWh and smallest in Tokyo at 13.17 yen/kWh.

③ Capacity market (OCCTO) | A market that buys future supply capacity

The capacity market buys electricity supply capacity 4 years ahead through an annual auction. Prices are set in yen per kW per year. The left figure shows the clearing prices of past main auctions. The right figure shows the battery and pumped hydro capacity awarded in the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction (a separate auction that promises capacity revenue, in principle for 20 years, for new decarbonized power sources and similar). These are published once a year, so they do not change every month. The latest is delivery fiscal year FY2029 (published January 2026).

The capacity market clearing price for Tokyo was 14,137 yen/kW for delivery FY2024 and 3,495 yen/kW for FY2025. The latest publication, which comes once a year and does not change monthly, is 15,111 yen/kW for FY2029. In the latest publication of the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction (FY2025 round), also once a year, the awarded battery and pumped hydro capacity was 819 MW for lithium-ion batteries and pumped hydro (excluding new build), and 886 MW for non-lithium-ion batteries, pumped hydro (new build) and LDES.

Source: Clearing results and capacity contract documents published by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO) (sources listed in the last section (Assumptions and terms)). As published.

Our records | How many months and how much it costs to connect to the grid

A grid connection study is a procedure in which you ask the grid operator to check "can we connect at this site, and how much will the work cost". The grid connection cost contribution is the part of the connection work cost that the applicant pays. From the response documents we received, we compiled only the high-voltage ones (connecting to 6,600 V distribution lines) by grid area. Extra-high voltage is not included. Grid areas with fewer than 5 cases are not shown.

The medians of grid connection studies (high voltage only, as of October 19, 2026) range from 8.0 months (Tokyo) to 17.0 months (Tohoku) for the construction period, and from ¥3.02 million (Tokyo) to ¥32.35 million (Tohoku) for the grid connection cost contribution.

Source: Our compilation of the high-voltage grid connection study response documents we received from general transmission and distribution operators (as of October 19, 2026).

Assumptions and terms

  • The figures are our compilation of the published values from the sources. We do not add up several months or convert to annual values. We do not show revenue amounts for any single battery storage plant.
  • Period covered: for the balancing market (EPRX), preliminary values for July 2026 (delivery dates up to the 31st); for wholesale electricity (JEPX), July 2026 (31 days). The month for the balancing market differs until the published values for July 2026 are included in our compilation.
  • Balancing market: nationwide (total column) of EPRX preliminary values. Procured volume is by grid operator (TSO); bid volume, winning volume and average winning price are by area where the power source is located. Volumes are averages per 30-minute slot. The average winning price is weighted by winning volume. Bid-to-cover ratio = bid volume ÷ procured volume; win rate = winning volume ÷ bid volume. The 3-hour blocks up to March 13, 2026 were counted as 6 30-minute slots per block.
  • Range of winning prices: the thin line runs from the lowest bid (minimum winning price) to the highest bid (maximum winning price) among the month's 30-minute slots with winning bids (published values by area where the power source is located). The published values for Primary reserve (online), Secondary reserve 1 and 2, and Tertiary reserve 1 also include Composite product bids (one price for several products combined). So we removed from the thin line any bids above the price cap of 15 yen for Primary reserve (online) and Secondary reserve 1 (for deliveries up to August 31, 2026), and above 10 yen for Secondary reserve 2 and Tertiary reserve 1, where a Composite product bid in the same slot and area can explain them. For Primary reserve (offline), we removed from the thin line bids above the price cap (15 yen this month) (the balancing market system also clears bids above the cap and lowers them to the cap at settlement). The thick band is the range from the bottom 10% to 90% when the average winning prices of the slots with winning bids are sorted from lowest to highest. The dot is the average weighted by winning volume.
  • Win rate by grid area × time of day: total winning volume ÷ total bid volume for slots at the same time of day in the month (by area where the power source is located).
  • The response time and duration of the balancing market products are the FY2026 specifications (Electric Power Reserve Exchange, "Product requirements", 6th edition). Primary reserve senses changes in frequency by itself and responds. Online is the category where the grid operator monitors operation over a dedicated line; offline is the category where no dedicated line is laid and a record of operation is submitted afterwards. The offline response time is within 30 seconds, and there is no set duration.
  • High-priced 30-minute slots for Tertiary reserve 2: slots with a nationwide average winning price of 10 yen/kW per 30 min or more. The top 1% means the top 1% of slots ranked by sales (average winning price × winning volume) (1% of the number of slots, rounded). The maximum winning price is the published value by area where the power source is located, and is the price of the single highest winning bid. In the balancing market, each winning bid is settled at its own bid price (pay-as-bid (multi-price)), so the average winning price for the same slot and area is lower than this.
  • Average prices are simple averages of 30-minute slots (not weighted by trading volume). Daytime is the slots from 8:00 to 16:00; evening is the slots from 16:00 to 20:00.
  • Spread: the day's 30-minute slots are sorted by price, and the average of the 8 lowest slots is subtracted from the average of the 8 highest slots (4 hours) (4 slots for 2 hours). It does not consider the order of cheap and expensive times, electricity lost in charging and discharging, or fees, taxes and grid usage charges. It does not represent actual revenue.
