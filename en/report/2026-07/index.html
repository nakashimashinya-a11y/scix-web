Battery Storage Market Notes, July 2026 issue

"Grid-scale batteries" (large batteries connected to the grid that store electricity and release it again) earn money by selling the "capacity" to fix surpluses and shortages of electricity, and from the price difference as electricity prices change through the day. These notes use published figures and our own records to show this month's trends in figures and tables. This issue is a back issue, prepared retroactively in November 2026. The grid connection study figures are as of the date this issue was prepared (October 19, 2026).

01

The 3 income sources of a battery storage plant (how to read these notes)

A battery storage plant (grid-scale battery) earns income from 3 markets. ① The balancing market (EPRX) is the market where you sell grid operators the "capacity" to correct surpluses or shortages of electricity. ② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) is the market where you buy electricity when it is cheap, store it, and sell it when it is expensive. ③ The capacity market (OCCTO) is the market where the country buys future electricity supply capacity in advance. These notes present the figures for these 3 markets in this order. The ①②③ at the start of each section title shows which market the section covers.

Source: Rules of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX) and the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO).

02

3 trends this month

We picked 3 trends this month from the figures of the 3 markets.

  • In the balancing market (EPRX), the bid ratio for Primary reserve (online) went above 100% at 106.8% in June 2026. This month it was 119.7%, and the average winning bid price was 4.35 yen/kW per 30 min.
  • In the balancing market (EPRX), 57.3% of Tertiary reserve 2 revenue in July 2026 came from the top 10% of 30-minute slots by revenue.
  • In wholesale electricity (JEPX), the monthly average gap between the 4 most expensive and the 4 cheapest hours in July 2026 was 13.17 yen/kWh in Tokyo and 15.71 yen/kWh in Kyushu.
03

① Balancing market (EPRX) | 7 products, and prices by area

The balancing market is the market where grid operators buy, every 30 minutes, the "capacity" to correct surpluses or shortages of electricity. Products are divided by how fast output must change after an instruction. Primary reserve is the fastest and Tertiary reserve 2 is the slowest. Primary reserve has the online and offline categories: "online", connected to the grid operator by a dedicated line, and "offline", which runs on the simplified dispatch system without a dedicated line. They are tendered and traded separately, so we show them separately. A Composite product is a bid that offers several products together. The price is set in yen per 1 kW of capacity offered per 30 minutes. kW is a unit of power capacity, not to be confused with kWh, which measures the amount of electricity. One 30-minute trading unit is called a "slot". The figure shows the range of winning bid prices by area (by the area where the power source is located). The thin line runs from the cheapest to the most expensive winning bid in the month, the thick band is the price range that covers 8 out of 10 slots with winning bids, and the dot is the average winning bid price. Nationwide figures are in the table in the "Market figures" section.

The highest average winning bid price by area in the balancing market was Primary reserve (offline) in Tohoku at 14.49 yen/kW per 30 min, and the lowest was Tertiary reserve 2 in Shikoku at 0.38 yen/kW per 30 min.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our tally.

① Balancing market (EPRX) | Primary reserve (online) became a contest for places

Primary reserve is the fastest product. Output must change within 10 seconds of an instruction, and batteries are often used for it. The left side is the online category and the right side is the offline category. The bars show the bid ratio (bid volume ÷ volume sought by grid operators). Above 100%, more bids came in than the volume sought, so some bids lose. A bar rises when bids increase and also when the volume sought falls. The line is the average winning bid price. These are the monthly figures we have, from April 2025.

In the balancing market, the bid ratio for Primary reserve (online) went above 100%, at 106.8% in June 2026 and 119.7% this month. Its average winning bid price was 2.89 yen/kW per 30 min in April 2025 and 4.35 yen/kW per 30 min this month. Primary reserve (offline) had a bid ratio of 65.1% and an average winning bid price of 10.25 yen/kW per 30 min this month. From April 2025 to this month, the volume sought fell from 3,066.1 MW to 1,408.5 MW for online and from 1,122.5 MW to 495.7 MW for offline.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our tally.

② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | Average price

Wholesale electricity is the electricity that power companies and retailers buy and sell. Its price is set on the "spot market" of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), where electricity for the next day is traded the day before. "System (nationwide)" is the price when all of Japan is treated as a single market (the system price). When the power lines between regions are congested, the price splits into separate prices for each area from Hokkaido to Kyushu (area prices).

The monthly average System (nationwide) price for wholesale electricity (JEPX) in July 2026 was 17.91 yen/kWh, compared with 12.75 yen/kWh in the same month last year.

Source: Spot market trading results published by the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX). Simple average of 30-minute prices. Our tally.

② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | How the price moves through the day

The wholesale electricity price changes every 30 minutes. The figure shows the shape of a day, averaging the price at the same time of day across the month. If a battery stores electricity when it is cheap and releases it when it is expensive, the difference becomes the battery's income.

The daily low and high for wholesale electricity were 15.28 yen/kWh around 6:00 and 28.07 yen/kWh around 18:30 in Tokyo, and 9.85 yen/kWh around 12:30 and 27.02 yen/kWh around 18:30 in Kyushu.

Source: JEPX spot market. Our tally.

② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | How much is the price gap per kWh

For each area, we took the average price of the day's 4 most expensive hours, subtracted the average price of the 4 cheapest hours, and show the monthly average. This equals the gain per kWh if you buy 1 kWh in the 4 cheapest hours and sell it in the 4 most expensive hours. It is a simple gap: it does not subtract electricity lost in charging and discharging or fees, and it ignores whether the cheap hours come before the expensive hours.

The monthly average gap between the 4 most expensive and the 4 cheapest hours of wholesale electricity was largest in Shikoku at 16.78 yen/kWh and smallest in Tokyo at 13.17 yen/kWh.

③ Capacity market (OCCTO) | The market that buys future supply capacity

The capacity market buys electricity supply capacity 4 years ahead through an auction held once a year. The price is set in yen per kW per year. The left figure shows the clearing prices of past main auctions. The right figure shows the capacity of batteries and pumped hydro contracted in the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction (a separate auction that supports new builds such as batteries and pumped hydro). These are published once a year, so they do not change every month. The latest is delivery FY2029 (published January 2026).

The capacity market clearing price for Tokyo was 14,137 yen/kW for delivery FY2024, 3,495 yen/kW for FY2025, and 15,111 yen/kW for FY2029, the latest publication (published once a year, so it does not change every month). In the latest publication of the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction, also held once a year (FY2025 auction), the contracted capacity of batteries and pumped hydro was 819 MW for lithium-ion and 886 MW for non-lithium.

Source: Clearing results and capacity contract documents published by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO) (links in the last section (Assumptions and terms)). As published.

Our records | How many months and how much it takes to connect to the grid

A grid connection study is the procedure where the grid operator checks "whether this site can connect, and how much the work will cost." The grid connection cost contribution is the part of the connection work cost paid by the applicant. Of the response letters we received, we tallied only the high-voltage ones (connecting to 6,600V distribution lines), by grid area. Extra-high voltage is not included. Grid areas with fewer than 5 cases are not shown.

The medians for grid connection studies (high voltage only, as of October 19, 2026) range from 8.0 months in Tokyo to 17.0 months in Tohoku for the construction period, and from ¥3.02 million in Tokyo to ¥32.35 million in Tohoku for the grid connection cost contribution.

Source: Tally of the high-voltage grid connection study response letters we received from general transmission and distribution operators (as of October 19, 2026).

Assumptions and terms

  • The figures are our tallies of the values published by the sources. We have not added up several months or converted figures to annual amounts. We do not show the revenue of any single battery storage plant.
  • Months covered: the balancing market (EPRX) is the July 2026 preliminary figures (delivery dates up to the 31st); wholesale electricity (JEPX) is July 2026 (31 days). For the balancing market, the month differs until the published figures for July 2026 are included in the tally.
  • Balancing market: nationwide EPRX preliminary figures (the total column). The volume sought is by grid operator (TSO), and the bid volume, winning volume and average winning bid price are by the area where the power source is located. Volumes are averages per 30-minute slot. The average winning bid price is weighted by winning volume. Bid ratio = bid volume ÷ volume sought; win rate = winning volume ÷ bid volume. The 3-hour blocks up to March 13, 2026 were counted as 6 30-minute slots per block.
  • Range of winning bid prices: the thin line runs from the cheapest bid (minimum winning bid price) to the most expensive bid (maximum winning bid price) among the month's slots with winning bids (published values by area where the power source is located). The published values for Primary reserve (online), Secondary reserve 1 and 2 and Tertiary reserve 1 also include Composite product bids (one price for several products together). So bids above the 15 yen price cap for Primary reserve (online) and Secondary reserve 1 (for delivery up to August 31, 2026), and above 10 yen for Secondary reserve 2 and Tertiary reserve 1, that can be explained by a Composite product bid in the same slot and area, were removed from the thin line. For Primary reserve (offline), bids above the price cap (15 yen this month) were removed from the thin line (the balancing market system also clears bids above the cap, and cuts them down to the cap at settlement). The thick band is the range from the bottom 10% to 90% when the average winning bid prices of slots with winning bids are sorted from lowest to highest. The dot is the average weighted by winning volume.
  • Win rate by grid area × time of day: total winning volume ÷ total bid volume for slots at the same time of day in the month (by area where the power source is located).
  • The time from instruction to output change and the duration for each balancing market product are the FY2026 specifications. Primary reserve online is the category connected to the grid operator by a dedicated line, and offline is the category that runs on the simplified dispatch system without a dedicated line.
  • High-price slots for Tertiary reserve 2: slots with a nationwide average winning bid price of 10 yen/kW per 30 min or more. The top 1% is the top 1% of slots ranked by revenue (average winning bid price × winning volume). The maximum winning bid price is the published value by area where the power source is located.
  • Average prices are simple averages of 30-minute slots (not weighted by trading volume). Daytime is the slots from 8:00 to 16:00, and evening is the slots from 16:00 to 20:00.
  • Price gap: the day's 30-minute slots are sorted by price, and the average of the 8 cheapest slots is subtracted from the average of the 8 most expensive slots (4 hours) (4 slots for 2 hours). It does not consider the order of cheap and expensive hours, electricity lost in charging and discharging, or fees, taxes and transmission charges. It does not show actual revenue.
