Battery Storage Market Notes, November 2026 issue

Grid-scale batteries are large batteries connected to the power grid that store electricity and release it. They earn money by selling the ability to fix surpluses and shortages of electricity, and by using the price differences between times of day. These notes use published figures and our own records to show the market in October 2026, in figures and tables. From this issue, each issue is named after the month it is published. This issue covers market figures for October 2026. Together with the previous issue (the October 2026 issue), it covers the October market; in the previous issue, only the balancing market used September figures. This issue uses the latest data published on the day we made it. Interim figures for November 2026 are in Section 3. Section 4 shows each source and the last day its data covers.

01

Three revenue streams for battery storage sites (how to read these notes)

A battery storage site (grid-scale battery) earns income from three markets. ① In the balancing market (EPRX), the ability to fix electricity surpluses and shortages is sold to grid operators. ② In wholesale electricity (JEPX), you buy and store electricity when it is cheap and sell it when it is expensive. ③ In the capacity market (OCCTO), the country buys future electricity supply capacity in advance. These notes show the figures in this order. The ①②③ at the start of a section title shows which market the section covers.

Source: rules of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX) and the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO).

05

① Balancing market (EPRX) | Seven products and prices by area

The balancing market is where grid operators buy, every 30 minutes, the ability to fix electricity surpluses and shortages. Products are split by how fast output must change. Primary reserve responds to changes in frequency; Secondary and Tertiary reserves respond to instructions from the grid operator. Primary reserve is the fastest, and Tertiary reserve 2 is the slowest. Primary reserve senses frequency changes by itself and responds. It has two categories, and we show them separately because each is sought and traded on its own. In "online", the grid operator watches operation over a dedicated line. In "offline", no dedicated line is installed and a record of operation is submitted afterwards. A Composite product is a bid that bundles several products. Prices are set in yen per 1 kW of capacity per 30 minutes. kW measures how much power can be delivered; it is different from kWh, which measures an amount of electricity. Each 30-minute trading round is called a "30-minute slot". The figure shows the range of winning prices by area (the area where the power source is). The thin line runs from the cheapest to the most expensive winning bid in the month. The thick band is the price range that holds 8 out of 10 slots with winning bids. The dot is the average winning price. Nationwide figures are in the table in the "Market figures" section.

Across all products and areas of the balancing market, the highest average winning price in October 2026 was Tertiary reserve 2 in Tohoku at 13.64 yen/kW per 30 min, and the lowest was Tertiary reserve 2 in Shikoku at 0.38 yen/kW per 30 min.

Source: trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Compiled by us.

② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | Average prices

Wholesale electricity is electricity that power companies and retailers buy and sell. Its price is set on the "spot market" of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), where electricity for the next day is traded the day before. "System (nationwide)" is the price when all of Japan is treated as one market (the system price). When the transmission lines between regions are congested, prices split by area from Hokkaido to Kyushu (area prices).

The monthly average wholesale (JEPX) price for System (nationwide) was 22.51 yen/kWh in October 2026 and 11.67 yen/kWh in the same month last year (a difference of 10.84 yen).

Source: spot market trading results published by the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX). Simple average of 30-minute prices. Compiled by us.

② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | How big the price gap is per kWh

For each area, we took the average price of the day's 4 most expensive hours minus the average price of its 4 cheapest hours, and show the monthly average. This equals the gain per kWh from buying 1 kWh in the 4 cheapest hours and selling it in the 4 most expensive hours. It is a simple gap. It does not subtract electricity lost in charging and discharging or fees, and it ignores whether the cheap hours come before the expensive ones.

In October 2026, the wholesale gap between the 4 most expensive and 4 cheapest hours (monthly average) was largest in Hokkaido at 29.02 yen/kWh and smallest in Tokyo at 17.14 yen/kWh.

③ Capacity market (OCCTO) | A market that buys future supply capacity

The capacity market buys electricity supply capacity for 4 years ahead through an auction held once a year. The price is set in yen per kW per year. The left figure shows past main auction clearing prices. The right figure shows the battery and pumped hydro capacity contracted in the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction. This is a separate auction that promises, in principle, 20 years of capacity income for new decarbonized power sources and similar projects. Results are published once a year, so they do not change every month. The latest main auction is for delivery FY2029 (published January 2026), and the latest additional auction is for delivery FY2027 (published August 2026). An additional auction buys, in the year before delivery, the supply capacity the main auction did not secure. Its clearing prices are in the "Market figures" table.

In the capacity market (latest annual results), main auction clearing prices for Tokyo for delivery FY2024 to FY2029 were 14,137 yen → 3,495 yen → 5,834 yen → 9,555 yen → 14,812 yen → 15,111 yen/kW. In the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction, bids in FY2025 won 819 MW for lithium-ion batteries and pumped hydro (excluding new builds) and 886 MW for non-lithium-ion batteries, pumped hydro (new builds) and LDES.

Source: auction results and capacity contract documents published by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO) (sources are listed in the "Assumptions and terms" section). As published.

From the replies we received, we compiled only high-voltage cases (connecting to 6,600 V distribution lines), by grid area. Extra-high voltage cases are not included. Assumptions and terms

  • We compiled the figures from values published by the sources. We have not added up several months or converted figures to annual amounts. We do not show revenue amounts for any single battery storage site.
  • Months covered: the balancing market (EPRX) uses preliminary figures for October 2026 (delivery days up to the 31st), and wholesale electricity (JEPX) uses October 2026 (31 days). In the previous issue (October 2026 issue), balancing market figures for September 2026 were preliminary (delivery days up to the 29th). This issue uses final figures, so they differ slightly.
  • The section on interim figures for November 2026 is preliminary and covers part of the month, not the full month. The balancing market covers delivery days up to November 23, and wholesale electricity covers delivery days up to November 24. Figures will change when the month ends.
  • Balancing market: nationwide values (total column) of EPRX preliminary figures. Volume sought is by grid operator (TSO); volume bid, volume won and average winning price are by area of the power source. Volumes are averages per 30-minute slot. The average winning price is weighted by volume won. Bid ratio = volume bid ÷ volume sought; award rate = volume won ÷ volume bid. For the period of 3-hour blocks (until March 13, 2025 for Tertiary reserve 2, and until March 13, 2026 for the other products), we counted 1 block as 6 slots of 30 minutes.
  • Final figures: values EPRX publishes after the month ends (auction results plus the results of extra procurement after the auction). Monthly trends (Figure 4, Figure 5, etc.) use final figures for months where they are published and preliminary figures for months where they are not yet published (final for April 2024 to September 2026, preliminary for October 2026). Volume bid, volume won and average winning price can differ slightly from preliminary figures, so a month's figures change when final figures come out.
  • Range of winning prices: the thin line runs from the cheapest winning bid (minimum winning price) to the most expensive winning bid (maximum winning price) in the month's slots with winning bids (published values by area of the power source). The published values for Primary reserve (online), Secondary reserve 1 and 2, and Tertiary reserve 1 also include Composite product bids (a single price for several bundled products). So we removed from the thin line any bids above 10 yen that a Composite product bid in the same slot and area can explain. For Primary reserve (offline), we removed from the thin line any bids above the price cap (10 yen this month). The balancing market system lets bids above the cap win and lowers them to the cap at settlement. The thick band is the 10% to 90% range of the average winning prices of slots with winning bids, sorted from lowest. The dot is the average weighted by volume won.
  • Award rate by grid area × time of day: total volume won ÷ total volume bid for the slots at the same time across the month (by area of the power source).
  • The response time and duration of balancing market products follow the FY2026 specifications (Electric Power Reserve Exchange, "Product requirements", 6th edition). Primary reserve senses frequency changes by itself and responds. Online is the category where the grid operator watches operation over a dedicated line. Offline is the category where no dedicated line is installed and a record of operation is submitted afterwards. For offline, the response time is within 30 seconds and there is no set duration.
  • High-priced slots for Tertiary reserve 2: slots with a nationwide average winning price of 10 yen/kW per 30 min or more. The top 1% means the top 1% of slots ranked by revenue (average winning price × volume won), with 1% of the number of slots rounded to the nearest whole number. The maximum winning price is the published value by area of the power source: the price of the single highest winning bid. In the balancing market, each winning bid is settled at its own bid price (pay-as-bid (multi-price)), so the average winning price for the same slot and area is lower than this.
  • Average prices are simple averages of 30-minute slots (not weighted by trading volume). Daytime means slots from 8:00 to 16:00; evening means slots from 16:00 to 20:00.
  • Price gap: sort the day's 30-minute slots by price, then subtract the average of the 8 cheapest slots from the average of the 8 most expensive slots (4 hours) (4 slots for 2 hours). It does not consider the order of cheap and expensive times, electricity lost in charging and discharging, or fees, taxes and grid usage charges. It does not show actual revenue.
