Battery Storage Market Notes, November 2026 issue

"Grid-scale batteries" are large batteries connected to the power grid that store electricity and release it. They earn money by selling the "capability" to fix surpluses and shortages of electricity. They also use the difference in electricity prices across the day. These notes use published figures and our records to show, in figures and tables, how the market moved in October 2026. From this issue, each issue is named after the month it is published. This issue covers market figures for October 2026 (together with the previous issue, the October 2026 issue, it covers the October market; in the previous issue, only the balancing market used September figures). This issue is built from the latest data published on the day we prepared it. Section 3 shows November 2026 so far, and section 4 shows which source each figure comes from and up to which day.

01

Three income sources for a battery storage site (how to read these notes)

A battery storage site (grid-scale battery) earns income from three markets. ① The balancing market (EPRX) is where the "capability" to fix surpluses and shortages of electricity is sold to grid operators. ② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) is the market where electricity is bought and stored when it is cheap and sold when it is expensive. ③ The capacity market (OCCTO) is where the country buys future electricity supply capacity in advance. These notes present the figures in the order of these three markets. The ①②③ at the start of each section title shows which market it covers.

Source: Rules of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX) and the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO).

① Balancing market (EPRX) | Seven products and prices by area

The balancing market is where grid operators buy, every 30 minutes, the "capability" to fix surpluses and shortages of electricity. Products are divided by how fast output can be changed (Primary reserve acts on frequency changes; Secondary and Tertiary reserves act on instructions from the grid operator). Primary reserve is the fastest, and Tertiary reserve 2 is the slowest. Primary reserve is a product that senses frequency changes by itself and acts. It has two categories, "online" and "offline." In online, the grid operator watches the response over a dedicated line. In offline, no dedicated line is laid, and a record of the response is submitted afterwards. The two are recruited and traded separately, so we show them separately. A Composite product is a bid that offers several products together. Prices are set in yen per 1 kW of capability per 30 min. kW is a unit for how much power can be delivered; it is different from kWh, which measures an amount of electricity. One round of the 30-minute trading is called a "30-minute slot." The figure shows the range of winning prices by area (by the area where the power source is). The thin line runs from the cheapest bid to the most expensive bid in the month. The thick band is the price range that holds 8 out of 10 slots with winning bids. The dot is the average winning price. Nationwide figures are in the table in the "Market figures" section.

Across all products and all areas, the highest average winning price by area in the balancing market (yen/kW per 30 min) was 13.64 yen for Tertiary reserve 2 in Tohoku, and the lowest was 0.38 yen for Tertiary reserve 2 in Shikoku.

Source: Trading results (preliminary figures) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Compiled by us.

Primary reserve is the fastest product. It acts on frequency changes and changes output within 10 seconds (online) or within 30 seconds (offline). Batteries are often used for it. The left side shows the online category and the right side shows the offline category. The bars are the bid ratio (volume bid ÷ volume grid operators asked for). ② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | Average price

Wholesale electricity is the electricity that power companies and retail electricity companies buy and sell. Its price is set on the "spot market" of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), where electricity for the next day is traded the day before. "System (nationwide)" is the price when all of Japan is treated as one market (the system price). When transmission lines between regions are congested, prices split by area, from Hokkaido to Kyushu (area prices).

The monthly average wholesale electricity (JEPX) price for System (nationwide) was 22.51 yen/kWh in October 2026, against 11.67 yen/kWh in the same month last year.

Source: Spot market trading results published by the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX). Simple average of 30-minute prices. Compiled by us.

③ Capacity market (OCCTO) | A market that buys future supply capacity

The capacity market buys electricity supply capacity 4 years ahead through an auction held once a year. The price is set in yen per kW per year. The left figure shows the clearing prices of past main auctions. The right figure shows the battery and pumped hydro capacity cleared in the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction (a separate auction that promises capacity income, in principle for 20 years, for things such as new decarbonized power sources). These are published once a year, so they do not change every month. The latest main auction is for delivery FY2029 (published January 2026), and the latest additional auction is for delivery FY2027 (published August 2026). The clearing prices of the additional auction (an auction that buys, in the year before delivery, the supply capacity the main auction did not secure) are in the "Market figures" table.

The capacity market main auction clearing price for Tokyo from delivery FY2024 to FY2029 was 14,137 yen → 3,495 yen → 5,834 yen → 9,555 yen → 14,812 yen → 15,111 yen (yen/kW). In the capacity market's Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction, the battery and pumped hydro capacity cleared in the FY2025 round was 819 MW for lithium-ion batteries and pumped hydro (excluding new builds), and 886 MW for non-lithium-ion batteries, pumped hydro (new builds) and LDES. Both are once-a-year values, so they are the latest published figures and do not change every month.

Source: Results and capacity securing contract materials published by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO) (sources are listed in the "Assumptions and terms" section). As published.

Our records | How many months and how much it takes to connect to the grid

A grid connection study is a procedure where the grid operator checks "whether you can connect at this location, and how much the work will cost." The grid connection cost contribution is the part of the connection work cost that the applicant pays. Of the response documents we received, we tallied only high-voltage cases (connecting to 6,600 V distribution lines), by grid area. Extra-high voltage is not included. Grid areas with fewer than 5 cases are not shown.

Assumptions and terms

  • Figures are our tallies of the published values from the sources. We have not added up several months or converted figures to yearly values. We do not show the revenue amount for any single battery storage site.
  • Months covered: the balancing market (EPRX) uses preliminary figures for October 2026 (delivery days up to the 31st), and wholesale electricity (JEPX) uses October 2026 (31 days). The September 2026 balancing market figures were preliminary figures in the previous issue (the October 2026 issue), covering delivery days up to the 29th. This issue uses final figures, which differ slightly.
  • The November 2026 section shows preliminary figures from partway through the month, not figures for the whole month. The balancing market covers delivery days up to November 23, and wholesale electricity covers delivery days up to November 24. Figures will change once the month ends.
  • Balancing market: nationwide preliminary figures from EPRX (the total column). Requested volume is by grid operator (TSO); bid volume, winning volume and average winning price are by area where the power source is. Volumes are averages per 30-minute slot. The average winning price is weighted by winning volume. Bid ratio = bid volume ÷ requested volume; clearing rate = winning volume ÷ bid volume. For the period of 3-hour blocks (until March 13, 2025 for Tertiary reserve 2, and until March 13, 2026 for other products), we counted each block as six 30-minute slots.
  • Final figures: the values EPRX publishes after the month ends (totals that reflect additional procurement and corrections made after clearing; the "Total (final figures)" row in the published file). Monthly trends (Figure 4, Figure 5 and others) use final figures for months where they have been published, and preliminary figures for months not yet published (final figures for April 2024 to September 2026, preliminary figures for October 2026). Bid volume, winning volume and average winning price may differ slightly from the preliminary figures, so when final figures come out, that month's figures change.
  • Range of winning prices: the thin line runs from the cheapest bid (lowest winning price) to the most expensive bid (highest winning price) among the month's slots with winning bids (published values by area where the power source is). The published values for Primary reserve (online), Secondary reserve 1 and 2, and Tertiary reserve 1 also include Composite product bids (one price for several products together). So bids above 10 yen that can be explained by a Composite product bid in the same slot and area were removed from the thin line. For Primary reserve (offline), bids above the price cap (10 yen this month) were removed from the thin line (the balancing market system also clears bids above the cap, and cuts them down to the cap at settlement). The thick band is the 10% to 90% range when the average winning prices of slots with winning bids are sorted from cheapest. The dot is the average weighted by winning volume.
  • Clearing rate by grid area × time of day: total winning volume ÷ total bid volume for slots at the same time across the month (by area where the power source is).
  • Response time and duration for balancing market products follow the FY2026 specifications (Electric Power Reserve Exchange "Product requirements," 6th edition). Primary reserve senses frequency changes by itself and acts. Online is the category where the grid operator watches the response over a dedicated line. Offline is the category where no dedicated line is laid, and a record of the response is submitted afterwards. The response time for offline is within 30 seconds, and it has no set duration.
  • High-priced slots for Tertiary reserve 2: slots with a nationwide average winning price of 10 yen/kW per 30 min or more. The top 1% is the top 1% of slots sorted by revenue (average winning price × winning volume) (1% of the number of slots, rounded). The top winning price is the published value by area where the power source is, and is the price of the single highest winning bid. In the balancing market, each winning bid is settled at its own bid price (pay-as-bid (multi-price)), so the average winning price for the same slot and area is lower than this.
  • Average prices are simple averages of 30-minute slots (not weighted by trading volume). Daytime covers slots from 8:00 to 16:00, and evening covers slots from 16:00 to 20:00.
  • Price gap: we sort the day's 30-minute slots by price and subtract the average of the 8 cheapest slots from the average of the 8 most expensive slots (4 hours) (4 slots for 2 hours). We do not consider the order of the cheap and expensive hours, electricity lost in charging and discharging, or fees, taxes and transmission charges. This does not show actual revenue.
