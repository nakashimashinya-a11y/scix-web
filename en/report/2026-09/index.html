Battery Storage Market Notes, September 2026 issue

The price of electricity changes with the time of day. A "grid-scale battery" (a large battery connected to the grid that stores electricity and releases it again) earns money from that difference. These notes use published figures and our own records to show this month's trends in figures and tables. This issue is a back issue, prepared retroactively in November 2026. The grid connection study tally is as of the time it was prepared (October 19, 2026).

01

Three revenue sources for a battery storage plant (how to read these notes)

A battery storage plant (grid-scale battery) earns revenue from three markets. ① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) is the market where you buy electricity when it is cheap, store it, and sell it when it is expensive. ② The balancing market (EPRX) is where you sell grid operators the "capacity" to fix surpluses and shortages of electricity. ③ The capacity market (OCCTO) is where the country buys future electricity supply capacity in advance. These notes present the figures in the order of these three markets. The ①②③ at the start of each section title shows which market the section covers.

Source: Market rules of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX) and the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO).

02

Three trends this month

From the figures for the three markets, we picked three trends for this month.

  • In wholesale electricity (JEPX), the monthly average spread between the 4 most expensive hours and the 4 cheapest hours of the day was 13.42 yen/kWh in Tokyo and 15.17 yen/kWh in Kyushu.
  • In the balancing market (EPRX), the bid ratio for Primary reserve (online) rose above 100% to 106.8% in June 2026, and was 123.1% this month, with an average awarded price of 3.43 yen/kW per 30 min.
  • In the balancing market (EPRX), the top 10% of 30-minute slots by revenue for Tertiary reserve 2 accounted for 56.9% of the month's revenue.
03

① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | Average price

Wholesale electricity is electricity bought and sold between power companies and retailers. Its price is set on the "spot market" of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX) (a market where electricity for the next day is traded the day before). "System (nationwide)" is the price when all of Japan is treated as one market (the system price). When the transmission lines between regions are congested, prices split by area from Hokkaido to Kyushu (area prices).

The monthly average for wholesale electricity (JEPX) was 19.12 yen/kWh this month for the System (nationwide), against 11.62 yen/kWh in the same month last year, and 23.78 yen/kWh this month in Tokyo, against 12.92 yen/kWh in the same month last year.

Source: Spot market trading results published by the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX). Simple average of 30-minute prices. Our tally.

04

① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | How the price moves through the day

The wholesale electricity price changes every 30 minutes. The figure shows the daily shape, averaging the price at the same time of day across the month. If you store electricity when it is cheap and release it when it is expensive, the difference becomes the battery's revenue.

The trough and peak of the average wholesale electricity day were 19.23 yen around 1:00 and 30.77 yen around 16:30 in Tokyo, and 9.61 yen around 12:00 and 23.75 yen around 17:30 in Kyushu.

Source: JEPX spot market. Our tally.

05

① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | How much is the spread per kWh

We took the average price of the day's 4 most expensive hours, subtracted the average price of its 4 cheapest hours, and show the monthly average of this difference by area. It equals the difference per kWh if you bought 1 kWh in the 4 cheapest hours and sold it in the 4 most expensive hours. It is a simple difference: it does not deduct electricity lost in charging and discharging or fees, and it ignores whether the cheap hours come before the expensive ones.

The monthly average spread between the 4 most expensive and 4 cheapest hours of wholesale electricity was largest in Tohoku at 19.11 yen/kWh and smallest in Tokyo at 13.42 yen/kWh.

Source: JEPX spot market. Our tally.

06

① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | There is a spread every day

We show the same 4-hour spread day by day. The larger the spread on a given day, the more room a battery has to earn from it.

Out of 30 days, the 4-hour wholesale electricity spread was 10 yen or more on 28 days for the System (nationwide), 27 days in Tohoku, and 19 days each in Hokuriku, Kansai and Kyushu.

Source: JEPX spot market. Our tally.

07

② Balancing market (EPRX) | Seven products, and prices by area

The balancing market is where grid operators buy, every 30 minutes, the "capacity" to fix surpluses and shortages of electricity. Products are split by how fast output must change after receiving an instruction. Primary reserve is the fastest and Tertiary reserve 2 is the slowest. Primary reserve has two categories, "online", connected to the grid operator by a dedicated line, and "offline", which runs on the simplified dispatch system without a dedicated line; they are procured and traded separately, so we show them separately. A Composite product is a bid that offers several products together. The price is set in yen per 1 kW of available capacity per 30 minutes. The figure shows the range of awarded prices by area (by area where the resource is located). The thin line runs from the month's cheapest to most expensive bid, the thick band is the price range covering 8 out of 10 of the 30-minute slots with awards, and the dot is the average awarded price. Nationwide figures are in the tables of the "Market figures" section.

Among the average awarded prices by area in the balancing market, the highest was Primary reserve (offline) in Tohoku at 9.51 yen/kW per 30 min, and the lowest was Tertiary reserve 2 in Shikoku at 0.38 yen/kW per 30 min.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our tally.

② Balancing market (EPRX) | Grid areas and times where bids win more easily

The award rate is the share of the bid volume that was awarded; the higher it is, the more easily bids win. The figure plots, for each grid area (area where the resource is located), the award rate of each product by time of day (every 30 minutes). Where a line breaks, there were no bids at that time.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our tally.

09

② Balancing market (EPRX) | Primary reserve (online) became a competition for places

Primary reserve is the fastest product, changing output within 10 seconds of an instruction, and batteries are often used for it. The left side is the online category and the right side the offline category. The bars show the bid ratio (bid volume ÷ volume procured by grid operators); above 100%, more bids came in than were procured, and some bids lose. The bars rise either when bids increase or when the procured volume falls. The line is the average awarded price. These are the monthly figures we have, from April 2025.

The bid ratio for Primary reserve (online) in the balancing market rose above 100% to 106.8% in June 2026, and was 123.1% this month, with an average awarded price of 3.43 yen/kW per 30 min; the procured volume was 3,066.1 MW in April 2025 and 1,388.7 MW this month. For Primary reserve (offline), this month's bid ratio was 84.0% and the average awarded price 5.79 yen/kW per 30 min; the procured volume was 1,122.5 MW in April 2025 and 497.8 MW this month.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our tally.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our tally.

② Balancing market (EPRX) | Revenue is concentrated in a small number of slots

We ranked the month's 30-minute slots with awards by revenue (average awarded price × awarded volume, nationwide), from highest to lowest, and plotted for each product what share of the month's revenue the top slots account for. The further a curve bulges above the dotted line, the more revenue is concentrated in a few slots. Tertiary reserve 2 is a product that covers forecast errors in weather-dependent renewable generation; its price is low in most slots and high in a few.

In the balancing market, the top 10% of slots by revenue accounted for 56.9% of the month's revenue for Tertiary reserve 2 and 15.8% for Primary reserve (online); for the top 5%, the figures were 36.9% and 8.6%.

14

③ Capacity market (OCCTO) | The market that buys future supply capacity

The capacity market buys electricity supply capacity 4 years ahead through an annual auction, with prices set in yen per kW per year. The left figure shows the clearing prices of past main auctions. The right figure shows the battery and pumped hydro capacity contracted in the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction (a separate auction that supports new builds such as batteries and pumped hydro). These are published once a year, so they do not change every month. The latest is delivery FY2029 (published January 2026).

The capacity market clearing price for Tokyo was 14,137 yen/kW for delivery FY2024, 3,495 yen/kW for FY2025 and 15,111 yen/kW for FY2029. In the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction, batteries and pumped hydro bid in FY2025 came to 819 MW of lithium-ion and 886 MW of non-lithium; both are annual values that do not change monthly, and are the latest published figures.

Source: Clearing results and capacity assurance contract documents published by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO) (sources listed in the last section (Assumptions and terms)). As published.

Our records | How many months and how much it takes to connect to the grid

A grid connection study is the procedure where the grid operator checks "can this site connect, and how much will the work cost?" The grid connection cost contribution is the part of the connection work cost paid by the applicant. Of the replies we received, we tallied only high-voltage cases (connections to 6,600 V distribution lines), by grid area. Extra-high voltage is not included. Grid areas with fewer than 5 cases are not shown.

The medians in grid connection study replies (tally of high voltage only, as of October 19, 2026) range from 8.0 months in Tokyo to 17.0 months in Tohoku for construction time, and from ¥3.02 million in Tokyo to ¥32.35 million in Tohoku for the grid connection cost contribution.

Source: Tally of the high-voltage grid connection study replies we received from general transmission and distribution operators (as of October 19, 2026).

Assumptions and terms

  • The figures are our tally of the values published by the sources. We have not summed several months or converted to annual figures. We do not show revenue amounts for any single battery storage plant.
  • Months covered: wholesale electricity (JEPX) is September 2026 (30 days); the balancing market (EPRX) is preliminary figures for September 2026 (delivery dates up to the 29th). For the balancing market, the month differs until the published figures for September 2026 are included in the tally.
  • Average prices are simple averages of 30-minute slots (not weighted by trading volume). Daytime is the slots from 8 to 16h, evening from 16 to 20h.
  • Spread: the day's 30-minute slots are ranked by price, and the average of the 8 cheapest slots is subtracted from the average of the 8 most expensive slots (4 hours) (4 slots for 2 hours). We do not consider whether the cheap hours come before the expensive ones, electricity lost in charging and discharging, or fees, taxes and wheeling charges. This does not show actual revenue.
  • Balancing market: nationwide (total column) of EPRX preliminary figures. Procured volume is by grid operator (TSO); bid volume, awarded volume and average awarded price are by area where the resource is located. Volumes are averages per 30-minute slot. The average awarded price is weighted by awarded volume. Bid ratio = bid volume ÷ procured volume; award rate = awarded volume ÷ bid volume. For 3-hour blocks up to March 13, 2026, we counted 1 block as 6 slots of 30 minutes.
  • Range of awarded prices: the thin line runs from the cheapest bid (minimum awarded price) to the most expensive bid (maximum awarded price) among the month's slots with awards (published values by area where the resource is located). The published values for Primary reserve (online), Secondary reserve 1 and 2, and Tertiary reserve 1 also include Composite product bids (one price covering several products), so bids above 10 yen that can be explained by a Composite product bid in the same slot and area were removed from the thin line. For Primary reserve (offline), bids above the price cap (10 yen this month) were removed from the thin line (the balancing market system also awards bids above the cap and cuts them down to the cap at settlement). The thick band is the 10% to 90% range when the average awarded prices of slots with awards are ranked from lowest. The dot is the average weighted by awarded volume.
  • Award rate by grid area × time of day: total awarded volume ÷ total bid volume for slots at the same time of day across the month (by area where the resource is located).
  • The time from instruction to output change and the duration for balancing market products are the FY2026 specifications. For Primary reserve, online is the category connected to the grid operator by a dedicated line, and offline is the category that runs on the simplified dispatch system without a dedicated line.
  • High-price slots for Tertiary reserve 2: slots with a nationwide average awarded price of 10 yen/kW per 30 min or more. The top 1% is the top 1% of slots when ranked by revenue (average awarded price × awarded volume). The maximum awarded price is the published value by area where the resource is located.
