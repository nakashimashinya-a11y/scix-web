Battery Storage Market Notes, September 2026 issue

A grid-scale battery is a large battery connected to the transmission grid that stores electricity and releases it. It earns money by selling the "capacity" to fix surpluses and shortages of electricity, and by using price differences between times of day. These notes use published figures and our own records to show this month's trends in figures and tables. This issue is a back issue, prepared retroactively in November 2026. The grid connection study figures are as of the time of preparation (October 19, 2026).

01

Three revenue streams for a battery storage plant (how to read these notes)

A battery storage plant (grid-scale battery) earns revenue from three markets. ① In the balancing market (EPRX), it sells grid operators the "capacity" to fix surpluses and shortages of electricity. ② In wholesale electricity (JEPX), it buys and stores electricity at cheap times and sells it at expensive times. ③ In the capacity market (OCCTO), the country buys future electricity supply capacity in advance. These notes list the figures for these three markets in that order. The ①, ② or ③ at the start of each section title shows which market it covers.

Source: Rules of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX) and the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO).

Three trends this month

We picked three of this month's trends from the figures of the three markets.

  • In the balancing market (EPRX), the bid-to-requirement ratio for Primary reserve (online) passed 100% in June 2026 at 106.8%. This month it was 123.1%, and the average winning price was 3.43 yen/kW per 30 min.
  • In the balancing market (EPRX), the top 10% of 30-minute slots by revenue for Tertiary reserve 2 made up 56.9% of the month's revenue.
  • In wholesale electricity (JEPX), the monthly average spread between the 4 most expensive hours and the 4 cheapest hours of the day was 13.42 yen/kWh in Tokyo and 15.17 yen/kWh in Kyushu.
03

① Balancing market (EPRX) | 7 products, and prices by area

The balancing market is where grid operators buy, every 30 minutes, the "capacity" to fix surpluses and shortages of electricity. Products are split by how fast they change output (Primary reserve responds to changes in frequency; Secondary and Tertiary reserves respond to instructions from grid operators). Primary reserve is the fastest, and Tertiary reserve 2 is the slowest. Primary reserve senses frequency changes on its own and responds. It has two categories. In "online", the grid operator monitors operation over a dedicated line. In "offline", no dedicated line is installed, and a record of the operation is submitted afterwards. The two are procured and traded separately, so we show them separately. A Composite product is a bid that combines several products. Prices are set in yen per 1 kW of available capacity per 30 minutes. kW is the unit for how much power can be delivered. It is different from kWh, which measures an amount of electricity. Each 30-minute trading round is called a "30-minute slot". The figure shows the range of winning prices by area (by the area where the power source is located). The thin line runs from the cheapest to the most expensive bid in the month. The thick band is the price range covering 8 out of every 10 slots with awards. The dot is the average winning price. Nationwide figures are in the tables in the "Market figures" section.

Among balancing market average winning prices by area, Primary reserve (offline) in Tohoku was the highest at 9.51 yen/kW per 30 min, and Tertiary reserve 2 in Shikoku was the lowest at 0.38 yen/kW per 30 min.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Compiled by ScienceX.

① Balancing market (EPRX) | Primary reserve (online) became a scramble for places

Primary reserve is the fastest product. It responds to frequency changes and changes output within 10 seconds (online) or within 30 seconds (offline). Batteries are often used for it. The left panel is the online category, and the right panel is the offline category. The bars are the bid-to-requirement ratio (bid volume ÷ volume requested by grid operators). Above 100% means more bids came in than were requested. Even below 100%, some bids are not awarded (see the award rate in the "Market figures" tables). The bars rise when bids increase, and also when the requested volume falls. The line is the average winning price. The figures are monthly, from April 2025, the earliest we have.

In the balancing market, the bid-to-requirement ratio for Primary reserve (online) passed 100% in June 2026 at 106.8%, and this month it was 123.1%. The average winning price was 3.43 yen/kW per 30 min. The requested volume was 3,066.1 MW in April 2025 and 1,388.7 MW this month. For Primary reserve (offline), this month's bid-to-requirement ratio was 84.0% and the average winning price was 5.79 yen/kW per 30 min. The requested volume was 1,122.5 MW in April 2025 and 497.8 MW this month.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Compiled by ScienceX.

② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | Average prices

Wholesale electricity is electricity bought and sold by power companies and retailers. Its price is set on the "spot market" of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), where electricity for the next day is traded the day before. "System (nationwide)" is the price when all of Japan is treated as one market (the system price). When the transmission lines between regions are congested, prices split by area, from Hokkaido to Kyushu (area prices).

The monthly average on wholesale electricity (JEPX) for System (nationwide) was 19.12 yen/kWh this month, against 11.62 yen/kWh in the same month last year. In Tokyo it was 23.78 yen/kWh this month, against 12.92 yen/kWh in the same month last year.

Source: Spot market trading results published by the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX). Simple average of 30-minute prices. Compiled by ScienceX.

② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | How large is the spread per kWh

We took the average price of the day's 4 most expensive hours, subtracted the average price of the 4 cheapest hours, and listed the monthly average by area. This equals the difference per kWh from buying 1 kWh in the 4 cheapest hours and selling it in the 4 most expensive hours. It is a simple difference: electricity lost in charging and discharging and fees are not subtracted, and the order of the cheap and expensive hours is not considered.

The monthly average spread between the 4 most expensive and 4 cheapest hours on wholesale electricity was largest in Tohoku at 19.11 yen/kWh and smallest in Tokyo at 13.42 yen/kWh.

③ Capacity market (OCCTO) | A market that buys future supply capacity

The capacity market buys electricity supply capacity 4 years ahead through an auction held once a year. Prices are set in yen per kW per year. The left figure shows the clearing prices of past main auctions. The right figure shows the battery and pumped hydro capacity awarded in the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction (a separate auction that, in principle, promises 20 years of capacity revenue for new decarbonized power sources and similar projects). These figures are published once a year, so they do not change each month. The latest is delivery FY2029 (published January 2026).

The capacity market clearing price for Tokyo was 14,137 yen/kW for delivery FY2024, 3,495 yen/kW for FY2025 and 15,111 yen/kW for FY2029. In the FY2025 Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction, battery and pumped hydro awards were 819 MW for lithium-ion batteries and pumped hydro (excluding new builds), and 886 MW for non-lithium-ion batteries, pumped hydro (new builds) and LDES. Both are the latest published annual values and do not change each month.

Source: Clearing results and capacity assurance contract documents published by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO) (sources listed in the last section (Assumptions and terms)). As published.

Our records | How many months and how much it takes to connect to the grid

A grid connection study is the procedure of asking the grid operator to check "can we connect at this site, and how much will the work cost?" The grid connection cost contribution is the part of the connection work cost paid by the applicant. Of the response documents we received, we compiled only the high-voltage ones (connecting to a 6,600 V distribution line), by grid area. Extra-high voltage is not included. Grid areas with fewer than 5 cases are not shown.

Median values in grid connection study responses (high voltage only, as of October 19, 2026) ranged from 8.0 months in Tokyo to 17.0 months in Tohoku for construction time, and from ¥3.02 million in Tokyo to ¥32.35 million in Tohoku for the grid connection cost contribution.

Source: Compilation of the high-voltage grid connection study responses we received from grid operators (as of October 19, 2026).

Assumptions and terms

  • Figures are compiled by ScienceX from the values published by the sources. We have not added up several months or converted figures to annual values. We do not show revenue amounts for any single battery storage plant.
  • Months covered: the balancing market (EPRX) uses preliminary data for September 2026 (delivery dates up to the 29th; up to the 30th for Primary reserve (offline)), and wholesale electricity (JEPX) covers September 2026 (30 days). For the balancing market, the month may differ until the published values for September 2026 are included in our compilation.
  • Balancing market: nationwide figures (the total column) of EPRX preliminary data. Requested volume is by grid operator (TSO); bid volume, awarded volume and average winning price are by area where the power source is located. Volumes are averages per 30-minute slot. The average winning price is weighted by awarded volume. Bid-to-requirement ratio = bid volume ÷ requested volume; award rate = awarded volume ÷ bid volume. The 3-hour blocks up to March 13, 2026 were counted as 6 30-minute slots per block.
  • Range of winning prices: the thin line runs from the cheapest bid (lowest winning price) to the most expensive bid (highest winning price) among the month's slots with awards (published values by area where the power source is located). The published values for Primary reserve (online), Secondary reserve 1 and 2 and Tertiary reserve 1 also include Composite product bids (one price for several combined products). So bids above 10 yen that can be explained by a Composite product bid in the same slot and area were removed from the thin line. For Primary reserve (offline), bids above the price cap (10 yen this month) were removed from the thin line (the balancing market system also awards bids above the cap, and cuts them down to the cap at settlement). The thick band is the range from 10% to 90% when the average winning prices of the slots with awards are ranked from cheapest. The dot is the average weighted by awarded volume.
  • Award rate by grid area × time: total awarded volume ÷ total bid volume for slots at the same time of day across the month (by area where the power source is located).
  • Response time and duration for balancing market products follow the FY2026 specifications (Electric Power Reserve Exchange, "Product Requirements", 6th edition). Primary reserve senses frequency changes on its own and responds. Online is the category where the grid operator monitors operation over a dedicated line; offline is the category where no dedicated line is installed and a record of the operation is submitted afterwards. The offline response time is within 30 seconds, with no set duration.
  • High-price slots for Tertiary reserve 2: slots with a nationwide average winning price of 10 yen/kW per 30 min or more. The top 1% is the top 1% of slots ranked by revenue (average winning price × awarded volume) (1% of the number of slots, rounded). The highest winning price is the published value by area where the power source is located, and is the price of the single most expensive awarded bid. In the balancing market, each winning bid is paid its own bid price (pay-as-bid (multi-price)), so the average winning price for the same slot and area is lower than this.
  • Average prices are simple averages of 30-minute slots (not weighted by trading volume). Daytime is the slots from 8:00 to 16:00, and evening is the slots from 16:00 to 20:00.
  • Spread: the day's 30-minute slots are ranked by price, and the average of the 8 cheapest slots is subtracted from the average of the 8 most expensive slots (4 hours) (4 slots for 2 hours). The order of the cheap and expensive hours, electricity lost in charging and discharging, and fees, taxes and transmission charges are not considered. This does not show actual revenue.
