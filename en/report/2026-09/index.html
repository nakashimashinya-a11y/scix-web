Battery Storage Market Notes, September 2026 issue

The price of electricity changes with the time of day. A "grid-scale battery" (a large battery connected to the power grid that stores electricity and releases it) earns money from that difference. These notes use published figures and our own records to show this month's trends in figures and tables. This issue is a back issue, prepared retroactively in November 2026. The grid connection study figures are as of the date it was prepared (October 19, 2026).

01

Three ways a battery storage plant earns money (how to read these notes)

A battery storage plant (grid-scale battery) earns income from three markets. ① Wholesale power (JEPX) is the market where you buy and store electricity when it is cheap and sell it when it is expensive. ② The balancing market (EPRX) is the market where you sell grid operators the "capability" to correct surpluses or shortages of electricity. ③ The capacity market (OCCTO) is the market where the country buys future electricity supply capacity in advance. These notes present figures for these three markets in this order. The ①②③ at the start of each section title shows which market it covers.

Source: Rules of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX) and the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO).

02

Three trends this month

From the figures for the three markets, we picked three trends for this month.

  • In wholesale power (JEPX), the monthly average spread between the most expensive 4 hours and the cheapest 4 hours in September 2026 is 13.42 yen/kWh in Tokyo and 15.17 yen/kWh in Kyushu.
  • In the balancing market (EPRX), the bid ratio for Primary reserve (online) went above 100% in June 2026 (106.8%). This month it is 123.1%, and the average winning price is 3.43 yen/kW per 30 min.
  • In the balancing market (EPRX), 56.9% of Tertiary reserve 2 revenue is concentrated in the top 10% of 30-minute slots.
03

① Wholesale power (JEPX) | Average price

Wholesale power is electricity bought and sold between power companies and retailers. Its price is set on the "spot market" of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), where electricity for the next day is traded the day before. "System (nationwide)" is the price when all of Japan is treated as one market (the system price). When the power lines between regions are congested, prices split by area from Hokkaido to Kyushu (area prices).

The average wholesale price in September 2026 is 19.12 yen/kWh for System (nationwide), against 11.62 yen/kWh in the same month last year (a difference of 7.50 yen).

Source: Spot market trading results published by the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX). Simple average of 30-minute prices. Our own count.

04

① Wholesale power (JEPX) | How the price moves through the day

The wholesale price changes every 30 minutes. The figure shows the shape of one day, averaging the price at the same time across the month. If a battery charges when power is cheap and discharges when it is expensive, the difference becomes the battery's income.

The cheapest and most expensive times of day for wholesale power are, in Tokyo, around 1:00 at 19.23 yen and around 16:30 at 30.77 yen, and in Kyushu, around 12:00 at 9.61 yen and around 17:30 at 23.75 yen.

Source: JEPX spot market. Our own count.

① Wholesale power (JEPX) | How large is the spread per kWh

We took the average price of the day's most expensive 4 hours, subtracted the average price of the cheapest 4 hours, and show the monthly average by area. This equals the difference per kWh if you buy 1 kWh in the cheapest 4 hours and sell it in the most expensive 4 hours. It is a simple difference. It does not deduct power lost in charging and discharging or fees, and it ignores the order of the cheap and expensive hours.

By area, the largest monthly average spread between the most expensive 4 hours and the cheapest 4 hours is in Tohoku at 19.11 yen/kWh, and the smallest is in Tokyo at 13.42 yen/kWh.

① Wholesale power (JEPX) | There is a spread every day

We show the same 4-hour spread day by day. The larger the spread on a day, the more room a battery has to earn from it.

For System (nationwide), the 4-hour wholesale spread was 10 yen or more on 28 of 30 days. By area, Tohoku had the most such days (27), and Hokuriku, Kansai and Kyushu had the fewest (19).

07

② Balancing market (EPRX) | Seven products and prices by area

The balancing market is where grid operators buy, every 30 minutes, the "capability" to correct surpluses or shortages of electricity. Products are divided by how fast output must change after an instruction. Primary reserve is the fastest and Tertiary reserve 2 is the slowest. Primary reserve has two categories: "online", connected to the grid operator by a dedicated line, and "offline", operated through the simplified dispatch system without a dedicated line. They are tendered and traded separately, so we show them separately. A Composite product is a bid that offers several products together. Prices are set in yen per 1 kW of capability per 30 min. The figure shows the range of winning prices by area (by area where the power source is located). The thin line runs from the month's cheapest winning bid to its most expensive, the thick band is the price range that holds 8 of every 10 slots with awards, and the dot is the average winning price. Nationwide figures are in the tables in the "Market figures" section.

By area where the power source is located, the highest average winning price in the balancing market is Primary reserve (offline) in Tohoku at 9.51 yen/kW per 30 min, and the lowest is Tertiary reserve 2 in Shikoku at 0.38 yen/kW per 30 min.

Source: Trading results (preliminary figures) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our own count.

09

② Balancing market (EPRX) | Primary reserve (online) became a contest for seats

Primary reserve is the fastest product: output must change within 10 seconds of an instruction. Batteries are often used for it. The left side is the online category and the right side the offline category. The bars are the bid ratio (bid volume ÷ volume the grid operators sought). Above 100%, more bids arrive than are sought, and some bids lose. The line is the average winning price. The figures are monthly, from April 2025, as far as our data goes. Primary reserve (offline) figures start in April 2026.

In the balancing market, the bid ratio for Primary reserve (online) went above 100% in June and is 123.1% this month, and for Primary reserve (offline) it is 84.0%. Their average winning prices are 3.43 and 5.79 yen/kW per 30 min.

Source: Trading results (preliminary figures) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our own count.

14

③ Capacity market (OCCTO) | A market that buys future supply capacity

The capacity market buys electricity supply capacity 4 years ahead through an auction held once a year. The price is set in yen per kW for one year. The left figure shows the clearing prices of past main auctions. The right figure shows the battery and pumped hydro capacity contracted in the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction (a separate auction that supports new builds such as batteries and pumped hydro). These are published once a year, so they do not change every month. The latest is delivery fiscal year FY2029 (published January 2026).

The capacity market clearing price (Tokyo) is 14,137 yen/kW for delivery FY2024 and 15,111 yen/kW for FY2029. In the FY2025 round of the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction, batteries and pumped hydro cleared 819 MW of lithium-ion and 886 MW of non-lithium. Both are the latest annual publication.

Source: Clearing results and capacity contract documents published by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO) (sources listed in the last section (Assumptions and terms)). As published.

Our records | How many months and how much it costs to connect to the grid

A grid connection study is the procedure of asking the grid operator to check "can we connect at this site, and how much will the work cost?" The grid connection cost contribution is the part of the connection work cost paid by the applicant. Of the response documents we received, we counted only high-voltage ones (connections to 6,600 V distribution lines), by grid area. Extra-high voltage is not included. Grid areas with fewer than 5 cases are not shown.

The medians of grid connection study responses (high voltage only, as of October 19, 2026) range from 8.0 months in Tokyo to 17.0 months in Tohoku for the construction period, and from ¥3.02 million in Tokyo to ¥32.35 million in Tohoku for the grid connection cost contribution.

Source: Our count of the high-voltage grid connection study responses we received from grid operators (as of October 19, 2026).

Assumptions and terms

  • The figures are our own counts based on the published values in the sources. We have not added up several months or converted anything to an annual figure. We do not show revenue amounts for any single battery storage plant.
  • Months covered: wholesale power (JEPX) is September 2026 (30 days); the balancing market (EPRX) is September 2026 preliminary figures (delivery dates up to the 29th). For the balancing market, the month may differ until the published September 2026 figures are included in our count.
  • Average prices are simple averages of 30-minute slots (not weighted by trading volume). Daytime is the 8:00–16:00 slots and evening the 16:00–20:00 slots.
  • Spread: the day's 30-minute slots are ranked by price, and the average of the 8 cheapest slots is subtracted from the average of the 8 most expensive slots (4 hours) (4 slots for 2 hours). We do not take into account the order of the cheap and expensive hours, the power lost in charging and discharging, or fees, taxes and grid usage charges. These figures do not show actual earnings.
  • Balancing market: nationwide figures (the total column) from EPRX preliminary data. Volume sought is by grid operator (TSO); bids, awarded volume and average winning price are by area where the power source is located. Volumes are averages per 30-minute slot. Average winning price is weighted by awarded volume. Bid ratio = bids ÷ volume sought; award rate = awarded volume ÷ bids. For the 3-hour blocks until March 13, 2026, we counted each block as six 30-minute slots.
  • Price range: the thin line runs, among the month's slots with awards, from the lowest winning bid (minimum winning price) to the highest (maximum winning price) (published figures by area where the power source is located). The published figures for Primary reserve (online), Secondary reserve 1 and 2, and Tertiary reserve 1 also include Composite product bids (one price for several products together), so we removed from the thin line any bids above 10 yen that can be explained by a Composite product bid in the same slot and area. For Primary reserve (offline), we removed from the thin line bids above the price cap (10 yen this month) (the balancing market system also awards bids above the cap and cuts them down to the cap at settlement). The thick band is the range from 10% to 90% from the bottom when the average winning prices of slots with awards are ranked from low to high. The dot is the average weighted by awarded volume.
  • Award rate by grid area × time of day: total awarded volume ÷ total bids for slots at the same time of day in the month (by area where the power source is located).
  • The time from instruction to output change and the duration for each balancing market product are the FY2026 specifications. For Primary reserve, online is the category connected to the grid operator by a dedicated line, and offline is the category operated through the simplified dispatch system without a dedicated line.
  • High-price slots for Tertiary reserve 2: slots with a nationwide average winning price of 10 yen/kW per 30 min or more. The top 1% are the top 1% of slots ranked by revenue (average winning price × awarded volume). The maximum winning price is the published figure by area where the power source is located.
