Battery Storage Market Notes, April 2026 issue

The price of electricity changes with the time of day. A "grid-scale battery" (a large battery connected to the power grid that stores electricity and releases it) earns money from that difference. These notes use published figures and our own records to show this month's market in figures and tables. This issue is a back issue, produced in November 2026 to cover an earlier month. The grid connection study figures are as of the date this issue was produced (October 19, 2026).

01

The 3 income sources of a battery storage plant (how to read these notes)

A battery storage plant (grid-scale battery) earns income from 3 markets. ① Wholesale power (JEPX): the battery buys electricity when it is cheap, stores it, and sells it when the price is high. ② The balancing market (EPRX): the battery sells grid operators the "capacity" to fix a surplus or shortage of electricity. ③ The capacity market (OCCTO): the state buys future power supply capacity in advance. These notes present figures for these 3 markets in that order. The ①, ② or ③ at the start of each section title shows which market the section covers.

Source: Market rules of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX) and Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO).

02

3 developments this month

From the figures for the 3 markets, we picked 3 developments this month.

  • In wholesale power (JEPX), the monthly average spread between the highest 4 hours and the lowest 4 hours in April 2026 was 23.12 yen/kWh in Tokyo and 13.02 yen/kWh in Kyushu.
  • In the balancing market (EPRX), the bid-to-requirement ratio for Primary reserve (online) was 74.3% this month. It has not yet gone above 100% in any month. The average winning price was 4.27 yen/kW per 30 min.
  • In the balancing market (EPRX), the top 10% of 30-minute slots by revenue for Tertiary reserve 2 accounted for 60.2% of the month's revenue.
03

① Wholesale power (JEPX) | Average price

Wholesale power is electricity bought and sold between power companies and retailers. Its price is set on the "spot market" of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), where electricity for the next day is traded the day before. "System (nationwide)" is the price if all of Japan is treated as one market (the system price). When the power lines between regions get congested, prices split by area from Hokkaido to Kyushu (area prices).

The monthly average wholesale system price for System (nationwide) was 14.77 yen/kWh this month (April 2026) and 9.82 yen/kWh in the same month last year.

Source: Spot market trading results published by the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX). Simple average of 30-minute prices. Our tally.

04

① Wholesale power (JEPX) | How the price moves through the day

The wholesale price changes every 30 minutes. The figure shows the daily shape, averaging the price at each time of day across the month. If a battery charges when power is cheap and discharges when it is expensive, the difference is its income.

On the daily average, wholesale prices in both Tokyo and Kyushu hit their low around 12:30 and their peak around 18:30: 11.42 yen/kWh and 33.06 yen/kWh in Tokyo, and 3.50 yen/kWh and 17.19 yen/kWh in Kyushu.

Source: JEPX spot market. Our tally.

05

① Wholesale power (JEPX) | How large is the spread per kWh

For each day, we took the average price of the highest 4 hours minus the average price of the lowest 4 hours, and show the monthly average by area. This equals the difference per kWh if you buy 1 kWh in the cheapest 4 hours and sell it in the highest 4 hours. It is a simple difference: it does not deduct electricity lost in charging and discharging or fees, and it ignores whether the cheap hours come before the expensive ones.

The monthly average spread between the highest 4 hours and the lowest 4 hours was largest in Tokyo at 23.12 yen/kWh and smallest in Shikoku at 9.41 yen/kWh.

Source: JEPX spot market. Our tally.

06

① Wholesale power (JEPX) | There is a spread every day

This shows the same 4-hour spread for each day. The larger the spread on a day, the more room a battery has to earn from it.

For System (nationwide), the 4-hour wholesale spread was 10 yen/kWh or more on 24 of 30 days. By area, Tokyo had the most such days (29) and Shikoku the fewest (11).

Source: JEPX spot market. Our tally.

07

② Balancing market (EPRX) | 7 products, and prices by area

In the balancing market, grid operators buy the "capacity" to fix a surplus or shortage of electricity, every 30 minutes. Products differ by how quickly output must change after an instruction. Primary reserve is the fastest and Tertiary reserve 2 is the slowest. Primary reserve has 2 categories: "online," connected to the grid operator by a dedicated line, and "offline," operated through the simplified dispatch system without a dedicated line. They are tendered and traded separately, so we show them separately. A Composite product is a bid that offers several products together. The price is set in yen per 1 kW of available capacity per 30 minutes. The figure shows the range of winning prices by area (the area where the power source is located). The thin line runs from the lowest to the highest winning bid in the month. The thick band is the price range covering 8 out of 10 slots with winning bids. The dot is the average winning price. Nationwide figures are in the table in the "Market figures" section.

By area where the power source is located, the highest average winning price in the balancing market was Tertiary reserve 2 in Tohoku at 10.06 yen/kW per 30 min, and the lowest was Tertiary reserve 2 in Shikoku at 0.37 yen/kW per 30 min.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our tally.

Where a line is broken, there were no bids at that time.

09

② Balancing market (EPRX) | How full are the Primary reserve slots

Primary reserve is the fastest product, changing output within 10 seconds of an instruction, and batteries are often used for it. The left side shows the online category and the right side the offline category. The bars show the bid-to-requirement ratio (bid volume ÷ volume tendered by grid operators). Above 100%, more bids come in than were tendered, and some bids lose. A bar rises when bids increase or when the tendered volume falls. The line shows the average winning price. These are monthly figures from April 2025, as far as our data goes.

For Primary reserve (online) in the balancing market, the bid-to-requirement ratio this month was 74.3% (no month has yet gone above 100%) and the average winning price was 4.27 yen/kW per 30 min. The tendered volume was 3,066.1 MW in April 2025 and 1,598.4 MW this month. For Primary reserve (offline), the ratio this month was 30.9% and the average winning price was 12.29 yen/kW per 30 min. The tendered volume was 1,122.5 MW in April 2025 and 500.6 MW this month.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our tally.

② Balancing market (EPRX) | How prices have moved by product

These are average winning prices by product from April 2025 (monthly averages weighted by awarded volume). From March 14, 2026, Primary through Tertiary reserve 1 and the Composite product changed from trading in 3-hour blocks to 30-minute slots (Tertiary reserve 2 was already in 30-minute slots).

The average winning price for Primary reserve (online) in the balancing market was 2.89 yen in April 2025 and 4.27 yen this month, with the highest at 4.90 yen in February 2026 (yen/kW per 30 min). Tertiary reserve 2 was 1.12 yen in April 2025 and 3.03 yen this month (yen/kW per 30 min), its highest month. For the other products, the highest month was July 2025.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our tally.

② Balancing market (EPRX) | Revenue is concentrated in a small number of 30-minute slots

We ranked the month's 30-minute slots with winning bids by revenue (average winning price × awarded volume, nationwide), and plotted, for each product, what percentage of the month's revenue the top slots hold. The more a curve bulges above the dotted line, the more revenue is concentrated in a few slots. Tertiary reserve 2 covers errors in forecasting weather-dependent renewable output. Its price is low in most slots and high in a small number of slots.

The share of the month's revenue held by the top 10% of slots in the balancing market was 60.2% for Tertiary reserve 2 and 16.0% for Primary reserve (online). For the top 1% of slots, it was 9.0% for Tertiary reserve 2 and 1.9% for Primary reserve (online).

14

③ Capacity market (OCCTO) | A market that buys future supply capacity

The capacity market buys power supply capacity 4 years ahead, through one auction a year. The price is set in yen per kW per year. The left figure shows clearing prices in past main auctions. The right figure shows the battery and pumped-hydro capacity awarded in the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction (a separate auction that supports new builds such as batteries and pumped hydro). These are published once a year, so they do not change every month. The latest is delivery fiscal year FY2029 (published January 2026).

The capacity market main auction clearing price for Tokyo was 14,137 yen/kW for FY2024, 3,495 yen/kW for FY2025, and 15,111 yen/kW for FY2029. In the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction, the awarded battery and pumped-hydro capacity in the FY2025 auction was 819 MW for lithium-ion and 886 MW for non-lithium. Both are from the latest annual publication and do not change every month.

Source: Auction results and capacity contract documents published by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO) (links in the last section (Assumptions and terms)). As published.

Our records | How many months and how much it costs to connect to the grid

A grid connection study is a procedure in which the grid operator checks "whether this site can be connected and how much the work will cost." The grid connection cost contribution is the part of the connection work paid by the applicant. Of the response documents we received, we tallied only the high-voltage cases (connected to 6,600 V distribution lines), by grid area. Extra-high-voltage cases are not included. Grid areas with fewer than 5 cases are not shown.

By median in the grid connection study responses (high-voltage only, as of October 19, 2026), the construction period was shortest in Tokyo at 8.0 months and longest in Tohoku at 17.0 months. The grid connection cost contribution was smallest in Tokyo at ¥3.02 million and largest in Tohoku at ¥32.35 million.

Source: Our tally of high-voltage grid connection study responses we received from general transmission and distribution operators (as of October 19, 2026).

Assumptions and terms

  • The figures are our tally of the published values in the sources. We have not added up several months or converted figures to an annual basis. We do not show income amounts for any individual battery storage plant.
  • Months covered: wholesale power (JEPX) is April 2026 (30 days), and the balancing market (EPRX) is the April 2026 preliminary data (delivery dates up to the 30th). For the balancing market, the month may differ until the published April 2026 figures are included in the tally.
  • Average prices are simple averages of 30-minute slots (not weighted by trading volume). Daytime is the slots from 8:00 to 16:00, and evening is from 16:00 to 20:00.
  • Spread: the day's 30-minute slots are sorted by price, and the average of the cheapest 8 slots (4 hours) is subtracted from the average of the most expensive 8 slots (4 slots for 2 hours). It ignores whether the cheap hours come before the expensive ones, electricity lost in charging and discharging, and fees, taxes and transmission charges. It does not show actual income.
  • Balancing market: nationwide (total column) of EPRX preliminary data. Tendered volume is by grid operator (TSO); bid volume, awarded volume and average winning price are by area where the power source is located. Volumes are averages per 30-minute slot. The average winning price is weighted by awarded volume. Bid-to-requirement ratio = bid volume ÷ tendered volume; win rate = awarded volume ÷ bid volume. The 3-hour blocks used up to March 13, 2026 are counted as 6 30-minute slots per block.
  • Range of winning prices: the thin line runs from the cheapest winning bid (minimum winning price) to the most expensive (maximum winning price) among the month's 30-minute slots with winning bids (published values by area where the power source is located). The published values for Primary reserve (online), Secondary reserve 1 and 2, and Tertiary reserve 1 also include Composite product bids (one price for several products together). So bids above the price cap of 15 yen for Primary reserve (online) and Secondary reserve 1 (for delivery up to August 31, 2026), and above 10 yen for Secondary reserve 2 and Tertiary reserve 1, that can be explained by a Composite product bid in the same 30-minute slot and area, were removed from the thin line. For Primary reserve (offline), bids above the price cap (15 yen this month) were removed from the thin line (the balancing market system also awards bids above the cap, and cuts them down to the cap at settlement). The thick band is the range from the 10% to the 90% point when the average winning prices of the 30-minute slots with winning bids are sorted from lowest to highest. The dot is the average weighted by awarded volume.
  • Win rate by grid area × time of day: total awarded volume ÷ total bid volume for the slots at the same time of day across the month (by area where the power source is located).
  • For balancing market products, the time from instruction to output change and the duration follow the FY2026 specifications. For Primary reserve, online is the category connected to the grid operator by a dedicated line, and offline is the category operated through the simplified dispatch system without a dedicated line.
  • High-price 30-minute slots for Tertiary reserve 2: slots with a nationwide average winning price of 10 yen/kW per 30 min or more. The top 1% is the top 1% of slots ranked by revenue (average winning price × awarded volume). The maximum winning price is the published value by area where the power source is located.
