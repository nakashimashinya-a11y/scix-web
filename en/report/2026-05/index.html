Battery Storage Market Notes, May 2026 issue

"Grid-scale batteries" (large batteries connected to the power grid that store electricity and release it) earn money in two ways. They sell the "capability" to fix surpluses and shortages of electricity, and they use the price differences between times of day. These notes use published figures and our own records to show this month's trends in figures and tables. This issue is a back issue, produced retroactively in November 2026. The grid connection study totals are as of the time of production (October 19, 2026).

01

Three revenue sources for a battery storage plant (how to read these notes)

A battery storage plant (grid-scale battery) earns revenue from three markets. ① The balancing market (EPRX) is where plants sell the "capability" to fix electricity surpluses and shortages to grid operators. ② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) is where plants buy and store electricity when it is cheap and sell it when it is expensive. ③ The capacity market (OCCTO) is where the country buys future electricity supply capacity in advance. These notes present the figures in the order of these three markets. The ①, ② or ③ at the start of each section title shows which market the section covers.

Source: Rules of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX) and Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO).

02

Three trends this month

We picked three trends this month from the figures for the three markets.

  • In the balancing market (EPRX), this month's bid-to-procurement ratio for Primary reserve (online) was 71.0%. No month has yet gone above 100%. The average winning price was 4.84 yen/kW per 30 min.
  • In the balancing market (EPRX), 54.2% of the month's revenue for Tertiary reserve 2 came from the top 10% of 30-minute slots by revenue.
  • In wholesale electricity (JEPX), the monthly average spread between the most expensive 4 hours and the cheapest 4 hours was 18.00 yen/kWh in Tokyo and 16.64 yen/kWh in Kyushu.
03

① Balancing market (EPRX) | Seven products and prices by area

The balancing market is where grid operators buy, every 30 minutes, the "capability" to fix electricity surpluses and shortages. Products are divided by how quickly output can be changed (Primary reserve responds to changes in frequency; Secondary and Tertiary reserve respond to instructions from the grid operator). Primary reserve is the fastest, and Tertiary reserve 2 is the slowest. Primary reserve is a product that senses changes in frequency on its own and responds. It has two categories, shown separately because they are offered and traded separately: "online", where the grid operator monitors operation through a dedicated line, and "offline", where no dedicated line is installed and the operating record is submitted afterwards. A Composite product is a bid that bundles several products together. Prices are set in yen per 1 kW of available capability per 30 minutes. kW is a unit of how much power can be delivered, which is different from kWh, the amount of electricity. Each 30-minute trading round is called a "30-minute slot". The figure shows the range of winning prices by area (by the area where the power source is located). The thin line runs from the cheapest bid to the most expensive bid in the month, the thick band is the price range covering 8 out of 10 slots with awards, and the dot is the average winning price. Nationwide figures are in the table in the "Market figures" section.

By area, the highest average winning price in the balancing market was 14.86 yen/kW per 30 min for Primary reserve (offline) in Tohoku, and the lowest was 0.33 yen/kW per 30 min for Tertiary reserve 2 in Shikoku.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our totals.

① Balancing market (EPRX) | How full are the Primary reserve seats?

Primary reserve is the fastest product. It responds to changes in frequency and changes output within 10 seconds (online) or within 30 seconds (offline). Batteries are often used for it. The left side shows the online category and the right side the offline category. The bars show the bid-to-procurement ratio (bid volume ÷ volume procured by grid operators). Above 100% means more bids came in than were procured. Even below 100%, some bids do not win (the award rate is in the "Market figures" table). A bar rises when bids increase or when the procured volume falls. The line shows the average winning price. These are monthly figures from April 2025, the earliest we have.

In the balancing market, Primary reserve (online) had a bid-to-procurement ratio of 71.0% this month and an average winning price of 4.84 yen/kW per 30 min. Primary reserve (offline) had a bid-to-procurement ratio of 40.4% and an average winning price of 12.53 yen/kW per 30 min. For both, no month has yet had a ratio above 100%. Procured volume for Primary reserve (online) was 3,066.1 MW in April 2025 and 1,581.1 MW this month; for Primary reserve (offline) it was 1,122.5 MW in April 2025 and 498.2 MW this month.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our totals.

② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | Average prices

Wholesale electricity is the electricity bought and sold between power companies and retailers. Its price is set on the "spot market" of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), where electricity for the next day is traded the day before. "System (nationwide)" is the price when all of Japan is treated as one market (the system price). When the transmission lines between regions become congested, prices split by area from Hokkaido to Kyushu (area prices).

The monthly average system price for wholesale electricity (JEPX), System (nationwide), was 14.06 yen/kWh, against 8.92 yen/kWh in the same month last year, a difference of 5.14 yen/kWh.

Source: Spot market trading results published by the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX). Simple average of 30-minute prices. Our totals.

We took the average price of the day's most expensive 4 hours minus the average price of its cheapest 4 hours, and show the monthly average by area. This equals the difference per kWh from buying 1 kWh in the cheapest 4 hours and selling it in the most expensive 4 hours. It is a simple difference: it does not deduct electricity lost in charging and discharging or fees, and it ignores the order of the cheap and expensive hours.

③ Capacity market (OCCTO) | A market that buys future supply capacity

The capacity market buys electricity supply capacity four years ahead through a once-a-year auction. Prices are set in yen per kW per year. The left figure shows past main auction clearing prices. The right figure shows the battery and pumped hydro capacity awarded in the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction (a separate auction that promises capacity revenue, in principle for 20 years, to new decarbonized power sources and similar). Results are published once a year, so they do not change monthly. The latest is for delivery FY2029 (published January 2026).

Capacity market main auction clearing prices for Tokyo were 14,137 yen/kW for delivery FY2024, 3,495 yen/kW for FY2025, 5,834 yen/kW for FY2026, 9,555 yen/kW for FY2027, 14,812 yen/kW for FY2028 and 15,111 yen/kW for FY2029. In the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction, awarded battery and pumped hydro capacity in the FY2025 round was 819 MW for lithium-ion batteries and pumped hydro (excluding new builds) and 886 MW for non-lithium-ion batteries, pumped hydro (new builds) and LDES. Both are annual values, so they are the latest published figures and do not change monthly.

Source: Clearing results and capacity assurance contract documents published by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO) (sources in the last section (Assumptions and terms)). As published.

Our records | How many months and how much does it take to connect to the grid?

A grid connection study is the procedure of asking the grid operator to check "can we connect at this site, and how much will the work cost?". The grid connection cost contribution is the part of the connection work cost paid by the applicant. Of the study responses we received, we totaled only the high-voltage ones (connections to a 6,600 V distribution line) by grid area. Extra-high voltage is not included. Grid areas with fewer than 5 cases are not shown.

Separately from the wholesale electricity and balancing market figures, the median grid connection study response (high-voltage only, as of October 19, 2026) shows construction periods from 8.0 months in Tokyo to 17.0 months in Tohoku, and grid connection cost contributions from ¥3.02 million in Tokyo to ¥32.35 million in Tohoku.

Source: Our totals of high-voltage grid connection study responses received from general transmission and distribution operators (as of October 19, 2026).

Assumptions and terms

  • Figures are our totals based on the values published by the sources. We have not summed multiple months or converted figures to annual amounts. We do not show revenue amounts for any single battery storage plant.
  • Months covered: the balancing market (EPRX) uses preliminary values for May 2026 (delivery dates up to the 31st), and wholesale electricity (JEPX) covers May 2026 (31 days). For the balancing market, the month may differ until the published values for May 2026 are included in our totals.
  • Balancing market: EPRX preliminary values, nationwide (total column). Procured volume is by grid operator (TSO); bid volume, awarded volume and average winning price are by the area where the power source is located. Volumes are averages per 30-minute slot. The average winning price is weighted by awarded volume. Bid-to-procurement ratio = bid volume ÷ procured volume; award rate = awarded volume ÷ bid volume. 3-hour blocks up to March 13, 2026 were counted as six 30-minute slots per block.
  • Range of winning prices: the thin line runs from the cheapest bid (lowest winning price) to the most expensive bid (highest winning price) among the month's slots with awards (published values by the area where the power source is located). The published values for Primary reserve (online), Secondary reserve 1 and 2, and Tertiary reserve 1 also include Composite product bids (a single price for a bundle of several products). So we removed from the thin line any bids above the price cap of 15 yen (for delivery up to August 31, 2026) for Primary reserve (online) and Secondary reserve 1, or above 10 yen for Secondary reserve 2 and Tertiary reserve 1, where a Composite product bid in the same slot and area explains them. For Primary reserve (offline), we removed from the thin line bids above the price cap (15 yen this month) (the balancing market system also awards bids above the cap and cuts them down to the cap at settlement). The thick band is the range from 10% to 90% from the bottom when the average winning prices of slots with awards are sorted from lowest to highest. The dot is the average weighted by awarded volume.
  • Award rate by grid area × time of day: total awarded volume ÷ total bid volume for slots at the same time across the month (by the area where the power source is located).
  • The response time and duration for balancing market products are the FY2026 specifications (Electric Power Reserve Exchange "Product requirements", 6th edition). Primary reserve senses changes in frequency on its own and responds. Online is the category where the grid operator monitors operation through a dedicated line; offline is the category where no dedicated line is installed and the operating record is submitted afterwards. The offline response time is within 30 seconds, and there is no set duration.
  • High-priced slots for Tertiary reserve 2: slots with a nationwide average winning price of 10 yen/kW per 30 min or more. The top 1% is the top 1% of slots ranked by revenue (average winning price × awarded volume) (1% of the number of slots, rounded). The top winning price is the published value by the area where the power source is located, and is the price of the single most expensive winning bid. In the balancing market, each winning bid is settled at its own bid price (pay-as-bid (multi-price)), so the average winning price for the same slot and area is lower than this.
  • Average prices are simple averages of 30-minute slots (not weighted by trading volume). Daytime is the slots from 8:00 to 16:00, and evening is the slots from 16:00 to 20:00.
  • Spread: the day's 30-minute slots are sorted by price, and the average of the cheapest 8 slots is subtracted from the average of the most expensive 8 slots (4 hours) (4 slots for 2 hours). It does not consider the order of the cheap and expensive hours, electricity lost in charging and discharging, or fees, taxes and transmission charges. It does not show actual revenue.
