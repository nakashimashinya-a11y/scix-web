Battery Storage Market Notes, October 2026 issue

The price of electricity changes with the time of day it is used. "Grid-scale battery storage" (large batteries connected to the transmission grid that store electricity and release it) earns money from that difference. These notes use published figures and our own records to show this month's movements in figures and tables.

01

The three income streams of a battery storage plant (how to read these notes)

A battery storage plant (grid-scale battery storage) earns income from three markets. ① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) is the market where you buy and store electricity when it is cheap and sell it when it is expensive. ② The balancing market (EPRX) is the market where you sell grid operators the "capacity" to correct surpluses or shortages of electricity. ③ The capacity market (OCCTO) is the market where the government buys future electricity supply capacity in advance. These notes set out the numbers for the three markets in this order. The ①, ② or ③ at the start of each section title shows which market it covers.

Source: Rules of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX) and the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO).

02

Three movements this month

We picked three movements this month from the numbers of the three markets.

  • In wholesale electricity (JEPX), the monthly average gap between the most expensive 4 hours and the cheapest 4 hours in October 2026 was 17.14 yen/kWh in Tokyo and 19.30 yen/kWh in Kyushu.
  • In the balancing market (EPRX), the bid ratio for Primary reserve passed 100% for the first time since April 2025, at 106.8% in June 2026. In September 2026 it was 123.1%, and the average winning price was 3.43 yen/kW per 30 min.
  • In the balancing market (EPRX), 56.9% of Tertiary reserve 2 revenue in September 2026 was concentrated in the top 10% of 30-minute slots by revenue.
03

① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | Average price

Wholesale electricity is the electricity that power companies and retail suppliers buy and sell among themselves. Its price is set on the "spot market" of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), where electricity for the next day is traded the day before. "System (nationwide)" is the price when all of Japan is treated as one market (the system price). When the transmission lines between regions are congested, the price splits into separate prices for each area from Hokkaido to Kyushu (area prices).

The average System (nationwide) price for wholesale electricity in October 2026 was 22.51 yen/kWh, compared with 11.67 yen/kWh in the same month last year.

Source: Spot market trading results published by the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX). Simple average of the 30-minute prices. Our own tally.

04

① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | How the price moves through the day

Wholesale electricity prices change every 30 minutes. The figure shows the shape of an average day: the prices at the same time of day are averaged across the month. If a battery stores electricity in the cheap hours and releases it in the expensive hours, the difference becomes its income.

The daily average low and high for wholesale electricity were 19.03 yen around 12:00 and 37.11 yen around 16:30 in Tokyo, and 4.94 yen around 12:00 and 24.74 yen around 17:30 in Kyushu (yen/kWh).

Source: JEPX spot market. Our own tally.

05

① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | How large is the price gap per kWh

For each area, we took the average price of the day's most expensive 4 hours, subtracted the average price of the day's cheapest 4 hours, and show the monthly average. This equals the gain per kWh from buying 1 kWh in the cheapest 4 hours and selling it in the most expensive 4 hours. It is a simple difference. It does not deduct electricity lost in charging and discharging or fees, and it ignores whether the cheap hours come before or after the expensive hours.

By area, the monthly average gap between the most expensive and the cheapest 4 hours of wholesale electricity was largest in Hokkaido at 29.02 yen/kWh and smallest in Tokyo at 17.14 yen/kWh.

① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | There is a price gap every day

We show the same 4-hour gap day by day. The bigger the gap on a given day, the more room a battery has to earn from the price gap.

07

② Balancing market (EPRX) | Six products, and prices by area

The balancing market is where grid operators buy, every 30 minutes, the "capacity" to correct surpluses or shortages of electricity. The products differ by how fast output must change after an instruction. Primary reserve is the fastest, and Tertiary reserve 2 is the slowest. A Composite product is a bid that offers several products together. Prices are set in yen per 1 kW of capacity offered per 30 minutes. The figure shows the average winning price and the clearing rate (the share of bid volume that won) by area (by the area where the power source is located). Darker colors mean higher values. Nationwide figures are in the tables in the "Market figures" section. Published figures for October 2026 are not yet in our tally, so we show the latest month with all products available: September 2026 (1st to 29th), preliminary figures.

In September 2026, by the area where the power source is located, the highest average winning price in the balancing market was Primary reserve in Tohoku at 8.18 yen, and the lowest was Tertiary reserve 2 in Shikoku at 0.38 yen (yen/kW per 30 min).

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our own tally.

08

② Balancing market (EPRX) | Primary reserve has become a race for seats

Primary reserve is the product where batteries have the highest share of the winning volume. The bars show the bid ratio (bid volume ÷ volume requested by grid operators). Above 100%, more bids come in than the volume requested, and some bids lose. The line shows the average winning price. These are the monthly figures we have, starting from April 2025.

The bid ratio for Primary reserve in the balancing market passed 100% for the first time since April 2025 in June 2026 (106.8%), and was 123.1% in September. The average winning price was 3.43 yen/kW per 30 min, compared with 3.57 yen in the same month last year.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our own tally.

The average winning price for Primary reserve in the balancing market was 2.89 yen in April 2025 and 3.43 yen in September 2026, with a high of 4.90 yen in February 2026 (yen/kW per 30 min).

② Balancing market (EPRX) | Revenue is concentrated in a small number of slots

For each product, we ranked the month's 30-minute slots that had winning bids by revenue (average winning price × winning volume, nationwide), from highest to lowest, and plotted what % of the month's revenue the top slots account for. The more the curve bulges above the dotted line, the more the revenue is concentrated in a few slots. Tertiary reserve 2 is a product that covers errors in forecasting renewable output caused by the weather. Its price is low in most slots and high in a very small number of slots.

In the balancing market (September 2026), the share of revenue concentrated in the top 10% of slots was 56.9% for Tertiary reserve 2 and 15.8% for Primary reserve.

13

③ Capacity market (OCCTO) | A market that buys future supply capacity

The capacity market buys electricity supply capacity four years ahead through a once-a-year auction. The price is set in yen per kW per year. The left figure shows the clearing prices of past main auctions. The right figure shows the battery and pumped hydro capacity that cleared in the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction (a separate auction that supports new builds of batteries, pumped hydro and others). These figures are published once a year, so they do not change from month to month. The latest is for delivery FY2029 (published January 2026).

The capacity market clearing price in Tokyo went from 3,495 yen/kW for delivery FY2025 to 15,111 yen/kW for FY2029. In the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction, batteries and pumped hydro bid in FY2025 cleared 819 MW of lithium-ion and 886 MW of non-lithium. Both are the latest once-a-year published figures.

Source: Clearing results and capacity assurance contract documents published by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO) (sources listed in section 15). As published.

Our records | Where battery storage projects are increasing

We counted the projects newly listed this month on DealRoom (our data room for buyers, a site where we list battery storage projects for buyers), by grid area of the grid operator. These are numbers for the projects we handled, not counts for the whole market. Transfer of rights is a way of selling by handing over the rights to the land and the power. Source: Our DealRoom records (project locations are given only down to the grid area; prices and parties are not listed).

Our records | How many months and how much it takes to connect to the grid

A grid connection study is a procedure where you ask the grid operator to check "Can we connect at this location, and how much will the work cost?" The grid connection cost contribution is the part of the connection work cost paid by the applicant. From the responses we received, we tallied only the high-voltage cases (connections to 6,600 V distribution lines), by grid area. Extra-high voltage is not included. Grid areas with fewer than 5 cases are not shown.

The medians of the grid connection study responses (high voltage only, as of October 19, 2026) ranged from 8.0 months in Tokyo to 17.0 months in Tohoku for the construction period, and from ¥3.02 million in Tokyo to ¥32.35 million in Tohoku for the grid connection cost contribution.

Source: Tally of the high-voltage grid connection study responses we received from general transmission and distribution operators (as of October 19, 2026).

Assumptions and terms

  • The figures are our own tally of the values published in the sources. We have not added up several months or converted anything to annual figures. We do not show revenue amounts for any single battery storage plant.
  • Months covered: wholesale electricity (JEPX) covers October 2026 (31 days), and the balancing market (EPRX) covers preliminary figures for September 2026 (delivery dates up to the 29th). The balancing market will cover a different month until the published figures for October 2026 are in our tally.
  • Average prices are simple averages of the 30-minute slots (not weighted by trading volume). Daytime means the slots from 08:00 to 16:00, and evening means the slots from 16:00 to 20:00.
  • Price gap: we rank the day's 30-minute slots by price and take the average of the highest 8 slots (4 hours) minus the average of the lowest 8 slots (4 slots for 2 hours). It does not consider whether the cheap hours come before or after the expensive hours, electricity lost in charging and discharging, or fees, taxes and transmission charges. It does not show actual revenue.
  • Balancing market: the nationwide figures (total column) of the EPRX preliminary figures. Volume requested is by grid operator (TSO); bid volume, winning volume and average winning price are by the area where the power source is located. Volumes are averages per 30-minute slot. The average winning price is weighted by winning volume. Bid ratio = bid volume ÷ volume requested; clearing rate = winning volume ÷ bid volume. For the 3-hour blocks up to March 13, 2026, we counted each block as six 30-minute slots.
  • The time from instruction to change in output and the duration for each balancing market product are the FY2026 specifications.
  • High-price slots for Tertiary reserve 2: slots where the nationwide average winning price was 10 yen/kW per 30 min or more. The top 1% means the top 1% of slots ranked by revenue (average winning price × winning volume). The maximum winning prices are the published values by the area where the power source is located.
