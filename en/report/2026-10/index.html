Battery Storage Market Notes, October 2026 issue

"Grid-scale batteries" (large batteries connected to the grid that store electricity and release it again) earn money by selling the "capacity" to fix surpluses and shortages of electricity, and from the price difference as electricity prices change through the day. These notes use published figures and our own records to show this month's trends in figures and tables.

01

The three income sources of a battery storage site (how to read these notes)

A battery storage site (a grid-scale battery) earns income from three markets. ① The balancing market (EPRX) is the market where the site sells grid operators the "capability" to correct surpluses and shortages of electricity. ② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) is the market where the site buys electricity at cheap times, stores it, and sells it at expensive times. ③ The capacity market (OCCTO) is the market where the government buys future electricity supply capacity in advance. These notes present figures for these three markets in this order. The ①②③ at the start of each section title shows which market the section covers.

Source: The market rules of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX) and the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO).

02

Three trends this month

We picked three trends this month from the figures of the three markets.

  • In the balancing market (EPRX), the bid ratio for Primary reserve (online) went above 100% in June 2026, at 106.8%. In September 2026 it was 123.1%, and the average awarded price was 3.43 yen/kW per 30 min.
  • In the balancing market (EPRX), the top 10% of 30-minute slots by sales accounted for 56.9% of the month's sales for Tertiary reserve 2 in September 2026.
  • In wholesale electricity (JEPX), the spread between the average of the 4 most expensive hours and the average of the 4 cheapest hours (monthly average) in October 2026 was 17.14 yen/kWh in Tokyo and 19.30 yen/kWh in Kyushu.
03

① Balancing market (EPRX) | Seven products, and prices by area

The balancing market is where grid operators buy, every 30 minutes, the "capability" to correct surpluses and shortages of electricity. Products differ by how fast output must change after a dispatch instruction. Primary reserve is the fastest, and Tertiary reserve 2 is the slowest. Primary reserve has two categories. "Online" is connected to the grid operator by a dedicated line. "Offline" runs on the simplified dispatch system without a dedicated line. The two are procured and traded separately, so we show them separately. A Composite product is a bid that offers several products together. Prices are set in yen per 1 kW of capability per 30 minutes. kW is a unit of power capacity, not to be confused with kWh, which measures the amount of electricity. One 30-minute trading unit is called a "slot". The figure shows the range of awarded prices by area (by the area where the power source is located). The thin line runs from the lowest to the highest awarded bid in the month. The thick band is the price range that contains 8 out of every 10 slots with awards. The dot is the average awarded price. Nationwide figures are in the table in the "Market figures" section. Published figures for October 2026 are not yet in our aggregation, so we show the latest month with all products available: September 2026 (1–29), preliminary figures.

In September 2026, the average awarded price in the balancing market, by the area where the power source is located, was highest for Primary reserve (offline) in Tohoku, at 9.51 yen/kW per 30 min, and lowest for Tertiary reserve 2 in Shikoku, at 0.38 yen/kW per 30 min.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our own aggregation.

05

① Balancing market (EPRX) | Primary reserve (online) became a competition for seats

Primary reserve is the fastest product. Output must change within 10 seconds of an instruction, and batteries are often used for it. The left side is the online category, and the right side is the offline category. The bars show the bid ratio (bid volume ÷ volume procured by grid operators). Above 100%, more bids come in than are procured, and some bids are rejected. The bars rise when bids increase and also when the procured volume falls. The line shows the average awarded price. These are monthly figures from April 2025, the data we have.

The bid ratio for Primary reserve (online) in the balancing market went above 100% in June 2026, at 106.8%, and was 123.1% in September 2026. The average awarded price went from 2.89 yen/kW per 30 min in April 2025 to 3.43 yen/kW per 30 min in September 2026. The procured volume went from 3,066.1MW in April 2025 to 1,388.7MW in September 2026. For Primary reserve (offline), the bid ratio in September 2026 was 84.0% and the average awarded price was 5.79 yen/kW per 30 min. The procured volume went from 1,122.5MW in April 2025 to 497.8MW in September 2026.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our own aggregation.

② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | Average price

Wholesale electricity is the electricity that power companies and retailers buy and sell among themselves. Its price is set on the "spot market" of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), where electricity for the next day is traded the day before. "System (nationwide)" is the price when all of Japan is treated as one market (the system price). When the power lines between regions are congested, prices split by area, from Hokkaido to Kyushu (the area prices).

The monthly average of the System (nationwide) price in wholesale electricity (JEPX) was 22.51 yen/kWh in October 2026 and 11.67 yen/kWh in the same month last year, a difference of 10.84 yen.

Source: Spot market trading results published by the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX). Simple average of the price for each 30-minute slot. Our own aggregation.

② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | How the price moves during the day

The wholesale electricity price changes every 30 minutes. The figure shows the shape of a day, made by averaging the prices at each time of day across the month. If a battery charges at cheap times and discharges at expensive times, the difference becomes its income.

In October 2026, the average wholesale price during the day ranged from a low of 19.03 yen around 12:00 to a high of 37.11 yen around 16:30 in Tokyo. In Kyushu it ranged from 4.94 yen around 12:00 to 24.74 yen around 17:30.

Source: JEPX spot market. Our own aggregation.

② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | How large the spread is per kWh

For each day, we took the average price of the 4 most expensive hours and subtracted the average price of the 4 cheapest hours. We show the monthly average by area. This equals the difference per kWh if you buy 1 kWh in the 4 cheapest hours and sell it in the 4 most expensive hours. It is a simple difference. It does not subtract electricity lost in charging and discharging or fees, and it ignores whether the cheap hours come before the expensive hours.

The October 2026 spread between the 4 most expensive and 4 cheapest hours in wholesale electricity (monthly average) was largest in Hokkaido, at 29.02 yen/kWh, and smallest in Tokyo, at 17.14 yen/kWh.

③ Capacity market (OCCTO) | The market that buys future supply capacity

The capacity market buys electricity supply capacity for 4 years ahead through an auction held once a year. Prices are set in yen per kW per year. The left figure shows the clearing prices of past main auctions. The right figure shows the battery and pumped-hydro capacity contracted in the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction (a separate auction that supports new builds such as batteries and pumped hydro). Results are published once a year, so they do not change every month. The latest is for delivery FY2029 (published January 2026).

The capacity market main auction clearing price for Tokyo was 14,137 yen/kW for delivery FY2024, 3,495 yen/kW for FY2025, 5,834 yen/kW for FY2026, 9,555 yen/kW for FY2027, 14,812 yen/kW for FY2028 and 15,111 yen/kW for FY2029. The battery and pumped-hydro capacity contracted in the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction was 1,669 MW in the FY2023 auction; 961 MW for 3–6 hours and 769 MW for 6 hours or more in the FY2024 auction; and 819 MW for lithium-ion and 886 MW for non-lithium in the FY2025 auction. Both are annual values, so they do not change every month; these are the latest published figures.

Source: Clearing results and capacity contract documents published by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO) (sources are listed in the last section (Assumptions and terms)). As published.

Our records | How many months and how much it takes to connect to the grid

A grid connection study is the procedure where the grid operator checks "whether this site can connect, and how much the work will cost." The grid connection cost contribution is the part of the connection work cost paid by the applicant. Of the answer documents we have received, we aggregated only high-voltage cases (connected to 6,600V distribution lines), by grid area. Extra-high-voltage cases are not included. Grid areas with fewer than 5 cases are not shown.

The medians of grid connection study answers (high voltage only, as of October 19, 2026) ranged from 8.0 months in Tokyo to 17.0 months in Tohoku for the construction period, and from ¥3.02 million in Tokyo to ¥32.35 million in Tohoku for the grid connection cost contribution (Tohoku: n=5 and n=8).

Source: Aggregation of the high-voltage portion of grid connection study answer documents from general transmission and distribution operators that we received (as of October 19, 2026).

Assumptions and terms

  • The figures are our own aggregation of the published values from the sources. We have not added up several months or converted anything to an annual basis. We do not show the income of any single battery storage site.
  • Months covered: the balancing market (EPRX) is preliminary figures for September 2026 (delivery dates up to the 29th); wholesale electricity (JEPX) is October 2026 (31 days). The balancing market covers a different month until published figures for October 2026 enter our aggregation.
  • Balancing market: nationwide (total column) of EPRX preliminary figures. Procured volume is by grid operator (TSO); bid volume, awarded volume and average awarded price are by the area where the power source is located. Volumes are averages per 30-minute slot. The average awarded price is weighted by awarded volume. Bid ratio = bid volume ÷ procured volume; clearing rate = awarded volume ÷ bid volume. For the 3-hour blocks up to March 13, 2026, we counted 1 block as 6 30-minute slots.
  • Range of awarded prices: the thin line runs from the lowest bid (minimum awarded price) to the highest bid (maximum awarded price) among the month's slots with awards (published values by the area where the power source is located). The published values for Primary reserve (online), Secondary reserve 1 and 2, and Tertiary reserve 1 also include Composite product bids (one price for several products together). So we removed from the thin line any bid above 10 yen that can be explained by a Composite product bid in the same slot and area. For Primary reserve (offline), we removed from the thin line bids above the price cap (10 yen this month). (The balancing market system still awards bids above the cap and cuts them down to the cap at settlement.) The thick band is the range from the bottom 10% to 90% when the average awarded prices of slots with awards are sorted from low to high. The dot is the average weighted by awarded volume.
  • Clearing rate by grid area × time of day: total awarded volume ÷ total bid volume for slots at the same time of day in the month (by the area where the power source is located).
  • The time from instruction to output change and the duration for each balancing market product are the FY2026 specifications. Primary reserve online is the category connected to the grid operator by a dedicated line; offline is the category that runs on the simplified dispatch system without a dedicated line.
  • High-price slots for Tertiary reserve 2: slots with a nationwide average awarded price of 10 yen/kW per 30 min or more. The top 1% is the top 1% of slots when ranked by sales (average awarded price × awarded volume). The maximum awarded price is the published value by the area where the power source is located.
  • Average prices are simple averages of 30-minute slots (not weighted by trading volume). Daytime is the slots from 8:00 to 16:00, and evening is the slots from 16:00 to 20:00.
  • Spread: we sort the day's 30-minute slots by price and subtract the average of the 8 cheapest slots from the average of the 8 most expensive slots (4 hours) (4 slots for 2 hours). It ignores whether the cheap hours come before the expensive hours, electricity lost in charging and discharging, and fees, taxes and grid usage charges. It does not show actual income.
