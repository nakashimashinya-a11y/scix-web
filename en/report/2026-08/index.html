Battery Storage Market Notes, August 2026 issue

"Grid-scale battery storage" means large batteries connected to the power grid that store electricity and release it. They earn money by selling the "capability" to fix electricity surpluses and shortages. They also earn from the way the price of electricity changes by time of day. These notes use published figures and our own records to show this month's movements in charts and tables. This issue is a back issue, made in November 2026 for an earlier month. The grid connection study figures are as of when the issue was made (October 19, 2026).

01

Three income streams for a battery storage plant (how to read these notes)

A battery storage plant (grid-scale battery) earns income from three markets. ① In the balancing market (EPRX), it sells the "capability" to fix electricity surpluses and shortages to grid operators. ② In wholesale electricity (JEPX), it buys and stores electricity in cheap hours and sells it in expensive hours. ③ In the capacity market (OCCTO), the country buys future electricity supply capacity in advance. These notes list the figures for these three markets in this order. The ①, ② or ③ at the start of each section title shows which market the section is about.

Source: Market rules of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX) and the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO).

02

Three trends this month

We picked three movements this month from the figures of the three markets.

  • In the balancing market (EPRX), the bid ratio for Primary reserve (online) went above 100% in June 2026, at 106.8%. This month it was 116.0%, and the average winning price was 4.40 yen/kW per 30 min.
  • In the balancing market (EPRX), 52.2% of the August 2026 sales of Tertiary reserve 2 came from the top 10% of 30-minute slots by sales.
  • In wholesale electricity (JEPX), we compared the average price of the 4 most expensive hours with the average of the 4 cheapest hours. In August 2026, the monthly average of this gap was 11.94 yen/kWh in Tokyo and 18.82 yen/kWh in Kyushu.
03

① Balancing market (EPRX) | 7 products, and prices by area

The balancing market is where grid operators buy, every 30 minutes, the "capability" to fix electricity surpluses and shortages. Products are split by how fast they change output. Primary reserve responds to changes in frequency. Secondary and Tertiary reserves respond to instructions from the grid operator. Primary reserve is the fastest, and Tertiary reserve 2 is the slowest. Primary reserve senses frequency changes by itself and responds. It has two categories. In "online", the grid operator watches its operation over a dedicated line. In "offline", no dedicated line is laid, and the record of operation is submitted afterwards. They are sought and traded separately, so we show them separately. A Composite product is a bid that offers several products together. Prices are set in yen per 1 kW of capability per 30 minutes. kW is a unit of how much power can be delivered. It is different from kWh, which measures an amount of electricity. Each 30-minute trading round is called a "30-minute slot". The figure shows the range of winning prices by area (by area where the power source is). The thin line runs from the cheapest bid to the most expensive bid in the month. The thick band is the price range that holds 8 out of every 10 slots with winning bids. The dot is the average winning price. Nationwide figures are in the table in the "Market figures" section.

By area where the power source is, the highest average winning price in the balancing market was 14.52 yen/kW per 30 min for Primary reserve (offline) in Tohoku. The lowest was 0.38 yen/kW per 30 min for Tertiary reserve 2 in Shikoku.

Source: Trading results (preliminary figures) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Compiled by us.

① Balancing market (EPRX) | Primary reserve (online) became a fight for seats

Primary reserve is the fastest product. It responds to changes in frequency and changes output within 10 seconds (online) or within 30 seconds (offline). Batteries are often used for it. The left side is the online category, and the right side is the offline category. The bars show the bid ratio (amount bid ÷ amount sought by grid operators). Above 100% means more bids came in than were sought. Even below 100%, some bids do not win (see the clearing rate in the "Market figures" table). The bars rise when bids increase, and also when the amount sought falls. The line is the average winning price. These are monthly figures from April 2025, the data we have.

The bid ratio for Primary reserve (online) in the balancing market went above 100% in June 2026, at 106.8%, for the first time since April 2025. This month it was 116.0% (amount sought: 3,066.1 MW in April 2025, 1,507.8 MW this month). The average winning price went from 2.89 yen/kW per 30 min in April 2025 to 4.40 yen/kW per 30 min this month. For Primary reserve (offline), the bid ratio this month was 73.4% (amount sought: 1,122.5 MW in April 2025, 497.4 MW this month), and the average winning price was 9.63 yen/kW per 30 min.

Source: Trading results (preliminary figures) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Compiled by us.

② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | Average prices

Wholesale electricity is the electricity that power companies and retailers buy and sell. Its price is set in the "spot market" of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), where electricity for the next day is traded the day before. "System (nationwide)" is the price when all of Japan is treated as one market (system price). When the transmission lines between regions are congested, prices split by area from Hokkaido to Kyushu (area prices).

The monthly average for System (nationwide) in wholesale electricity (JEPX) was 19.14 yen/kWh in August 2026, against 11.98 yen/kWh in the same month last year (a difference of 7.15 yen/kWh).

Source: Spot market trading results published by the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX). Simple average of 30-minute prices. Compiled by us.

② Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | How big is the price gap per kWh

For each day, we took the average price of the 4 most expensive hours and subtracted the average price of the 4 cheapest hours. The figure shows the monthly average of this gap by area. It equals the gain per kWh from buying 1 kWh in the 4 cheapest hours and selling it in the 4 most expensive hours. It is a simple difference. It does not subtract electricity lost in charging and discharging, or fees. It also ignores whether the cheap hours come before the expensive hours.

By area, the monthly average gap between the 4 most expensive hours and the 4 cheapest hours in wholesale electricity was largest in Shikoku at 19.67 yen/kWh, and smallest in Tokyo at 11.94 yen/kWh.

③ Capacity market (OCCTO) | A market that buys future supply capacity

The capacity market buys electricity supply capacity for 4 years ahead, through an auction held once a year. Prices are set in yen per kW per year. The left chart shows the clearing prices of past main auctions. The right chart shows the battery and pumped hydro capacity that won in the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction. This is a separate auction that promises capacity income, in principle for 20 years, to things like new decarbonized power sources. Results are published once a year, so they do not change every month. The latest is for delivery FY2029 (published January 2026).

Clearing prices for Tokyo in the capacity market main auction were 14,137 yen/kW for delivery FY2024, 3,495 yen/kW for FY2025, 5,834 yen/kW for FY2026, 9,555 yen/kW for FY2027, 14,812 yen/kW for FY2028 and 15,111 yen/kW for FY2029. In the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction (bids in FY2025), the battery and pumped hydro capacity that won was 819 MW for lithium-ion batteries and pumped hydro (excluding new builds), and 886 MW for non-lithium-ion batteries, pumped hydro (new builds) and LDES. Both come from the latest once-a-year publication, so they do not change every month.

Source: Clearing results and capacity securing contract documents published by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO) (sources listed in the last section (Assumptions and terms)). As published.

Our records | How many months and how much it takes to connect to the grid

A grid connection study is a process where you ask the grid operator to check: "Can we connect at this site, and how much will the work cost?" The grid connection cost contribution is the part of the connection work cost that the applicant pays. From the response letters we received, we compiled only the high-voltage ones (connecting to 6,600 V distribution lines), by grid area. Extra-high voltage is not included. Grid areas with fewer than 5 cases are not shown.

Median responses to grid connection studies (high voltage only, as of October 19, 2026) ranged from 8.0 months in Tokyo (n=48) to 17.0 months in Tohoku (n=5) for construction time. For the grid connection cost contribution, they ranged from ¥3.02 million in Tokyo (n=50) to ¥32.35 million in Tohoku (n=8).

Source: Compilation of the high-voltage grid connection study response letters we received from grid operators (as of October 19, 2026).

Assumptions and terms

  • We compiled the figures from the values published by the sources. We have not added up several months or converted anything to annual figures. We do not show revenue amounts for any single battery storage plant.
  • Months covered: for the balancing market (EPRX), preliminary figures for August 2026 (delivery dates up to the 31st); for wholesale electricity (JEPX), August 2026 (31 days). For the balancing market, the month may differ until the published values for August 2026 are included in our compilation.
  • Balancing market: EPRX preliminary figures, nationwide (the total column). The amount sought is by grid operator (TSO). The amount bid, amount won and average winning price are by area where the power source is. Amounts are averages per 30-minute slot. Average winning prices are weighted by the amount won. Bid ratio = amount bid ÷ amount sought; clearing rate = amount won ÷ amount bid. For the 3-hour blocks up to March 13, 2026, we counted each block as 6 slots of 30 minutes.
  • Range of winning prices: the thin line runs from the cheapest bid (lowest winning price) to the most expensive bid (highest winning price) among the month's slots with winning bids (published values by area where the power source is). The published values for Primary reserve (online), Secondary reserve 1 and 2, and Tertiary reserve 1 also include Composite product bids (one price for several products combined). Some bids are above the price cap of 15 yen (for deliveries up to August 31, 2026) for Primary reserve (online) and Secondary reserve 1, or above 10 yen for Secondary reserve 2 and Tertiary reserve 1. We removed such bids from the thin line when Composite product bids in the same slot and area can explain them. For Primary reserve (offline), we removed bids above the price cap (15 yen this month) from the thin line. (The balancing market system also clears bids above the cap, then cuts them down to the cap at settlement.) The thick band is the range from the bottom 10% to 90% of slots with winning bids, sorted by average winning price from cheapest. The dot is the average weighted by the amount won.
  • Clearing rate by grid area × time of day: total amount won ÷ total amount bid, for slots at the same time of day across the month (by area where the power source is).
  • The response time and duration of balancing market products are the FY2026 specifications (Electric Power Reserve Exchange, "Product requirements", 6th edition). Primary reserve senses frequency changes by itself and responds. Online is the category where the grid operator watches its operation over a dedicated line. Offline is the category where no dedicated line is laid, and the record of operation is submitted afterwards. For offline, the response time is within 30 seconds, and there is no set duration.
  • High-priced slots for Tertiary reserve 2: slots with a nationwide average winning price of 10 yen/kW per 30 min or more. The top 1% means the top 1% of slots ranked by sales (average winning price × amount won), with 1% of the number of slots rounded to the nearest whole number. The maximum winning price is the published value by area where the power source is. It is the price of the single highest winning bid. In the balancing market, each winning bid is settled at its own bid price (pay-as-bid (multi-price)). So the average winning price for the same slot and area is lower than the maximum.
  • Average prices are simple averages of 30-minute slots (not weighted by trading volume). Daytime covers the slots from 8:00 to 16:00, and evening covers the slots from 16:00 to 20:00.
  • Price gap: we sort the day's 30-minute slots by price. The gap is the average of the 8 most expensive slots (4 hours) minus the average of the 8 cheapest slots (4 slots for 2 hours). We do not consider the order of cheap and expensive hours, electricity lost in charging and discharging, or fees, taxes and transmission charges. It does not show actual revenue.
