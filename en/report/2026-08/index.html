Battery Storage Market Notes, August 2026 issue

The price of electricity changes with the time of use. A "grid-scale battery" (a large battery connected to the power grid that stores electricity and releases it) earns money from that difference. These notes use published figures and our own records to show this month's trends in figures and tables. This issue is a back issue, prepared retroactively in November 2026. The grid connection study figures are as of the time of preparation (October 19, 2026).

01

Three revenue streams for a battery storage plant (how to read these notes)

A battery storage plant (grid-scale battery) earns revenue from three markets. ① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) is a market where you buy and store electricity when it is cheap and sell it when it is expensive. ② The balancing market (EPRX) is a market where you sell grid operators the "capacity" to correct surpluses or shortages of electricity. ③ The capacity market (OCCTO) is a market where the country buys future electricity supply capacity in advance. These notes present the figures in the order of these three markets. The ①②③ at the start of each section title shows which market it covers.

Source: Rules of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX) and the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO).

02

Three trends this month

We picked three trends this month from the figures of the three markets.

  • In wholesale electricity (JEPX), the monthly average spread between the 4 most expensive hours and the 4 cheapest hours in August 2026 was 11.94 yen/kWh in Tokyo and 18.82 yen/kWh in Kyushu.
  • In the balancing market (EPRX), the bid-to-procurement ratio for Primary reserve (online) was 106.8% in June 2026, exceeding 100% for the first time since April 2025. This month it was 116.0%, and the average winning price was 4.40 yen/kW per 30 min.
  • In the balancing market (EPRX), 52.2% of Tertiary reserve 2 revenue in August 2026 was concentrated in the top 10% of 30-minute slots by revenue.
03

① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | Average price

Wholesale electricity is electricity bought and sold between power companies and retailers. Its price is set in the "spot market" of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), a market where electricity for the next day is traded the day before. "System (nationwide)" is the price when all of Japan is treated as one market (the system price). When the transmission lines between regions are congested, the price splits by area from Hokkaido to Kyushu (area prices).

The average wholesale electricity price for System (nationwide) in August 2026 was 19.14 yen/kWh. In the same month last year it was 11.98 yen/kWh.

Source: Spot market trading results published by the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX). Simple average of the price for each 30 minutes. Our tally.

04

① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | How the price moves during the day

The wholesale electricity price changes every 30 minutes. The figure shows the shape of a day, averaging the price at the same time of day across the month. If a battery stores electricity in cheap hours and releases it in expensive hours, the difference becomes its revenue.

The daily low of wholesale electricity was 17.32 yen/kWh around 6:30 in Tokyo and 10.55 yen/kWh around 12:00 in Kyushu. The daily peak was 28.06 yen/kWh around 17:30 in Tokyo and 30.21 yen/kWh around 18:00 in Kyushu.

Source: JEPX spot market. Our tally.

① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | How large is the spread per kWh

We took the average price of the 4 most expensive hours of each day, subtracted the average price of the 4 cheapest hours, and listed the monthly average by area. This equals the difference per kWh when you buy 1 kWh in the 4 cheapest hours and sell it in the 4 most expensive hours. It is a simple difference: it does not subtract electricity lost in charging and discharging or fees, and it ignores the order of the cheap and expensive hours.

The monthly average spread between the 4 most expensive hours and the 4 cheapest hours of wholesale electricity was largest in Shikoku at 19.67 yen/kWh and smallest in Tokyo at 11.94 yen/kWh.

① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | There is a spread every day

We listed the same 4-hour spread day by day. The larger the spread on a day, the more room a battery has to earn from it.

07

② Balancing market (EPRX) | Seven products, and prices by area

The balancing market is a market where grid operators buy, every 30 minutes, the "capacity" to correct surpluses or shortages of electricity. The products are divided by how fast output changes after a dispatch instruction. Primary reserve is the fastest and Tertiary reserve 2 is the slowest. Primary reserve has two categories: "online", connected to the grid operator by a dedicated line, and "offline", which runs on the simplified dispatch system without a dedicated line. They are procured and traded separately, so we show them separately. A Composite product is a bid that offers several products together. The price is set in yen per 1 kW of capacity offered per 30 minutes. The figure shows the range of winning prices by area (by the area where the resource is located). The thin line runs from the cheapest to the most expensive bid in the month, the thick band is the price range that contains 8 out of 10 slots with awards, and the dot is the average winning price. Nationwide figures are in the table in the "Market figures" section.

By area, the average winning price in the balancing market was highest for Primary reserve (offline) in Tohoku at 14.52 yen/kW per 30 min and lowest for Tertiary reserve 2 in Shikoku at 0.38 yen/kW per 30 min.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our tally.

09

② Balancing market (EPRX) | Primary reserve (online) became a contest for seats

Primary reserve is the fastest product, changing output within 10 seconds of an instruction, and batteries are often used for it. The left side is the online category and the right side is the offline category. The bars show the bid-to-procurement ratio (bid volume ÷ volume procured by grid operators). Above 100%, more bids come in than the procured volume, and some bids lose. The line shows the average winning price. These are monthly figures from April 2025, the data we have. Primary reserve (offline) figures start in April 2026.

In the balancing market, the bid-to-procurement ratio for Primary reserve (online) exceeded 100% in June 2026. This month it is 116.0%, with an average winning price of 4.40 yen/kW per 30 min. Primary reserve (offline) was 73.4% and 9.63 yen/kW per 30 min.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our tally.

14

③ Capacity market (OCCTO) | A market that buys future supply capacity

The capacity market is a market that buys electricity supply capacity 4 years ahead through an auction once a year. The price is set in yen per kW per year. The left figure shows the clearing prices of past main auctions. The right figure shows the battery and pumped hydro capacity contracted in the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction (a separate auction that supports new builds such as batteries and pumped hydro). Results are published once a year, so they do not change every month. The latest is for delivery FY2029 (published January 2026).

The capacity market clearing price in Tokyo was 14,137 yen/kW for delivery FY2024, 3,495 yen/kW for FY2025 and 15,111 yen/kW for FY2029. In the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction, batteries and pumped hydro in the FY2025 round were 819 MW for lithium-ion and 886 MW for non-lithium (the latest annual publication).

Source: Clearing results and capacity assurance contract documents published by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO) (sources listed in the last section (Assumptions and terms)). As published.

Our records | How many months and how much it costs to connect to the grid

A grid connection study is a procedure in which the grid operator checks "whether a site can connect and how much the work will cost". The grid connection cost contribution is the part of the connection work cost paid by the applicant. Of the responses we received, we tallied only the high-voltage ones (connecting to 6,600 V distribution lines) by grid area. Extra-high voltage is not included. Grid areas with fewer than 5 responses are not shown.

In the grid connection study responses (high voltage only, as of October 19, 2026), the median construction period ranged from 8.0 months in Tokyo to 17.0 months in Tohoku, and the median grid connection cost contribution from ¥3.02 million in Tokyo to ¥32.35 million in Tohoku.

Source: Tally of the high-voltage grid connection study responses from general transmission and distribution operators that we received (as of October 19, 2026).

Assumptions and terms

  • The figures are our tally of the values published by the sources. We do not add up several months or convert to annual amounts. We do not show revenue amounts for any single battery storage plant.
  • Months covered: wholesale electricity (JEPX) is August 2026 (31 days), and the balancing market (EPRX) is August 2026 preliminary data (delivery dates up to the 31st). For the balancing market, the month may differ until the published figures for August 2026 are included in our tally.
  • Average prices are simple averages of 30-minute slots (not weighted by trading volume). Daytime is the slots from 8:00 to 16:00, and evening is the slots from 16:00 to 20:00.
  • Spread: we sort the day's 30-minute slots by price and subtract the average of the cheapest 8 slots from the average of the most expensive 8 slots (4 hours) (4 slots for 2 hours). We do not account for the order of the cheap and expensive hours, electricity lost in charging and discharging, or fees, taxes and transmission charges. It does not represent actual revenue.
  • Balancing market: nationwide (total column) of EPRX preliminary data. Procured volume is by grid operator (TSO); bid volume, awarded volume and average winning price are by area where the resource is located. Volumes are averages per 30-minute slot. The average winning price is weighted by awarded volume. Bid-to-procurement ratio = bid volume ÷ procured volume; award rate = awarded volume ÷ bid volume. For the 3-hour blocks up to March 13, 2026, we counted 1 block as 6 slots of 30 minutes.
  • Winning price range: the thin line runs from the cheapest bid (minimum winning price) to the most expensive bid (maximum winning price) among the month's slots with awards (published values by area where the resource is located). The published values for Primary reserve (online), Secondary reserve 1 and 2, and Tertiary reserve 1 also include Composite product bids (one price for several products bundled together). So we removed from the thin line the bids above the price cap of 15 yen for Primary reserve (online) and Secondary reserve 1 (for delivery up to August 31, 2026), and above 10 yen for Secondary reserve 2 and Tertiary reserve 1, where they can be explained by a Composite product bid in the same slot and area. For Primary reserve (offline), we removed from the thin line the bids above the price cap (15 yen this month) (the balancing market system also clears bids above the cap and cuts them down to the cap at settlement). The thick band is the range from 10% to 90% from the bottom when the average winning prices of slots with awards are sorted from cheapest. The dot is the average weighted by awarded volume.
  • Award rate by grid area × time of day: total awarded volume ÷ total bid volume for the slots at the same time of day across the month (by area where the resource is located).
  • For balancing market products, the time from instruction to output change and the duration are the FY2026 specifications. The online category of Primary reserve is connected to the grid operator by a dedicated line, and the offline category runs on the simplified dispatch system without a dedicated line.
  • High-priced slots for Tertiary reserve 2: slots with a nationwide average winning price of 10 yen/kW per 30 min or more. The top 1% is the top 1% of slots sorted by revenue (average winning price × awarded volume). The maximum winning price is the published value by area where the resource is located.
