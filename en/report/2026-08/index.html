Battery Storage Market Notes, August 2026 issue

The price of electricity changes with the time of day. A "grid-scale battery" (a large battery connected to the power grid that stores electricity and releases it) earns money from that difference. These notes use published figures and our own records to show this month's movements in figures and tables. This issue is a back issue, prepared retroactively in November 2026. The grid connection study figures are as of the time of preparation (October 19, 2026).

01

The three income sources of a battery storage plant (how to read these notes)

A battery storage plant (grid-scale battery) earns income from three markets. ① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) is the market where you buy and store electricity when it is cheap and sell it when it is expensive. ② The balancing market (EPRX) is the market where you sell grid operators the "capability" to correct surpluses or shortages of electricity. ③ The capacity market (OCCTO) is the market where the country buys future electricity supply capability in advance. These notes present the figures in the order of these three markets. The ①, ②, ③ at the start of each section title show which market the section covers.

Source: Rules of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX) and the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO).

02

Three movements this month

From the figures for the three markets, we picked three movements this month.

  • In wholesale electricity (JEPX), the monthly average of the spread between the average of the 4 highest-price hours and the average of the 4 lowest-price hours in August 2026 was 11.94 yen/kWh in Tokyo and 18.82 yen/kWh in Kyushu.
  • In the balancing market (EPRX), the bid-to-requirement ratio for Primary reserve (online) went above 100% in June 2026, at 106.8%. This month it was 116.0%, and the average winning price was 4.40 yen/kW per 30 min.
  • In the balancing market (EPRX), 52.2% of Tertiary reserve 2 revenue in August 2026 was concentrated in the top 10% of 30-minute slots by revenue.
03

① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | Average price

Wholesale electricity is the electricity that power companies and retailers buy and sell. Its price is set on the "spot market" of the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX), where electricity for the next day is traded the day before. "System (nationwide)" is the price when all of Japan is treated as one market (the system price). When transmission lines between regions are congested, prices split by area from Hokkaido to Kyushu (area prices).

The monthly average System (nationwide) price in wholesale electricity (JEPX) was 19.14 yen/kWh in August 2026, against 11.98 yen/kWh in the same month last year (a difference of 7.15 yen/kWh).

Source: Spot market trading results published by the Japan Electric Power Exchange (JEPX). Simple average of the 30-minute prices. Our tally.

① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | How the price moves through the day

The wholesale electricity price changes every 30 minutes. The figure shows the daily shape, averaging the prices at the same time of day across the month. If a battery charges when prices are low and discharges when they are high, the difference becomes its income.

The daily low and high in wholesale electricity were 17.32 yen/kWh around 6:30 and 28.06 yen/kWh around 17:30 in Tokyo, and 10.55 yen/kWh around 12:00 and 30.21 yen/kWh around 18:00 in Kyushu.

Source: JEPX spot market. Our tally.

① Wholesale electricity (JEPX) | How large is the spread per kWh

For each area, we took the average price of the 4 highest-price hours of each day, subtracted the average price of the 4 lowest-price hours, and show the monthly average. This equals the difference per kWh if you buy 1 kWh in the 4 cheapest hours and sell it in the 4 most expensive hours. It is a simple difference: it does not subtract electricity lost in charging and discharging or fees, and it ignores the order of the cheap and expensive hours.

By area, the monthly average spread between the average of the 4 highest-price hours and the 4 lowest-price hours in wholesale electricity was largest in Shikoku at 19.67 yen/kWh and smallest in Tokyo at 11.94 yen/kWh.

07

② Balancing market (EPRX) | Seven products, and prices by area

The balancing market is where grid operators buy, every 30 minutes, the "capability" to correct surpluses or shortages of electricity. Products are divided by how quickly output changes after a dispatch instruction. Primary reserve is the fastest, and Tertiary reserve 2 is the slowest. Primary reserve has two categories: "online", connected to the grid operator by a dedicated line, and "offline", which runs on the simplified dispatch system without a dedicated line. They are tendered and traded separately, so we show them separately. A Composite product is a bid that offers several products together. The price is set in yen per 1 kW of capability per 30 minutes. The figure shows the range of winning prices by area (by the area where the power source is located). The thin line runs from the cheapest to the most expensive winning bid in the month, the thick band is the price range covering 8 of every 10 slots with winning bids, and the dot is the average winning price. Nationwide figures are in the table in the "Market figures" section.

By the area where the power source is located, the highest average winning price in the balancing market was Primary reserve (offline) in Tohoku at 14.52 yen/kW per 30 min, and the lowest was Tertiary reserve 2 in Shikoku at 0.38 yen/kW per 30 min.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our tally.

09

② Balancing market (EPRX) | Primary reserve (online) became a competition for seats

Primary reserve is the fastest product, changing output within 10 seconds of an instruction, and batteries are often used for it. The left side is the online category, the right side the offline category. The bars show the bid-to-requirement ratio (bid volume ÷ volume tendered by grid operators). Above 100%, more bids came in than were tendered, so some bids lose. The bars rise both when bids increase and when the tendered volume falls. The line is the average winning price. These are the monthly figures we have, from April 2025.

The bid-to-requirement ratio for Primary reserve (online) in the balancing market went above 100% for the first time since April 2025 in June 2026, at 106.8%. This month it was 116.0% (tendered volume 3,066.1 MW in April 2025, 1,507.8 MW this month). The average winning price went from 2.89 yen/kW per 30 min in April 2025 to 4.40 yen/kW per 30 min this month. For Primary reserve (offline), this month's bid-to-requirement ratio was 73.4% (tendered volume 1,122.5 MW in April 2025, 497.4 MW this month), and the average winning price was 9.63 yen/kW per 30 min.

Source: Trading results (preliminary) published by the Electric Power Reserve Exchange (EPRX). Our tally.

Their highest months were, in that order, February 2026 at 4.90 yen, July 2025 at 18.37 yen and May 2026 at 4.18 yen. ③ Capacity market (OCCTO) | A market that buys future supply capability

The capacity market buys electricity supply capability 4 years ahead through an annual auction. The price is set in yen per kW per year. The left figure shows the clearing prices of the main auctions so far. The right figure shows the battery and pumped hydro capacity contracted in the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction (a separate auction that supports new builds such as batteries and pumped hydro). These are published once a year, so they do not change every month. The latest is for delivery FY2029 (published January 2026).

The Tokyo clearing price in the capacity market main auction was 14,137 yen/kW for delivery FY2024, 3,495 yen/kW for FY2025, 5,834 yen/kW for FY2026, 9,555 yen/kW for FY2027, 14,812 yen/kW for FY2028 and 15,111 yen/kW for FY2029. In the Long-Term Decarbonization Power Source Auction (FY2025 auction), contracted battery and pumped hydro capacity was 819 MW for lithium-ion and 886 MW for non-lithium. Both are the latest annual publication, so they do not change every month.

Source: Clearing results and capacity securing contract documents published by the Organization for Cross-regional Coordination of Transmission Operators (OCCTO) (sources in the last section (Assumptions and terms)). As published.

Our records | How many months and how much it costs to connect to the grid

A grid connection study is the procedure of asking the grid operator to check "whether we can connect at this location, and how much the works will cost". The grid connection cost contribution is the part of the connection works paid by the applicant. Of the response letters we received, we tallied only the high-voltage ones (connecting to 6,600 V distribution lines) by grid area. Extra-high voltage is not included. Grid areas with fewer than 5 cases are not shown.

Median values from grid connection study responses (high voltage only, as of October 19, 2026) range from 8.0 months in Tokyo (n=48) to 17.0 months in Tohoku (n=5) for the construction period, and from ¥3.02 million in Tokyo (n=50) to ¥32.35 million in Tohoku (n=8) for the grid connection cost contribution.

Source: Our tally of the high-voltage grid connection study response letters we received from general transmission and distribution operators (as of October 19, 2026).

Assumptions and terms

  • The figures are our tallies of the values published by the sources. We do not add up several months or convert to annual figures. We do not show revenue amounts for any single battery storage plant.
  • Months covered: wholesale electricity (JEPX) is August 2026 (31 days); the balancing market (EPRX) is August 2026 preliminary values (delivery dates up to the 31st). For the balancing market, the month may differ until the published values for August 2026 enter our tally.
  • Average prices are simple averages of the 30-minute slots (not weighted by trading volume). Daytime is the slots from 8:00 to 16:00, evening the slots from 16:00 to 20:00.
  • Spread: the day's 30-minute slots are sorted by price, and the average of the 8 cheapest slots is subtracted from the average of the 8 most expensive slots (4 hours) (4 slots for 2 hours). It ignores the order of the cheap and expensive hours, electricity lost in charging and discharging, and fees, taxes and transmission charges. It does not show actual revenue.
  • Balancing market: nationwide (total column) of EPRX preliminary values. Tendered volume is by grid operator (TSO); bid volume, winning volume and average winning price are by area where the power source is located. Volumes are averages per 30-minute slot. The average winning price is weighted by winning volume. Bid-to-requirement ratio = bid volume ÷ tendered volume; contract rate = winning volume ÷ bid volume. For the 3-hour blocks up to March 13, 2026, we counted one block as six 30-minute slots.
  • Range of winning prices: the thin line runs, among the month's slots with winning bids, from the cheapest bid (minimum winning price) to the most expensive bid (maximum winning price) (published values by area where the power source is located). The published values for Primary reserve (online), Secondary reserve 1 and 2, and Tertiary reserve 1 also include Composite product bids (one price for several products bundled). So we removed from the thin line the bids that exceed the price cap of 15 yen (for delivery up to August 31, 2026) for Primary reserve (online) and Secondary reserve 1, or exceed 10 yen for Secondary reserve 2 and Tertiary reserve 1, and that can be explained by a Composite product bid in the same slot and area. For Primary reserve (offline), we removed from the thin line the bids above the price cap (15 yen this month) (the balancing market system also accepts bids above the cap and cuts them down to the cap at settlement). The thick band is the range from 10% to 90% from the bottom when the average winning prices of slots with winning bids are sorted from lowest to highest. The dot is the average weighted by winning volume.
  • Contract rate by grid area × time of day: total winning volume ÷ total bid volume for slots at the same time of day across the month (by area where the power source is located).
  • The time from instruction to output change and the duration for balancing market products are the FY2026 specifications. For Primary reserve, the online category connects to the grid operator by a dedicated line, and the offline category runs on the simplified dispatch system without a dedicated line.
  • High-price slots for Tertiary reserve 2: slots with a nationwide average winning price of 10 yen/kW per 30 min or more. The top 1% is the top 1% of slots ranked by revenue (average winning price × winning volume). The maximum winning price is the published value by area where the power source is located.
