First, MW and MWh

A battery’s size is stated in two numbers. MW (megawatts) is how much power it can deliver at once. MWh (megawatt-hours) is how much it can store.

When a company says how much it runs, the figure is usually in MW. 1 GW (gigawatt) is 1,000 MW. Some Japanese companies write in units of 10,000 kW, each equal to 10 MW: Tokyo Gas’s “1.076 million kW” is 1,076 MW.

The highlighted row is the one figure whose announcement says it counts only plants in operation.

We went through the news pages of about 76 companies. For 60 we read every item since January 2024; for 3 we read only part; for 13 there was no list or the site would not open. These nine published a number. Apart from the roughly 14 MW it now monitors, Power Pool states about 60 MW it plans to start operating within 2026. We do not count companies that publish only a figure combined with solar or wind power plants.

In an interview rather than an announcement, Toshiba said it has contracts for about 120 grid-scale battery sites so far (Nikkei xTECH, 14 October 2026). That yardstick is a count of sites.

Added up, about three times what is running in Japan

The nine figures add up to about 3,800 MW. Yet grid-scale batteries actually connected and running across Japan came to 1,280 MW at the end of June 2026 (ANRE; see our Japan BESS market overview). That is about 1.5 times the 840 MW at the end of March, three months earlier (COLUMN 47-3).

The dates and the counting differ. Even so, it is plain that most of the announced figures are still to come. The bigger the number, the more it matters to ask which stage it describes.

Every figure sits at one of four stages

“Expected” means plants not yet running, and can include some not even contracted. “Contracted” includes plants not yet built. Only from the third stage can you call it a track record.

Table 2 | The four stages of a volume figure
StageWhat is countedWhat to watch
1. Expected or plannedPlants to be run in future, including targets and plansIf connection or construction slips, the figure moves
2. Ordered, contracted, applied forContracts signed and applications receivedIncludes plants not yet built
3. In operationPlants that have started runningWhich markets they run in is a separate question
4. Admitted to the marketPlants that have passed the tests and can bid in that marketThe figure differs from market to market

The figure shrinks as you move from 1 to 4. A large number is not a bad thing. Comparing figures at different stages is where the mistake lies.

One company, two numbers

Digital Grid published two figures in November 2026: 250 MW in operation (as of 4 November) and over 150 MW admitted to the balancing market (as of 19 November). How it counts is written in the chart note. Few companies say this much.

The 250 MW and the “over 150 MW” count different stages. A plant that has started running still has to pass the exchange’s checks before it can enter the balancing market.

When you read a figure, look beyond the body text to the notes on the charts. How it was counted is usually written there.

Look inside “No. 1 share” as well. Shizen Connect’s “about 26%” (announced May 2026) comes from a Fuji Keizai survey and counts corporate contracts for operation-and-control systems (AC and RA systems), as estimated for fiscal 2025. It is not a share of grid-scale battery MW.

What the figure includes also changes it

The same MW can cover different things. If you are handing over a grid-scale plant, ask for the grid-scale figure alone.

Five things to ask before you sign

  1. Which stage is that MW? Expected, contracted, operating, or admitted to the market. A forecast and an operating plant carry very different weight as a record.
  2. What does it include? Grid-scale only, or also batteries beside solar plants and small low-voltage units? Ask for the figure for the same kind of plant as your own.
  3. How many MW excluding your own and your group’s plants? What it runs for outside owners is the record that matches your position.
  4. As of when? In 2026 the figures have moved a great deal within months. Ask for the date it was counted, not the date it was announced.
  5. Which markets? Which balancing-market products? What about the wholesale and capacity markets? What has been admitted to a market is what has passed the tests and can actually bid.

A company that can answer all five with numbers is easier to move forward with. Fees and contract structures are covered in COLUMN 17.

Frequently asked questions

Which aggregator runs the most grid-scale batteries in Japan?

Public material cannot settle it. Leaving targets aside, the largest announced figure is E-Flow’s “over 1.5 GW”, but that is what it expects to be operating by 2030. Tokyo Gas’s 1,076 MW is planned operation, Marubeni Power Retail’s 179 MW is orders received, and Digital Grid’s 250 MW is plants in operation. The yardsticks do not match, so no ranking is possible.

If I add up each company’s volume, do I get the total for Japan?

No. Expected, contracted and operating figures are mixed, and far more companies publish no figure at all.

Is a company with a larger volume the safer choice?

Size alone does not tell you. Look at how much is actually operating and how much is run for outside owners.
