Filing and eligibility to bid are different things

In law, the aggregator’s business is called the Specified Wholesale Supply Business: gathering power from other people’s generators and batteries and delivering it to power companies and the like. When the total gathered exceeds 1,000 kW, the company files with the government before starting. The scheme began in April 2022.

Filing is a matter of submitting papers to the government. Thirty days after acceptance, the business may begin. In those thirty days the government checks how much power the company can expect to gather, and its cyber security. Neither experience of running a battery nor a bidding system is asked for.

After the filing, the government can still order a company to improve its operations. But at the point of accepting a filing, it is not judging how well the company operates.

Some companies are not on the government list. A power generation company that only runs its own plants and batteries and sells their power need not file. Nor does a company that sells power only to another aggregator.

One in three started less than a year ago

Counting by the start date in the government list (the planned date written in the filing), 55 started in 2026 alone: 14 in October and 9 in November up to the 23rd. A company that started less than a year ago has no full year of results as a filed aggregator yet. Some had traded in other forms before.

The colours of the bars show the kind of company. Of the 30 present when the scheme began in April 2022, 24 were listed companies or their subsidiaries. In 2025 independents numbered 16, overtaking the 14 from listed groups. In 2026 there are 22 independents.

By main business, the 55 that started in 2026 include 15 power retailers, 13 renewable developers and builders, and 10 specialists. Companies that sell power, or that develop generation and battery plants, are increasingly filing themselves. Of the 187, 127 give a Tokyo address in their filing.

Of the 187 filed, 115 can bid

To bid in the balancing market, a company must become a trading member of the exchange (EPRX). There are three conditions: being a corporation, net assets of at least ¥10 million, and being a qualified invoice issuer.

The left circle is the government filing, the right circle the trading members. The 115 in the overlap have both filed and hold the qualification to bid. The 72 on the left only cannot bid in the balancing market in their own name; they have to go through another trading member.

There is no joining fee or annual fee. From application through the examinations, works and tests, though, it can take several months (EPRX FAQ).

Membership alone does not yet allow bidding. Each battery has to be checked to confirm that it can respond as the requirements say, and a contract has to be signed with the transmission and distribution company.

EPRX, trading results for fiscal 2025: 139 qualifications (126 companies), of which 59 (56 companies) had bid.

The 126 is the count at the end of March 2026. The list of trading members as of 20 November shows 156.

Holding the qualification and actually bidding in the market are different things. After “we are a member”, ask “have you actually bid?”

The material does not say why they have not bid. So ask the company: “Have you bid? In which product, and since when?”

What kinds of company

Of the 40 specialists, 14 are Japanese arms of overseas firms, 10 are subsidiaries or joint ventures of listed or major companies, and 8 are independents founded within the past ten years. For three of the 187 we found neither a website nor a release and could not tell what they do; a few others we classified from their names.

By capital, 85 are listed companies or their subsidiaries, 60 are independents, 18 are Japanese arms of overseas firms and 4 are tied to local governments. For 20 we could not tell. Table 1 gives examples by name.

Among the big groups are companies set up to run the batteries of their parent or group. Their figures mean something different from those of a company that takes plants widely from outside owners (Volumes).

Three things to check on the lists

  1. Is it a trading member? If not, balancing-market revenue has to go through another member.
  2. When did it start? Under a year, and there is no full-year record under this filing yet.
  3. Whose capital? Set up to run its parent’s batteries, or taking plants widely from outside?

This is not to say new companies are bad. The newer the company, the more you need to ask in numbers.

You can look up the lists yourself

Both lists are open to anyone. With the company’s name, the check takes five minutes.

  1. Open the government list. Find the company in ANRE’s list of Specified Wholesale Supply Operators (in Japanese). It shows the start date: the planned date written in the filing.
  2. Open the EPRX list. See whether the same name appears in EPRX’s list of trading members (in Japanese).
  3. Ask the company. That is as far as the lists go. Whether it has actually bid, and whose batteries it runs and how many, have to be asked.

Frequently asked questions

Does filing as an aggregator mean the government has screened the company?

It does not certify operating skill. It is a filing, not a licence or registration. In the 30 days after acceptance, the government checks how much power the company can expect to gather, and its cyber security. Experience in running batteries and bidding capability are not examined.

Can a company that has filed bid in the balancing market?

Not on that basis alone. Bidding requires becoming an EPRX trading member. Of the 187 filed companies, 115 are members (member list as of 20 November 2026).

Can a company not on the government list do an aggregator’s work?

It can. A power generation company that only runs its own plants and batteries and sells their power is outside the filing. Of EPRX’s 156 trading members, 41 are not on the government list.
