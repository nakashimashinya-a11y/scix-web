What is an aggregator?

Owning a battery earns nothing by itself. Someone has to decide every day when to charge and when to sell, and place the bids. The company that takes on that job is the aggregator.

On the left of the figure is the battery owner, in the middle the aggregator, on the right the markets. The owner holds the battery and hands over the job of running it. The aggregator bids into the markets for the owner. The money comes in from the markets and goes back to the owner after the fee.

Money comes in through three markets

A battery earns in three markets. What it sells differs in each.

Table 1 | The three markets where a battery earns
MarketWhat is soldIn a wordRun by
Wholesale marketElectricity itself: buy and charge when cheap, sell when dearBuy and resellJEPX
Balancing marketA promise to stand by and respond at once when calledA standby feeEPRX
Capacity marketA promise to be able to supply power in futureA reservation feeOCCTO

The prices and rules this series looks at are mainly those of the balancing market, because a battery responds fast and suits it (Pricing).

What does an aggregator do every day?

A day is divided into 48 half-hour slots. The day before, the aggregator decides for each of tomorrow’s 48 slots: sell in the wholesale market, stand by in the balancing market, or charge. On the day, it runs the battery as sold.

An owner can also become a trading member itself. But that takes the qualification to bid, communications to receive dispatch instructions, and a team that bids every day. Most owners hand this work to an aggregator and pay a fee. How fees are set is covered in COLUMN 17.

Three numbers first

The three numbers, from the top.

  1. 187 is the number of companies that have filed with the government to work as aggregators. A filing is not a licence, and operating skill is not tested (The roster).
  2. 115 of them are trading members of the balancing market: qualified to bid, in their own name, in one of the main sources of battery revenue.
  3. Nine, of about 76 companies we checked, published how much they run. Even then, forecasts and operating plants are mixed together (Volumes).

What is known, and what is not

How many there are and whether a company is a trading member are known. What owners most want to know — results by company — cannot be compared in Japan.

In Britain and Texas, third parties calculate results operator by operator and publish them (Overseas). In Japan we could not find such material.

So for now the owner has to ask the company for numbers. What to ask is set out in each article.

Our view

“The aggregator decides everything” is, in our view, half right. The chances to shine by selling high have shrunk (Pricing). What remains is quieter skill: not failing delivery, not stopping, and allocating between markets.

So do not stop at “how many MW do you run?” Follow with “is that a forecast, or plants already operating?” The answer changes (Volumes).

Here are four questions to ask first.

  • “Are you a trading member? Have you actually bid?” (The roster)
  • “Is that MW a forecast, or plants already operating?” (Volumes)
  • “How many penalties and suspensions have there been?” (Pricing)
  • “If we end the contract midway, how does that work?” (Switching)

Frequently asked questions

What does an aggregator do?

It decides, for the owner of a battery or other resource, when to charge and when to sell, and bids into the markets. Its legal name is Specified Wholesale Supply Operator, and if the total it gathers exceeds 1,000 kW, it files with the government before starting. A battery’s revenue comes from three markets: wholesale, balancing and capacity.

How many aggregators are there in Japan?

187 companies had filed as Specified Wholesale Supply Operators as of 30 October 2026. Of these, 115 are trading members of the balancing market (qualified to bid) (member list as of 20 November). The filing is not limited to batteries, so not all 187 run grid-scale batteries.

Which aggregator runs the most batteries?

Public material cannot settle it, because the nine companies that publish a figure count differently. Leaving targets aside, the largest announced figure is E-Flow’s “over 1.5 GW”, but that is what it expects to be operating by 2030. Digital Grid counts only plants in operation: over 250 MW as of 4 November 2026.

How can I tell a skilled aggregator from the rest?

Japan has no third-party league table of operating results yet. Public material lets you check whether a company is a trading member, when it said it would start, and which stage its volume figure describes. For results themselves, ask the company for numbers.
