## @tldr

- A battery storage plant in Japan is sold at one of four stages — land, rights with a connection study response, interconnection approved, or operating — and there are four ways to buy it: completed asset, rights transfer, SPC equity, or an operating asset.
- What moves the price is connection and the grid cost contribution, not the service area; the gap between rights and a completed plant is mostly construction cost.
- Before you buy, check "approved" against paper, and do not pay a deposit while even one Red is visible. A seller decides before the connection study response turns one year old.

## What is for sale comes in four stages

"Buying a battery storage plant" means something different at each stage: land alone; rights backed by a connection study response; rights where the contract with the transmission and distribution company (TSO) is signed and the cost contribution paid; and a plant that is energized and trading in the markets. The price bracket, the documents you check first and the work left after you buy all change with the stage.

| Stage | What is for sale | What remains after buying |
|---|---|---|
| ① Land | Ownership or leasehold of the site | Everything, from the connection study application |
| ② Rights with a connection study response | The TSO's response plus the land rights | Contract application, cost contribution, equipment, construction |
| ③ Interconnection approved, contribution paid | Rights with the TSO contract signed (the plant may not be built yet) | Finishing construction, the aggregator agreement, market participation |
| ④ Operating asset | A plant with a revenue record | Taking over operations — market qualifications do not transfer automatically |

The same 2 MW is treated as worth more at ④ than at ②, because the revenue is already visible. Projects awarded in the Long-Term Decarbonized Power Source Auction (LDA) are not covered here: the scheme assumes the winner builds and runs the plant itself.

## What "development rights" are — land and rights are contracted separately

"Development rights" is not a legally defined term. In practice it means a bundle of three things:

- The connection study response — the TSO's document setting the connection conditions (cost contribution, construction period, connection point). This is the core.
- Land use rights — ownership or leasehold of the site.
- Survey and permit deliverables — geological surveys, boundary surveys, farmland conversion and development permits.

Because the response sets the conditions "at that location, with that capacity", the land and the response only hold value as a set. The value of the rights is the time, cost and risk the buyer saves by holding them — site search, a connection study that may come back unworkable, an unknown cost contribution, and the one to two years or more it takes to get from site selection to a construction application.

## There are four ways to buy

- **Completed-asset sale** — you buy a plant the seller has finished, with the equipment and the land or land-use rights.
- **Rights transfer** — you buy the land and the grid connection position (connection study response, connection contract position, cost contribution) and build it yourself.
- **SPC equity transfer** — you buy the company that owns the plant, taking over its contracts, permits and grid position inside the entity.
- **Operating acquisition** — you buy a plant that has begun commercial operation and has a market trading history. What you are buying is not equipment but verifiable cash flow accumulated month by month.

These four are not ranked; the right one depends on what the buyer can do and what risk it can carry. When you buy an operating plant, its track record comes in two layers. Cleared prices and availability pass to you as information. Market qualifications and contract positions survive or not depending on the legal form — the dividing line is whether the contracting entity changes. Decide the rung and the vehicle first, then look at price.

## What it costs — connection and the cost contribution move the price

We counted the asking prices we saw for high-voltage 2 MW-class plants between January and September 2026, one figure per project (total including land and the grid cost contribution, excluding tax, median; our observation, not a published statistic). Rights: ¥120 million. Completed handover before connection: ¥670 million. Connected or operating: ¥750 million.

The gap between rights and a completed plant is mostly construction cost — the government study group's FY2024 domestic average of ¥68,000/kWh comes to ¥540 million for 8 MWh. The premium only shows clearly once the plant is connected: not one yen of equipment is added, but the plant is on the grid and the wait before it earns is gone.

Splitting the listings by service area shows little difference. What moves the price is whether the plant is connected, whether the cost contribution is final, and whether the deposit and contract application are done. For development rights, a low cost contribution is the largest price-determining factor.

## The sequence — from listing to transfer of applicant status

1. The project is listed on Chikudensho Market — prefecture, size, expected connection timing and progress, viewable without registering.
2. Free registration — the confidentiality undertaking is agreed on screen; prices and documents become visible.
3. Full disclosure of the due diligence package — connection study response, registry, cost contribution receipts, system configuration, JC-STAR, draft agreement.
4. Letter of intent with price and terms — the first point at which you stand ahead of other buyers.
5. Deposit — paid once the agreement spells out what happens if the contract application response changes the terms.
6. Purchase agreement and application to the TSO to transfer applicant status — most of the price is customarily paid here.
7. Transfer completed and the balance paid.

A buyer in a hurry should submit the letter of intent early; a seller in a hurry should have the step 3 package ready from the start. The TSO's review of the transfer typically takes three to six months. Whether you buy assets, SPC equity or the whole business changes tax treatment, how the Construction Business Act applies and how contracts carry over.

## Checks before you buy — is that "approved" for real?

At the end of December 2025, about 640 MW of grid-scale storage was connected in Japan, against about 172 GW under connection study. Most projects offered to buyers are not yet power plants: they are land, grid paperwork and a business plan.

Two rules carry the whole check. Treat what the seller says ("agreed with the TSO", "already paid") as a claim until a third-party document — a TSO letter, a bank record, a registry — backs it. And sort every finding into Green (proceed), Yellow (proceed only with a condition, a price adjustment or a warranty) or Red (pass, or at least stop the deposit).

The rights stage has three steps: connection study answered → contract application filed (receipt notice) → interconnection approved (approval notice). A project described as "approved" that can only show a study response is a Red. The column sets out 43 items across nine domains, from grid rights and land to JC-STAR, degradation warranties, the site, power contracts and credit, in two passes: desk screening before the deposit, and a full investigation before closing.

## Why plants are bought with cash — debt does not arrive in time

Sellers frequently want settlement within about a month, while a corporate loan takes one and a half to two months and project finance six months to a year (our field observation). Cash dominates not because debt is unavailable, but because it does not arrive in time.

Battery equipment can be taken as collateral — for example through a security assignment over aggregate movables. What decides whether debt is available is not the collateral but the contract: how much of the revenue is fixed, and for how long. Five archetypes of domestic debt were disclosed between 2023 and 2026. Project finance on a single site is structurally disadvantaged: its fixed costs — due diligence, documentation, agency work — cannot be absorbed into the interest rate on a project of a few hundred million yen. The practical designs are cash first and refinancing once operating, locking in a long-term contract before raising debt, or leasing.

## For overseas investors — the gates to clear before you buy

"If we order from a Japanese EPC, will the plant get built?" The equipment, yes. But to run as a business a plant also needs four things beyond the hardware: the grid capacity to connect, the land to host that capacity, the regulation governing foreign capital entering Japan's electricity sector, and a route to move capital out of the home country. Grid capacity sits with the developers who already hold it; it is not something an EPC can sell.

The column walks through six gates in order: whether the funds can leave the source country; FEFTA (the electricity business is a designated sector, so prior notification is likely, while core sectors are limited to plants of 50 MW and above, which a 2 MW-class asset does not reach); scarce grid capacity and land; buying rights or a completed asset; how equipment marks the exit (JC-STAR and the U.S. lists); and land and tax, including the Important Land Survey Act. If the first gate does not open, nothing downstream can move.

## If you hold a connection study response and will not build

Dropping a project after the response arrives is not uncommon — the cost contribution came in above expectations, financing could not be secured, or corporate strategy changed. There are three options: develop it yourself, sell the development rights, or withdraw. Letting the response sit is the costliest choice: once a year has passed since the response, the project disappears, scarcity and all.

Before putting a project on the market, assemble seven documents: the original connection study response as PDF, the contract application copy and deposit receipt, the final cost contribution notice, the land registry or lease, the PCS and EMS models with their JC-STAR status, the revenue record if it is operating, and the grant decision notice if it received a subsidy.

## @faq

### What are "grid rights" or "development rights" for a battery storage project?
What is often called "grid rights" is what our columns call development rights, and it is not a legally defined term. In practice it means the TSO's connection study response — the core — bundled with the land use rights and the survey and permit deliverables. The response sets the conditions "at that location, with that capacity", so the land and the response only hold value as a set.

### How much does a battery storage plant cost to buy in Japan?
For a high-voltage 2 MW-class plant (total including land and the cost contribution, excluding tax), the medians of the asking prices we saw from January to September 2026 were ¥120 million for rights, ¥670 million on completed handover and ¥750 million once connected — our observation, not a published statistic. Prices for individual projects are shown on Chikudensho Market after free registration.

### If I buy an operating plant, do its market contracts and qualifications come with it?
It depends on whether the contracting entity changes. Buy the SPC and its qualifications and contracts stay in place. Buy the assets alone and the buyer has to obtain balancing-market qualification again; the capacity market is the main exception, where the position can pass with OCCTO's prior consent.

### Can I get debt to buy a battery storage plant in Japan?
Debt has been disclosed on Japanese plants. What separates the projects that get it from those that do not is not the collateral but the contract — how much of the revenue is fixed, and for how long. Project finance on a single site is structurally disadvantaged by its fixed costs, so the practical designs are paying cash and refinancing once the plant is operating, locking in a long-term contract before raising debt, or leasing.
